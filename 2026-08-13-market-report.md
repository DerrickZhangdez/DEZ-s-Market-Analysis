## Macro & Monetary Environment

**The two-day inflation package.** Thursday's PPI came in below expectations — described as essentially flat — following Wednesday's CPI, which showed headline inflation at 3.4% year-over-year and core at 2.5%. Both prints landed at or below consensus (economists had modeled +0.2% headline monthly with the annual rate easing to 3.4%, and +0.1% core holding 2.5%). Taken together, the two reports removed the near-term argument for a hawkish surprise.

**The Fed's actual problem.** The FOMC has now left the federal funds rate unchanged at 3.50%–3.75% for five consecutive meetings. But the July meeting produced **three dissents**, all preferring a 25bp *hike* — an unusually hawkish split that keeps a September move genuinely live. Futures pricing moved meaningfully on Thursday's data: money markets now assign roughly **35% odds to a September hike**, down from ~40% on Wednesday and ~50% earlier in the week.

This is the critical framing for investors: the 2026 debate is not about *cuts*. It is about whether the Fed **hikes** into a 3.4% headline inflation rate that has refused to converge to target. Core at 2.5% is respectable; headline at 3.4% is not, and the gap between them is largely energy — which is precisely why the Iran de-escalation matters so much to the rates path.

**What to watch.** The FOMC meets **September 15–16**. Between now and then, the August CPI print is the single highest-leverage data point. Retail sales, jobless claims, and the University of Michigan inflation expectations series are the intermediate markers. A hawkish contingent that already numbers three dissenters does not need much provocation.

**Rate-path read-through for equities.** A Fed on hold with a hawkish tail is a mixed regime: it caps multiple expansion at the index level while rewarding businesses that compound earnings without needing lower discount rates. It is also quietly favorable for spread-and-float businesses — payments processors, exchanges, and custodial fintechs earn real money on client balances at 3.50%–3.75% that they would lose in a cutting cycle. That is a theme today's spotlight section develops directly.

## Geopolitical Factors

**Iran de-escalation is the dominant macro variable.** Renewed signs of US-Iran dialogue triggered a sharp crude correction, with Brent and WTI both recording declines near 4%. This follows an extraordinary run: oil surged roughly **21% in July** on Iran-related supply disruption, and Brent traded at $84.89 on August 4 after a 7% single-session drop to a three-week low. Iran-Oman talks concerning the Strait have taken the acute phase of the conflict off the boil.

**The tail risk has not been removed, only repriced.** Approximately **20% of global oil supply** transits the Strait of Hormuz. Any escalation threatening that transit would trigger immediate and severe repricing across energy and equity markets. Crude continues to trade with a persistent geopolitical risk premium, and analysts broadly expect volatility to remain the defining feature of the oil market while the situation is unresolved.

**Why this matters more than usual.** The Iran premium and the Fed's reaction function are now directly coupled. July's 21% oil spike threatened to lift consumer inflation broadly — the exact mechanism that would force the hawkish FOMC bloc's hand. De-escalation is therefore doing double duty: it lowers input costs *and* it lowers the probability of a September hike. Conversely, a Hormuz incident would be a double hit — an energy shock *and* a rates shock. Investors treating the oil retreat as merely an energy-sector story are underweighting the linkage.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Fintech, Payments & Financial Data

*(Day-of-year 225; 225 mod 6 = 3 → Fintech, payments & financial data)*

This is the sector where the gap between fundamental performance and market pricing is currently widest, and it is where today's picks are concentrated.

**The valuation setup.** PitchBook's Q2 2026 fintech and payments comp work found that **most fintech cohorts remain below their 2025 year-end valuation levels**, underscoring how uneven the sector's recovery has been. This is a sector that has been de-rated for two years on a thesis — disintermediation, take-rate compression, AI disruption — while the underlying cash generation largely held. That combination is the raw material for valuation-based investing, but it also demands discrimination: some of the de-rating is deserved.

**Four distinct sub-sectors, four distinct stories:**

**1. Merchant acquiring — maximum pessimism, real cash flow.** The legacy acquirers have been left for dead on fears that Stripe, Adyen, and embedded-payment platforms will structurally erode their SMB base. Global Payments now trades at a price-to-free-cash-flow of **14.35, some 29% below its own 10-year median of 20.27**, while generating $1.06 billion in trailing free cash flow ($1.81B operating cash flow less $752M capex). The Worldpay integration is the swing factor: adjusted operating margin expanded ~70bps on a normalized basis, which is early evidence of synergy capture, though adjusted net revenue growth of only 4–5% confirms this is a cash-return story rather than a growth story. The bear case — leverage — is legitimate and quantified below.

**2. Corporate, fleet & benefits payments — the spread businesses.** WEX is the cleanest expression of the "Fed on hold is good for float" thesis. Gross margin of 83% and EBIT margin of 20.2% confirm the durability of a fee- and spread-based model, and ROE of 22% sits well above sector averages. The company guided to **24.1% year-over-year earnings growth in Q2 2026** across Mobility, Benefits, and Corporate Payments, and is targeting ~$50 million in AI- and automation-driven cost savings through 2026. An activist truce and buyback reset the capital-allocation narrative in May. The structural bear case — EV adoption eroding fuel-card economics — is real but is a decade-long transition being priced as though it were a three-year one.

**3. Emerging-market payments — the growth outlier.** dLocal is doing something almost no one else in the sector is doing: compounding at genuinely high rates. Q1 2026 TPV hit **$14.1 billion, up 73% year-over-year**, with revenue of $335.9 million (+55%) and record gross profit of $118.7 million (+40%). Full-year 2025 closed at $41 billion TPV (+60%), revenue crossing $1 billion for the first time, and gross profit of $403 million (+37%). Management guides 2026 to TPV growth of 50–60%, gross profit growth of 22.5–27.5%, and operating profit growth of 27.5–32.5%. The structural improvement worth noting: **local-to-local payments now represent 55% of TPV, up from 47%** a year ago, meaning dLocal is embedding itself into domestic payment rails rather than merely arbitraging cross-border flow. Note carefully that the growth rates decay down the P&L — TPV +73%, revenue +55%, gross profit +40% — which is take-rate compression, and it is the central analytical question in the name.

**4. Exchanges & financial data — the toll collectors.** Exchanges remain, in Morgan Stanley's framing, the essential plumbing of global markets — venues for accessing liquidity and managing risk whose critical infrastructure continues to collect tolls regardless of direction. Cboe delivered Q2 2026 net revenue of **$731.6 million, up 25%**, with diluted EPS up 50% to $3.35, GAAP operating margin of 65.1% and adjusted operating margin of 70.4%. Options volume set records: quarterly ADV of 21.9 million contracts, monthly ADV of 23.0 million, and a single-day high of **33.4 million contracts in June**. Management raised 2026 organic net revenue growth guidance to the mid-to-high teens.

On the data side, FactSet posted Q2 2026 revenue of $611 million with **organic ASV up 6.7%**, adjusted EPS growth of 4.2%, and raised full-year revenue, ASV, and EPS guidance while increasing buybacks — yet the stock trades at a forward P/E in the 14.5–16.3x range against a US Capital Markets industry average near 14x. The market is pricing FactSet as an AI casualty. The counter-argument is that FactSet no longer sells commoditizable price access; it sells operational infrastructure across four pillars that a chatbot cannot replace: **CUSIP** (the global securities identification standard — a regulatory monopoly), **Cobalt** (private markets data), **Vermilion** (client reporting), and **Portware** (trade execution). Whether that argument holds is the single most important debate in financial data today.

**5. Financial back-office software — the forgotten compounder.** SS&C reported Q2 2026 organic constant-currency revenue growth of **7.6%, accelerating from 5.0% in Q1** and beating its own 5.6% guidance midpoint. Revenue rose to $1.70 billion from $1.54 billion, and basic EPS climbed to $0.99 from $0.74. The stock trades at roughly 10x forward earnings and 11.5x EV/EBITDA — well below sector medians — with consensus modeling 42% EPS growth over three years.

**The spotlight synthesis.** This sector contains three genuinely different opportunities that the market is conflating: (a) cheap, slow, cash-generative incumbents being priced for terminal decline (GPN, WEX, SSNC); (b) high-multiple-but-earning-it compounders where volatility is a tailwind (CBOE); and (c) a genuine high-growth EM asset with a take-rate question (DLO). A portfolio that owns all three shapes is not diversified by ticker alone — it is diversified by *what has to go right*.

### Artificial Intelligence

The AI complex is entering its highest-stakes earnings stretch of the year, and the financing structure of the buildout has become the live debate.

**The circular-financing question.** NVIDIA shares fell 5% in late July after the Wall Street Journal reported the company is in talks to **guarantee up to $250 billion in financing for OpenAI's data-center buildout**. This reignited "circular financing" concerns — the worry that a chip vendor underwriting its own customer's purchases is manufacturing demand rather than meeting it. This is the most important structural question in AI investing right now, and it is not going away before the September quarter.

**The earnings gauntlet.** NVIDIA reports **August 26**, Marvell follows **August 27**, and Broadcom closes the sequence on **September 2**. NVIDIA closed at $217.50 on August 11, up 16.76% year-to-date and 996% over five years, and became the world's first $4 trillion company on data-center demand.

**The spending backdrop remains extraordinary.** Hyperscalers have guided to **$600 billion-plus in AI data-center capex for 2026**, with several of NVIDIA's largest customers raising their forecasts. Global semiconductor sales hit a record $120.6 billion in May 2026, marking the fifteenth consecutive monthly record.

**Today's cautionary datapoint.** Cerebras fell 15.3% on mixed Q2 results. The AI hardware cohort below the megacap tier is now demonstrating that being in the right sector is not sufficient — execution and unit economics are being underwritten individually.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

Semiconductors continue to run on record fundamentals — the fifteenth consecutive record monthly sales print at $120.6 billion in May — while the equity response has become increasingly selective ahead of the NVIDIA/Marvell/Broadcom reporting sequence.

Recent sessions have shown the group's two-sided sensitivity. Earlier in August, Intel led chips lower in a session where the S&P closed little changed on Iran uncertainty, and August 10 saw back-to-back index losses driven by a tech sell-off compounded by Iran risk. The complex is trading as a high-beta expression of both the AI capex cycle and the geopolitical risk premium simultaneously.

The circular-financing debate around NVIDIA's potential $250 billion OpenAI guarantee is the overhang that matters most for the group. If the market concludes that a meaningful share of AI demand is vendor-financed, the entire semiconductor supply chain's multiple is at risk — not because the revenue is fake, but because the *durability* of that revenue would be reassessed.

### Software & SaaS

The Trade Desk's 21.9% single-session decline is the sector's defining event today and is characterized as profit-taking and continued volatility in specific high-valuation software names. That framing matters: this is not a sector-wide de-rating, it is a valuation-specific one. Names trading at premium multiples on premium growth expectations are being marked to reality one report at a time.

The contrast with today's spotlight is instructive. Financial-infrastructure software — SS&C at ~10x forward earnings growing organic constant-currency revenue at 7.6%, FactSet at ~15x forward with 6.7% organic ASV growth — offers a fraction of the growth rate but at a fraction of the multiple, with vastly lower guidance risk. In a market punishing disappointment at 20%-per-event severity, the *distribution* of outcomes matters as much as the mean.

**2. Hormuz escalation reversing the oil unwind — severity: extreme, probability: low but non-trivial.** Roughly 20% of global oil transits the Strait. Oil rose 21% in July on Iran conflict and has retraced on Iran-Oman dialogue. Because the energy shock and the inflation path are now coupled, a re-escalation is a double hit — it lifts headline inflation directly into the hands of the FOMC's hawkish bloc while simultaneously compressing equity multiples. Analysts uniformly flag that the situation is unresolved and market sensitivity to deterioration remains acute.

**3. AI capex credibility and the circular-financing question — severity: high, probability: moderate.** The report that NVIDIA is in talks to guarantee up to $250 billion of OpenAI's data-center financing knocked 5% off the stock and reopened a legitimate question about whether hyperscaler and neocloud demand is genuinely arm's-length. With NVIDIA reporting August 26, Marvell August 27, and Broadcom September 2, and $600B+ of 2026 hyperscaler capex underwriting a large share of index earnings growth, any evidence of vendor-financed demand would force a re-rating well beyond semis.

**4. Single-name gap risk in a low-VIX market — severity: moderate, probability: certain and ongoing.** The VIX at 14.68 is near its 2026 low, but today alone produced a 21.9% decline in The Trade Desk, a 15.3% drop in Cerebras, and an 8.8% fall in Tapestry — the last of those *despite an earnings beat*, purely on guidance. Index-level complacency is coexisting with extreme constituent dispersion. Position sizing, not index hedging, is the appropriate defense.

**5. Fintech take-rate compression as a secular rather than cyclical force — severity: moderate, probability: moderate.** Most fintech cohorts remain below 2025 year-end valuation levels. If the de-rating reflects a permanent structural shift in payment economics rather than a cyclical trough, then the cheap multiples in this report's spotlight sector are value traps rather than opportunities. dLocal's TPV-to-gross-profit growth decay (73% → 55% → 40%) is the most direct evidence available that this force is real and measurable.

## Bottom Line

Two soft inflation prints bought the market a reprieve, not an all-clear: September hike odds fell to 35%, but three FOMC members already dissented toward tightening and headline CPI sits at 3.4%, so the risk here is asymmetric and skewed against the recent rally. The more actionable observation is the divergence beneath a 14.68 VIX — index calm masking 20%-plus single-day drawdowns on guidance disappointments, which argues for owning cash-generative businesses at defensible multiples rather than paying up for narrative. Today's spotlight sector offers exactly that: financial infrastructure trading at 10–15x forward earnings with accelerating fundamentals, where a Fed staying on hold at 3.50–3.75% quietly supports float and spread economics that the market has priced as though rates were headed to zero.
