## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The decision, and why it is a regime change

The FOMC met September 15–16, with the statement at 14:00 ET and Chair **Kevin Warsh's** press conference at 14:30 ET. Consensus and market pricing both point to a **25bp hike to 3.75%–4.00%**, with implied odds around 90%–92%. This is not a routine meeting. It would be the **first increase since 2023**, and it reverses the direction of policy rather than merely pausing it.

The path here is worth tracing, because it explains the bond market's violence:

- At the **July 28–29** meeting, the Committee voted **9–3** to hold at 3.50%–3.75%, with **Hammack, Kashkari and Logan dissenting in favor of a hike**. Three dissents in the hawkish direction is an unusually loud signal from a Committee that normally prizes consensus.
- Before **Jackson Hole**, markets put roughly **70%** odds on a September hold.
- Warsh's Jackson Hole keynote flipped that. Kalshi odds for a 25bp hike jumped to **48%** immediately after, and the 2-year yield jumped on his "have work to do" language.
- By **August 28**, the September decision was described as a coin flip. By **September 15**, the market was at **~90%** for a hike.

That is a full repricing of the policy path inside three weeks — which is precisely why the long end has gapped to 5.04% and why equity multiples are under pressure irrespective of earnings.

### The inflation problem Warsh is responding to

- **Headline CPI: 3.4% y/y in August**, unchanged from the prior month — the stall is the problem, not the level.
- **Core: 2.4% y/y.**
- Warsh has flagged explicitly that **underlying inflation is not slowing**.
- **Energy is re-accelerating into the print**: Brent at a four-month high and diesel at ~$6/gal feed directly into goods and freight costs with a lag. A central bank that cut into a disinflation it now cannot finish has every incentive to act before the energy pass-through arrives.

### The labor market is not the constraint

The August Employment Situation gave Warsh the cover to hike:

- **Payrolls +162,000** (one source reports +142,000 — the discrepancy is worth noting but does not change the signal).
- **Unemployment 4.1%**, unchanged and in line with expectations.
- **Labor force +683,000**, lifting participation to **61.6%** from a five-year low of 61.4% — supply is coming back in, which is disinflationary at the margin but also removes the "labor market is cracking" argument against tightening.
- **U-6 eased to 7.7% from 7.9%.**
- Retail employment fell 8,400, though the BLS attributed much of that to disruptions at a single New England grocery chain — a statistical artifact, not a trend.

A 4.1% unemployment rate with rising participation is simply not a labor market that forces a central bank to tolerate 3.4% headline inflation.

### What to watch this week

1. **14:00 ET today** — statement, and critically the **dot plot**. The hike is ~90% priced; the terminal-rate path in the SEP is not.
2. **14:30 ET today** — Warsh's press conference. The question that matters: is this a one-off insurance hike, or the start of a sequence? A "one and done" framing is worth more to equities than the hike itself costs.
3. **The 10-year's reaction.** A hike that *lowers* the long end (credibility restored) is bullish for duration-sensitive equities. A hike that pushes the 10-year toward 5.25% is not.
4. **Fed–Treasury friction.** Analysts flagged after Jackson Hole that a hawkish Warsh may put the Fed "at odds" with Treasury. Any sign of that tension is a risk-premium event.

### The mechanical consequence for equities

A 5% risk-free rate is a valuation ceiling. At 5%, the equity risk premium on an S&P 500 trading near 7,600 is thin, and the discount rate applied to long-duration cash flows — which is what high-multiple software *is* — rises mechanically. This matters directly for today's spotlight: part of the software de-rating attributed to AI disruption is in fact **duration repricing**, and the two have been conflated. Distinguishing them is where the opportunity lies.

## Geopolitical Factors

**Iran remains the dominant energy risk.** US–Iran tensions have re-entered the pricing mechanism of oil and gas markets, with the Iranian regime's internal political crisis deepening, intensifying protests, and repeated warnings that the US could intervene.

The year's trajectory has been extraordinary:

- **Early March:** US and Israeli military action against Iran triggered rapid regional escalation and disrupted global oil supply. **Brent briefly exceeded $110/bbl** — a multi-year high — and approached **$117** in some sessions.
- **Mid-June:** prices collapsed **below $70/bbl** after the Strait of Hormuz reopened and supply/demand rebalanced.
- **Now:** Brent back to a four-month high in the $105–$110 zone, +9% last week.

**The Strait of Hormuz handles roughly 20% of the world's oil shipments.** Any interruption reverberates through global energy markets within hours. Several Middle Eastern producers cut output during the spring escalation on security grounds, some facilities were attacked directly, and transport operations were impaired.

**The forecasting community is split**, which is itself informative:

- J.P. Morgan's end-2026 projection is **$78/bbl**, cut from a prior **$95** — a call that the geopolitical premium deflates.
- Goldman projects lower 2026 prices as supply swells.
- Against that, ING argues lingering geopolitical uncertainty requires "a crude rethink," and Energy Aspects has documented repeated geopolitical shocks roiling energy markets through the year.

**Why this matters today specifically:** the Fed is hiking into an energy-price upswing. If Brent holds above $105, the headline CPI stall at 3.4% becomes a headline CPI *re-acceleration* in the October and November prints, and a single insurance hike becomes a sequence. The oil tape is, right now, a leading indicator for the terminal rate.

**Trade and tariffs** remain a second-order but persistent input, most visibly in semiconductor supply chains — see the TSMC pricing discussion below, where 2027 wafer price increases of up to 10% on mature nodes are being pushed through and will eventually reach end-product pricing.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Enterprise Software & SaaS

*(Rotating spotlight: day-of-year 259 mod 6 = 1 → Enterprise Software & SaaS)*

#### The setup: the worst sector de-rating of the modern software era

2026 has delivered a violent, sector-wide repricing of enterprise software. The numbers are stark:

| Metric | Before | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Median forward P/E, software | ~39x | ~21x |
| Software P/S | 9x | 6x |
| Median public SaaS EV/Revenue | 18.6x (2021 peak) | ~6–7x |
| iShares Expanded Tech-Software ETF | — | **−21%+ YTD** |

Dispersion within that median is enormous: top-quartile names still carry **13–14x** EV/Revenue while the bottom quartile trades at **1–2x**. And the headline fact that defines the moment — **for the first time ever, software trades at a discount to the S&P 500.**

The market-cap destruction is concentrated in the largest names. Salesforce, Workday and Adobe have shed a combined **$2 trillion** in market value across the disruption cycle. At the trough of the February–April drawdown, Salesforce was **−33% YTD**, ServiceNow **−45%** over twelve months, and Workday **−30% YTD**. Roughly **$300 billion** evaporated in the initial February leg alone.

#### The bear case, stated fairly

The argument is not stupid, and it deserves to be stated at its strongest:

**Per-seat pricing is an AI-fragile business model.** SaaS vendors charge per user. If one AI agent performs the work of ten people, a customer needing 1,000 seats today needs 100 tomorrow. The entire predictable-recurring-revenue architecture of the industry rests on headcount growth at its customers — and agentic AI is explicitly a headcount-substitution technology. This is not a demand shock that mean-reverts; it is a *unit-of-value* shock.

**Budgets are already moving.** Surveys indicate roughly **40% of IT budgets are being reallocated** from traditional SaaS subscriptions toward agentic platforms and LLM token consumption. That is a real, observable reallocation, not a narrative.

**The April confirmation.** Software stocks plunged on **ServiceNow and IBM results in late April** as AI fears escalated — the market took results from bellwethers as evidence the thesis was showing up in the numbers.

#### The bull case, and why the selloff over-generalized

**First, the rebound already happened once, hard.** In late July, the group staged one of the sharpest single-day reversals of the year: **Salesforce +7% to $176, ServiceNow +8% to $107, Workday +10% to $149**. The trigger was simple — when large-cap SaaS names reported *durable growth*, the risk premium that had been baked into the entire group repriced. ServiceNow and Salesforce extended the rebound in subsequent sessions, helped by government AI deals. The lesson: the de-rating was applied to the sector indiscriminately, and it un-derates on evidence, name by name.

**Second, JPMorgan's view.** JPMorgan analysts have argued the software selloff "has gone too far," calling the AI-disruption fears **"overblown"** and based on **"broken logic."**

**Third — and this is the core analytical point of today's report — the disruption risk is pricing-model-specific, not sector-wide.** The seat-substitution argument has real teeth against software priced per human user. It has *far less* teeth against software priced against:

- **Transaction or message volume** (the customer's throughput, not its headcount)
- **Regulatory or compliance obligations** (which scale with jurisdictions and rules, not employees)
- **Assets, infrastructure or data under management** (which AI adoption tends to *increase*)
- **Network participation** (where the value is the counterparties, and no agent can synthesize a trading partner that isn't there)

An AI agent that replaces ten accounts-payable clerks still generates the same number of invoices requiring the same tax determinations across the same jurisdictions. An AI agent running a logistics desk still files the same customs declarations against the same shipment volume. An agent-driven supply chain still needs an EDI connection to Walmart. In several cases agentic adoption is *revenue-accretive* to these vendors, because agents transact faster and more often than the humans they replace.

The market has, in 2026, applied a uniform multiple compression across a sector whose exposure to the thesis is radically non-uniform. **That is the mispricing.**

#### Where the survivors are

Screening for the intersection of *(a)* a de-rated multiple, *(b)* real free cash flow rather than adjusted-EBITDA storytelling, and *(c)* a pricing model not indexed to customer headcount produces a short and unglamorous list — and it is deliberately not where the crowd is looking. The broadly-circulated "reasonably valued SaaS" lists still lead with Microsoft, Adobe, Salesforce, Shopify, Palantir, ServiceNow and CrowdStrike, which is precisely the seat-priced, mega-cap cohort carrying the most thesis risk and the most crowding. A P/S below 5 is the conventional reasonable-valuation floor for software; the names below mostly clear it, and the two that don't clear it on sales clear it emphatically on cash.

Six names carry today's picks. All six sit outside the megacap complex; five of the six are priced on something other than seats.

### Artificial Intelligence

The capital-formation story continues to dwarf the revenue story, and the financing structures are becoming genuinely novel.

**Nvidia's vendor-financing architecture.** Nvidia is providing up to **$105 billion in financing** for an OpenAI data center in Ohio, supporting an initial **4.25 GW** of compute with an option for a further **3.75 GW**. SB Energy builds and manages the facility at the PORTS-Pike Technology Campus in Pike County under a **20-year lease** to OpenAI, with Nvidia supplying the compute and capacity phasing in from **2028**. Separately, Nvidia and OpenAI have been in talks over a backstop arrangement reported at up to **$250 billion**, and the two announced a **$100 billion** strategic partnership to build **10 GW** of AI data centers.

**The $500 billion third-party vehicle.** Nvidia has teamed with six large asset managers to build financing platforms deploying **$500 billion of third-party capital** into data center projects. This is the structurally important development: it moves AI infrastructure funding off the hyperscaler balance sheet and into institutional credit markets. That is bullish for build velocity and unambiguously negative for systemic transparency — the leverage becomes harder to see, and it is being created into a rising-rate environment.

**Hyperscaler capex:** Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle have collectively committed **$660–690 billion** of 2026 capex, close to double 2025 levels.

**The gap that defines the risk.** Pure-play AI vendors led by OpenAI and Anthropic are posting rapid revenue growth, but **their combined revenues remain a fraction of the infrastructure being deployed on their behalf.** Every element of the bull case depends on that gap closing on schedule. With the 10-year at 5.04% and Nvidia-affiliated vehicles raising half a trillion dollars of third-party capital, the financing cost of being wrong about the timing has gone up materially — today, specifically.

**The circularity to keep in view:** a chip vendor financing its customer's purchase of its own chips, with the debt increasingly syndicated to third parties, is a structure that works beautifully while end demand compounds and stops working abruptly if it doesn't.

### 1. VERX — Vertex, Inc. *(Indirect tax & e-invoicing compliance software)*

**Thesis.** Vertex sells indirect tax determination to large enterprises — the software that decides what sales tax, VAT and GST applies to each transaction across thousands of jurisdictions. The stock has been sold down to roughly **$12.48** (as of July 14), hovering near a **52-week low of $10.21**, on the generic SaaS de-rating, despite the fact that its revenue scales with *its customers' transaction volume and the number of tax jurisdictions they touch* — neither of which an AI agent reduces. If anything, agentic commerce increases transaction counts.

**Valuation metrics.** Market cap approximately **$2.0–2.6B** (non-affiliate equity value was $2.55B as of June 30, 2025). Against 2026 revenue guidance of **$823.5–831.5M**, that is roughly **2.5–3.2x sales** — comfortably below the "under 5x P/S" reasonable floor and less than half the 6x sector median. Adjusted EBITDA guidance of **$188–192M** implies **EV/EBITDA in the low-to-mid teens**. GAAP P/E is not the right lens here — the company is in the margin-inflection phase of its transition, and the trailing multiple is distorted by it. Consensus price target **$18.44** (median $19.00), implying roughly **+52%** upside.

**Growth profile.** Q2 2026 revenue **+10.5% y/y to $204M**. Adjusted EBITDA **+33%** with margin expanding to **25%** — growth of profits at triple the rate of revenue is the entire investment case. **Cloud revenue guided to +18%** for FY26. The e-invoicing business is growing at an accelerating rate in its first full year, which matters disproportionately: mandatory e-invoicing regimes are being rolled out by governments worldwide, and each new mandate is a non-discretionary purchase trigger.

**Competitive moat.** Tax content is the moat, and it is an unusually durable one. Vertex maintains determination rules across thousands of taxing jurisdictions that change continuously; that content library takes decades to build and must be maintained forever. It embeds directly into SAP, Oracle and NetSuite ERP systems at the transaction layer — the single hardest place in an enterprise stack to rip out. And the buying decision is made by tax and audit functions, where the cost of being wrong is a regulatory penalty, not a productivity loss. That is the most switching-cost-resistant buying committee in enterprise software.

**Key risk.** The obvious one is that the ERP vendors themselves — SAP and Oracle, both of whom are partners — decide to build native tax determination and compress Vertex out of the stack. The second is execution: at 10.5% revenue growth Vertex is not fast enough to outrun a multiple problem on its own, so the thesis depends on margin expansion continuing at the Q2 pace. Both are live. The price already discounts a fair amount of each.

**Rating: Buy.**

### 2. SPSC — SPS Commerce, Inc. *(Retail supply-chain network / cloud EDI)*

**Thesis.** SPS runs the plumbing of retail supply chains — cloud EDI connecting more than **50,000 retailers, brands and suppliers** including Walmart, Costco, Macy's, Best Buy, Adidas and Hershey. The stock de-rated hard when management guided 2026 revenue growth to **6–7%** after delivering **18% in 2025**, taking it from a 52-week high above **$140** to as low as the **$60s** before recovering to roughly **$82** (September 15). A network business does not lose its network because its growth rate halves for a year.

**Valuation metrics.** Market cap approximately **$3.0B**. Roughly **30x earnings** and **3.5x sales** — but the number that matters is a **free-cash-flow multiple of ~14x**, with Q2 FCF of about **$57M**. A 14x FCF multiple on a genuine network asset with mid-90s% recurring revenue is not a premium valuation; it is a valuation that prices in permanent deceleration.

**Growth profile.** Guided to **6–7%** for 2026 against 18% in 2025 — the deceleration is real and should not be minimized. Roughly **mid-90% of sales are recurring**, with a long record of consecutive quarterly revenue growth. Gross margin **~70%**, EBIT margin in the low teens, both above Software & IT Services medians. The company beat in Q2 and lifted its EPS outlook, and has been executing buybacks.

**Competitive moat.** This is a two-sided network, which is the strongest moat structure in software and the one least exposed to the AI thesis. A supplier connects to SPS because Walmart is on it; Walmart stays because 50,000 suppliers are on it. An AI agent cannot synthesize a trading partner that isn't connected. Crucially, SPS is paid on **connections and document volume, not seats** — a customer that automates its EDI desk with agents sends the same number of documents, or more.

**Key risk.** The **strategic review is the double-edged catalyst**. Reuters reported on June 23 that SPS engaged Morgan Stanley to explore a sale under pressure from **Anson Funds and Irenic Capital**, with a private-equity buyer the most natural marginal acquirer. A deal caps the upside at a takeout premium; a *failed* process removes the floor the speculation has been providing and leaves a 6–7% grower to be re-rated on fundamentals alone. The underlying risk is that retail-sector cyclicality makes 6–7% the new normal rather than a trough.

**Rating: Buy.**

### 3. TENB — Tenable Holdings, Inc. *(Exposure management & vulnerability security)*

**Thesis.** Tenable is priced at **$21.34** for a **2.4x forward price-to-sales** multiple and a **0.7x PEG** — a valuation that implies structural decline in a business that is growing with positive cash margins. Security spending is the last line item an enterprise cuts, and Tenable's pricing is indexed to the **number of assets scanned**, not the number of employees. Agentic AI expands the attack surface — more machine identities, more autonomous agents with credentials, more API endpoints — which is a direct tailwind to an asset-priced vulnerability-management vendor.

**Valuation metrics.** Share price **$21.34**, implying roughly **$2.4–2.6B** market cap against approximately **$1.0B** of forward revenue. **2.4x forward P/S** against a 6x software median and a 13–14x top quartile. **PEG of 0.7x** — below 1.0, which for a security asset is the clearest signal on this list that growth is not in the price. Consensus target **$40**, rising to **$50 by 2028**.

**Growth profile.** Consistent revenue growth with positive cash margins. The company is past the phase where it was buying growth with losses, which is exactly the transition the 2026 market rewards and the 2021 market ignored.

**Competitive moat.** Tenable's Nessus scanning engine is effectively the industry reference standard for vulnerability assessment, with two decades of vulnerability signature accumulation behind it. Displacing it means re-baselining an entire estate and losing historical remediation data — a migration that creates an audit gap most CISOs will not accept. Exposure management is also a compliance-adjacent purchase (PCI, FedRAMP, cyber-insurance requirements), which makes it a budget line with regulatory backing rather than a discretionary tool.

**Key risk.** Competitive intensity is severe and genuinely asymmetric: Tenable competes against Qualys and Rapid7 at its own scale, and against CrowdStrike, Microsoft Defender and Palo Alto — all of whom bundle vulnerability management into broader platforms and can effectively give it away. Platform consolidation is the real threat to a point solution, and the 2.4x multiple is partly the market pricing that. The acquisition-candidate angle noted by analysts is a consequence of the same dynamic, and is a reason the downside is somewhat protected rather than a reason to own it.

**Rating: Buy.**

### 4. PRGS — Progress Software Corporation *(Infrastructure software & data platform roll-up)*

**Thesis.** The deepest value name on this list. Progress trades at a **forward P/E of 7.67** — down at **5.39** as recently as June 3 — with management guiding **$263–275M of adjusted free cash flow** for FY2026 against a **levered FCF yield of 16–17%**. This is an acquire-and-harvest infrastructure software operator being priced as if its cash flows are impaired, when the actual issue is a leveraged balance sheet in a rising-rate world — a risk that is real, quantifiable, and being actively paid down.

**Valuation metrics.** **Trailing P/E 31.69, forward P/E 7.67** — the gap between the two is the entire story, and it reflects acquisition amortization depressing GAAP earnings rather than any deterioration in cash generation. The stock trades at roughly a **50% discount to its own 3- and 5-year average GAAP P/E** and about **one-third of the sector P/S average**. TTM operating cash flow **$176.25M** against capex of only **$5.73M** for **$170.53M** of trailing FCF, with FY2026 guidance of **$263–275M** adjusted. **Levered FCF yield of 16–17%** is the anchor.

**Growth profile.** Progress does not grow organically in any meaningful way — it acquires, integrates, and harvests. Judging it on revenue growth is the wrong lens, and the market's insistence on doing so is part of why it is cheap. The relevant metrics are FCF per share and debt paydown. On **July 22, 2026** it agreed to acquire substantially all assets of the **Data Platform Business of Domo, Inc. for approximately $400M** (net **$355M**), targeting AI data platform positioning and margin improvement through integration.

**Competitive moat.** The moat is switching cost on embedded infrastructure — OpenEdge, Chef, MOVEit, WhatsUp Gold and the rest sit inside customer systems where replacement is a multi-year project with no visible business benefit. These are products customers renew because ripping them out is harder than paying. That is an unglamorous moat and a highly durable one, and it is close to immune to the seat-substitution thesis because the software is not licensed to humans doing work.

**Key risk.** **Leverage, and it is the real thing.** $102.01M of cash against **$1.49B of debt** — a net debt position of **$1.39B**. Management guides to **$250M of debt repayment** in FY2026, which is credible against $263–275M of FCF, but it means essentially all free cash flow is committed to the balance sheet rather than to shareholders. In a rising-rate environment — and the Fed is hiking *today* — refinancing costs are the direct threat to the thesis. Deleveraging is the catalyst and the risk simultaneously: it works if rates stabilize, and it compresses if the Fed delivers a sequence rather than a single hike. There is also integration risk on Domo. This is the highest-risk, highest-return name in the group and should be sized accordingly.

**Rating: Accumulate.**

### 5. NTNX — Nutanix, Inc. *(Hybrid cloud & distributed infrastructure software)*

**Thesis.** Nutanix generated **$840.7M of free cash flow in FY26** on **$2.85B of revenue** — a free-cash-flow margin approaching 30% — while the stock sits roughly **39% below its all-time high**. It is priced on GAAP earnings, where stock compensation and the residual of its subscription transition distort the picture, rather than on cash, where it is one of the more efficient businesses in infrastructure software. Nutanix is also the primary structural beneficiary of VMware-related displacement following Broadcom's licensing changes — a multi-year migration tailwind that has nothing to do with AI sentiment.

**Valuation metrics.** Market cap approximately **$17–18B** at roughly **$66/share** (derived from the $76.41 average target and its stated +15.44% implied upside). **EV/FCF in the low-20s** against $840.7M of FY26 free cash flow. On P/E the stock genuinely screens expensive — it trades above both its peer group and the wider software industry, and an honest assessment must concede that. A DCF points to intrinsic value near **$80/share**, roughly **17% above** market. KeyBanc raised its target to **$79 from $75**; the 15-analyst average is **$76.41**.

**Growth profile.** FY26 revenue **+12%**, **ARR +16% y/y** — ARR outgrowing revenue is the healthy configuration, indicating the subscription base is compounding faster than recognized revenue. Q4 FY26 free cash flow **$277.6M**, up from **$207.8M** a year earlier, a **+34%** increase. Growth is being driven by expansion beyond core HCI into external storage, AI workloads and broader hybrid cloud.

**Competitive moat.** Nutanix is a **Leader in the 2026 Gartner Magic Quadrant for Distributed Hybrid Infrastructure**, cited for both Ability to Execute and Completeness of Vision. The practical moat is migration cost: hypervisor and storage-layer infrastructure is the single most disruptive thing in an enterprise to replace, which cuts both ways — it is why Nutanix wins slowly and why it loses almost nothing once won. Licensing is capacity- and node-based, not per-seat.

**Key risk.** The GAAP multiple leaves no room for a growth stumble — at 12% revenue growth and a premium P/E, any deceleration gets punished twice. The VMware displacement tailwind is finite and partially harvested; the market has known about it for two years. And the competitive set is formidable in the extreme: Nutanix is squeezed between the public clouds above and Broadcom/VMware's installed base below. *(Coverage note: NTNX was last picked in the July 30 report — seven weeks ago and well outside the seven-report exclusion window. It is included on the strength of the FY26 cash flow print, which post-dates that report.)*

**Rating: Accumulate.**

### 6. DSGX — The Descartes Systems Group Inc. *(Global trade, customs compliance & logistics network)*

**Thesis.** The highest-quality business on this list, at the highest price — deliberately included as the quality anchor. Descartes operates the **Global Logistics Network**, and charges customers **to send and receive messages, data and documents across it**, with customers contracting for monthly minimums over multi-year terms. It is a toll booth on global trade flows. In an environment where tariffs, sanctions regimes and customs complexity are *increasing*, the compliance burden Descartes automates is growing structurally, and none of it is priced per seat.

**Valuation metrics.** Market cap **$6.36B**, with the stock trading between **$75.47 and $80.29** on September 14. **Trailing P/E 38.78, forward P/E 27.14, EV/EBITDA 18.64.** This is the only name in today's group that does not screen cheap on an absolute basis, and it should not be bought as a value stock. The forward-to-trailing P/E compression from 38.8x to 27.1x reflects expected earnings growth; on a PEG basis against low-double-digit revenue growth and faster earnings growth, it lands near fair value rather than at a discount. Analyst average target **$99.79** (15 analysts, "Buy" consensus), implying **+28.6%**.

**Growth profile.** FY2026 revenue **$728.99M, +11.98%** from $651.00M. Growth has been consistent in the low double digits, with Descartes compounding through a disciplined tuck-in acquisition program layered onto organic network expansion.

**Competitive moat.** The Global Logistics Network is a genuine network effect — carriers, brokers, forwarders and shippers all connect to reach each other, and the value of joining scales with who is already there. Layered on top is a customs and trade-content moat structurally similar to Vertex's tax content: denied-party screening lists, tariff schedules and customs regulations across every major jurisdiction, maintained continuously. The transaction-based pricing model is the specific point of the inclusion — Descartes is paid on **document and message volume**, which agentic automation *increases* by making its customers transact faster.

**Key risk.** Valuation is the risk, plainly stated. At 27x forward earnings in a market where the 10-year yields 5.04%, Descartes has the least multiple support of any name here, and a rate-driven de-rating would hit it hardest. The business is also directly levered to global trade volumes — a genuine trade contraction, as opposed to trade *complexity*, hits message volumes directly. Buy on weakness rather than at the highs.

**Rating: Accumulate.**

## Risk Factors

**1. A hawkish dot plot, not the hike itself — SEVERE.** The 25bp move is ~90% priced and is not the risk. The risk is the **Summary of Economic Projections** and Warsh's press conference framing this as the *start of a cycle* rather than a one-off insurance move. With core at 2.4% and headline stalled at 3.4%, there is a coherent case for a sequence. Equities are positioned for one and done. This is today's binary, and it resolves at 14:00 and 14:30 ET.

**2. The 10-year at 5.04% — SEVERE.** The long end is at its highest since 2007, having broken 5% for the first time since 2023. At a 5% risk-free rate the equity risk premium on an S&P near 7,600 is thin, and every long-duration cash flow reprices mechanically. This is the dominant threat to today's entire pick list and to the AI infrastructure complex, which is now financing itself through third-party credit markets at exactly the wrong moment in the rate cycle.

**3. Oil re-accelerating into a Fed that is already hiking — HIGH.** Brent at a four-month high above $105 after +9% in a week, with diesel at ~$6/gal and Iran escalation risk unresolved. If energy holds these levels, the 3.4% headline stall becomes a re-acceleration in the October and November prints, converting risk #1 from a possibility into a certainty. The Hormuz chokepoint — 20% of global oil shipments — remains the tail risk that turns this from a headwind into a shock.

**4. AI capex circularity meeting a 5% funding cost — HIGH.** $660–690B of committed hyperscaler capex, Nvidia financing $105B for a single OpenAI facility, a reported $250B backstop discussion, and a $500B third-party capital vehicle with six asset managers — against pure-play AI revenues that remain "a fraction" of the infrastructure being deployed. The structure is sound while end demand compounds on schedule and while capital is cheap. Today, capital gets more expensive. The off-balance-sheet syndication of this leverage means the market cannot easily see where the exposure sits.

**5. The AI-disruption thesis proving right where it is currently only feared — MODERATE.** 40% of IT budgets reallocating toward agentic platforms and token consumption is an observable trend, not a forecast. Today's spotlight argues the risk is concentrated in seat-priced software and that transaction-, compliance- and network-priced vendors are being wrongly tarred. If that distinction fails to hold — if enterprises cut *total* software spend rather than reallocating within it — the entire basket above de-rates together. The July reversal (CRM +7%, NOW +8%, WDAY +10% in one session) is evidence the market will pay for proof; it is not yet proof.

---

## Bottom Line

Nothing that matters today happens before 14:00 ET. The hike is priced; the *path* is not, and the distance between "insurance move" and "start of a cycle" is worth several hundred S&P points and considerably more than that to anything with duration. Into that, the differentiated opportunity is in enterprise software — a sector now trading at a discount to the S&P 500 for the first time in history, where an undifferentiated AI-disruption discount has been applied to businesses whose exposure to the thesis ranges from existential to essentially zero. Own the ones paid on transactions, compliance obligations and network participation rather than on headcount, insist on real free cash flow rather than adjusted-EBITDA narrative, size the basket as the single concentrated sector bet that it is, and recognise that this positioning defends against the AI thesis but not against a 5% ten-year.
