## Macro & Monetary Environment

**Where the Fed actually is.** The effective fed funds rate stood at 3.88% as of the Fed's October 7 H.15 release, inside a 3.75–4.00% target range after September's unanimous quarter-point *hike*. That word matters: the Fed spent early 2026 on hold at 3.50–3.75% and then reversed into tightening because core PCE has printed above 3% every single month of this year. The September Summary of Economic Projections put 2026 headline and core PCE at 3.7% and 3.4%, and — the line that should anchor every valuation model on the Street — policymakers do not forecast a return to the 2% target until **2029**.

**Where the October 27–28 meeting sits.** The market has swung hard dovish in the last two weeks, and the sources genuinely conflict:

- Writing in late September, Forbes reported fixed-income traders viewed October 28 as the most likely point for the next move, and that move would be a hike.
- More recently, the September employment report landed at just **+29,000 payrolls**, and futures-implied odds of a 25bp October hike collapsed to roughly 17–20%.
- As of October 7, StreetStats has markets anticipating no change in October, with roughly 4.1% priced by January 2027 and roughly 4.7% by October 2027.

Read those together and the message is that the market now expects the Fed to *pause in October and resume hiking later*. That is a far more hawkish terminal path than anything priced in the spring, and it is being priced while the S&P sits near a record. The reconciliation is Q3 earnings: FactSet estimates S&P 500 Q3 earnings grew **29.5%** year over year. Equity holders are being paid in earnings growth for a rate path that would, in isolation, be fatal to multiples.

**The calendar.** September CPI lands **October 14**, with further prints November 10 and December 10 — note the December release falls *after* the December FOMC, so the Fed goes into its final meeting of the year with one fewer datapoint than the market. September industrial production publishes October 16; it will be the first hard read on whether the energy cost shock is slowing real output, since the August figure was flat at 0.0% m/m. The August trade deficit has already widened to a 17-month high of **$105.6 billion**.

**The uncomfortable combination.** A +29k payroll print alongside a 77.9 ISM prices-paid reading is the signature of a supply shock, not a demand cycle. Weak labor plus hot prices is the one configuration in which the Fed's dual mandate pulls in opposite directions, and it is the configuration in which the Fed historically chooses the inflation side of the mandate. Investors treating soft payrolls as automatically dovish are, I think, making an error this cycle.

## Geopolitical Factors

The Iran war is no longer a risk factor to be hedged — it is the operating environment, and it is now **day 221** of disrupted Strait of Hormuz transit.

**Oil.** Brent has struggled to break decisively below $100 even as Gulf crude exports recovered, because the cost structure around moving that crude has permanently reset. Earlier this week prices actually *fell* on signs Middle East exports were approaching pre-war levels and that G7 nations had agreed to tap strategic stockpiles; today's 3.7% reversal came on reports the US president is weighing further strikes ahead of the midterms. The IEA has been releasing stocks into the market. The US and Iran have not reached a deal.

**The freight and insurance channel — this is the underappreciated part.** The physical cost of moving oil has gone vertical in ways that do not reverse when a headline improves:

- Chartering a VLCC to move US crude to Asia now costs roughly **$77 million**, against a 2025 average of $9.2 million (Baltic Exchange, per Bloomberg, October 7).
- VLCC day rates have reached roughly **$1.3 million/day**, about **43 times** early-January levels; Persian Gulf–to-China fixtures hit $1.035 million/day in September versus roughly $208,000/day pre-war.
- War-risk insurance on Hormuz transits has gone from a historical 1–3% of hull value to roughly **7.5–10%**, and Marsh reports underwriters are increasingly unwilling to quote spot terms at any price.

**On traffic volumes, the sources genuinely disagree and I will not paper over it.** Kpler data cited by CNBC puts shipments at roughly 10.3 million bpd for the week ended Saturday, about 23% below a 13.5 million bpd pre-war baseline. A live tracker claims the strait is effectively closed to commercial shipping as of October 8, with transits at 5% of pre-crisis vessel counts as of October 4 — that source labels its own flow reporting "contested." The honest position is that flows are somewhere between "down a quarter" and "nearly stopped," and the spread between those two scenarios is worth $40 a barrel. Size positions accordingly.

**The investable conclusion.** Freight, insurance and routing inefficiency are now a structural premium embedded in the oil price, not a spike. Even a ceasefire would leave war-risk underwriting capacity impaired for quarters. This argues for owning the *cost pass-through* layer of the energy complex rather than the commodity itself, and against owning anyone whose margins depend on cheap distillate.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Industrials, Energy, Materials & Picks-and-Shovels

*(Rotation: day-of-year 281; 281 mod 6 = 5.)*

This bucket was also the October 2 spotlight, so the discipline today is to go nowhere near the ground that report covered — aggregates, metals fabrication, contract gas compression, flow control and infrastructure structures — nor the oilfield-equipment, fertilizer, precious-metals and uranium ground covered on September 14. What follows is deliberately routed into government services, additive chemistry, industrial gases, rail equipment and agricultural machinery.

**The macro setup for this bucket is the best it has been all year, and the market has partially noticed.** ISM Manufacturing printed **54.5** in September, the ninth consecutive month of expansion, with New Orders *accelerating* to 55.3 from 53.7. Employment crossed back above 50 to 52.7. Production slipped to 56.7 from 58.3. Demand is fine. The problem is entirely on the cost line: prices paid at 77.9, up 6.8 points, with respondents also naming memory and electronic components among active shortages — a rare case of the AI capex cycle showing up as an input constraint on conventional manufacturers.

**What that means for stock selection.** A 54.5 PMI with a 77.9 prices index separates industrials into two populations. Companies that price on a cost-plus, contractual or formula basis expand margins into this. Companies that bid fixed-price work, hold inventory, or burn distillate as a primary input have their margins taken from them. Almost all of the sector's dispersion from here is explained by which side of that line a business sits on, and very little of it is explained by end-market growth. I have weighted today's picks almost entirely toward the pass-through side.

**On refining — the obvious trade, and why I am not taking it.** Marathon, Valero and HF Sinclair have each gained over 80% this year against the S&P's low-teens. The WTI 3-2-1 crack reached a record ~$69.66/bbl, with the diesel crack above $91. HF Sinclair's Q2 net income was $892 million against $208 million a year earlier. The structural argument — roughly 5–6 MMb/d of global refining capacity offline — is real, not promotional. I am still passing, for three specific reasons. First, the forward curve disagrees with the spot print: August 2027 Nymex 3:2:1 futures sit more than 35% below the September contract. Second, Benzinga notes the average analyst target on Valero is now **31% below** the share price — the sell side, which is structurally reluctant to be bearish, cannot justify the tape. Third, the 2022 analogue saw cracks collapse toward mid-cycle within 18 months. Buying peak-cycle commodity margins at a peak multiple after an 80% run is not a valuation-based decision, whatever the crack spread says today. The entire bull case is a geopolitical premium that reverses on a headline. Mizuho's downgrade of Phillips 66 to Neutral this morning is a reasonable first crack in that consensus.

**Energy services, likewise fully priced.** Baker Hughes screens as the thoughtful compromise — a record ~$33 billion backlog, IET orders running above $3 billion a quarter, and a credible AI-data-center gas-turbine order pipeline. But at roughly $55.73 (October 7) it trades at ~18.4x trailing and **21.2x forward with a PEG near 3.94**, bracketed by fair-value estimates of ~19.8x and ~22.1x. That is a fairly-valued stock with added leverage from the Chart Industries acquisition. No edge.

**Where the edge actually is:** businesses selling into inelastic, contractually-priced demand that are *not* commodity price bets. Defense services, lubricant additives, industrial gases and rail aftermarket all share the same structure — multi-year contracts, cost pass-through or surcharge mechanics, and demand that does not care about the ten-year yield. Three of today's five picks are explicitly that trade. The fourth (rail) is an installed-base aftermarket annuity. The fifth (agriculture) is the one genuine contrarian cyclical in the list, taken at a trough rather than a peak — the mirror image of the refining trade I just declined.

### Artificial Intelligence

The dominant AI story has quietly shifted from compute demand to **how the compute is being financed**, and the structures are getting more exotic.

- **Broadcom** is reportedly working to arrange **more than $50 billion** to fund the custom accelerators it is co-developing with OpenAI, with Broadcom itself organizing the financing (WSJ). Separate reporting puts a bank syndicate near **$60 billion** for AI chip purchases.
- **SpaceX**, now home to xAI, has discussed borrowing roughly **$40 billion** for Nvidia GPUs.
- **Oracle** is in talks with Apollo and Goldman Sachs to finance chips for a new data center.
- Background to all of it: Nvidia's up-to-**$105 billion** financing commitment for OpenAI's Ohio campus (August), supporting an initial 4.25GW with an option for 3.75GW more, on top of the earlier 10GW letter of intent and up-to-$100 billion staged investment.

**The analytical point.** When the chip vendor finances the customer's purchase of its own chips, revenue quality deteriorates even as revenue growth accelerates. This is vendor financing at a scale the technology sector has not attempted since the telecom buildout, and the collateral — depreciating accelerators with a three-to-four year useful life — is materially worse than fiber or spectrum. Nothing here is yet impaired, and the end demand appears real. But investors should stop treating these announcements as pure demand signals. They are increasingly credit events, and they belong in a credit framework. The appearance of memory and electronic components on the ISM shortage list is the first evidence that this capex is now crowding out the conventional economy.

On product, Nvidia used GTC 2026 to introduce an LPX rack built on technology licensed from Groq for $20 billion, alongside the Vera-Rubin NVL72 and Spectrum-6 SPX racks. International buildout continues — OpenAI is adding Malaysian capacity via a Firmus deal deploying Vera Rubin systems, and Indonesia's Zankore platform (Indosat/Ooredoo with Nvidia and Nokia) secured a senior term loan of up to $3.1 billion.

### Software & SaaS

Beyond the HubSpot and Tyler downgrades above, the notable item is how little software is participating in the index's record. The sector's problem is arithmetic, not fundamental: a business whose cash flows are weighted five to ten years out loses roughly a fifth of its present value when the discount rate moves from 4% to 5.3%, before any change to the forecast. With the Fed signaling no return to 2% inflation until 2029, the market is being forced to re-underwrite SaaS terminal values against a permanently higher risk-free rate. Expect continued de-rating of growth-at-any-multiple names and continued relative strength in software with government, defense or regulated-utility revenue — the same rotation visible in today's analyst calls.

### 1. LDOS — Leidos Holdings, Inc. *(Defense technology & government services)*

- **Thesis:** Leidos raised 2026 guidance on accelerating defense-tech demand explicitly tied to Pentagon efforts to replenish weapons stockpiles drawn down by Iran operations and the Russia-Ukraine conflict — and the stock trades at a single-digit earnings multiple anyway. - **Valuation Metrics:** Trailing P/E ~9.2x (Google Finance, ~$100.85) to ~9.7x (July 2026 source). ## Risk Factors

**1. A Hormuz escalation that converts a 23% flow reduction into a closure. (Severity: Critical)** This is the risk that reprices every other line on this page. Brent at $104 already embeds a large premium, but the gap between the two credible flow estimates circulating today — roughly 10.3 mbpd versus near-total closure — is worth tens of dollars a barrel. With tanker rates at 43x January levels and underwriters withdrawing spot capacity, the physical market has very little shock absorption left. Reports of further US strikes ahead of the midterms make the tail fatter, not thinner.

**2. A Fed forced to resume hiking into a weakening labor market. (Severity: High)** September's +29k payrolls has pushed October hike odds down to 17–20%, but futures still price ~4.1% by January and ~4.7% by October 2027. With core PCE above 3% every month this year, prices paid at 77.9, and no projected return to target until 2029, the path of least resistance for the Fed is to pause now and tighten later. Equities are priced for the pause and not for the later. The **October 14 CPI** is the proximate trigger.

**3. Long-end yields at 5.28% / 5.66% compressing equity multiples. (Severity: High)** The S&P's record level is being carried by 29.5% Q3 earnings growth, not by multiple expansion — which means any earnings disappointment arrives with no valuation cushion. The Russell 2000's 1.27% single-day decline and 5.6% monthly drawdown is the leading indicator here: floating-rate borrowers are already transmitting the rate shock. A VIX at 15.08 says this is not hedged.

**4. AI capex increasingly funded by vendor and structured credit. (Severity: Medium-High, rising)** Broadcom arranging $50–60B for OpenAI silicon, Nvidia backstopping up to $105B in Ohio, SpaceX discussing $40B for GPUs, Oracle negotiating chip financing with Apollo and Goldman. Individually rational; collectively this is a credit cycle wearing a technology cycle's clothing, collateralized by assets with a three-to-four-year useful life. The transmission channel into the broad index is that roughly a third of S&P earnings growth now depends on counterparties whose own funding is structured rather than operating cash flow.

**5. Margin compression from the 77.9 ISM prices index. (Severity: Medium)** Demand is fine — PMI 54.5, new orders accelerating to 55.3. The risk is entirely that input costs, including memory and electronic components now appearing as named shortages, outrun pricing power for the half of the industrial complex that bids fixed-price work. **October 16 industrial production** is the first hard read.

## Bottom Line

The market is paying a record price for a record earnings number while the Fed signals no return to 2% inflation until 2029, the long bond sits at 5.66%, and a shipping chokepoint that carries a fifth of the world's crude is in its 221st day of disruption — and the VIX is at 15. That is not a crash signal, but it is a clear instruction about *what kind* of risk to own. Favor businesses that pass costs through by contract over businesses that absorb them: defense services, additive chemistry, industrial gases and rail aftermarket have the pricing architecture this environment rewards, and today's picks are concentrated there deliberately. The trade to avoid is the obvious one — refiners up 80% on record crack spreads that the 2027 futures curve says fall 35%, with sell-side targets already 31% below the share price. Peak margins at peak multiples are not a valuation thesis, however good the headline looks.
