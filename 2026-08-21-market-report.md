## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The Fed is no longer the friend of this market

The FOMC left the funds rate unchanged at **3.50%-3.75%** in July — a fifth consecutive hold — but the vote is what matters: **three members dissented in favor of a 25bp hike.** Markets are now assigning roughly a 30% probability to a *hike* by year end. This is a complete inversion of how the year began, when the consensus trade was multiple cuts.

The mechanism is straightforward and it runs through the oil price. Fed officials now see both headline and core inflation at **2.7% at end-2026**, above target, and explicitly attribute part of that to supply shocks in energy. When crude is up ~50% YTD, the "look through the supply shock" argument gets harder to make every month, because at some point a supply shock that persists for four quarters stops being a shock and starts being the price level.

### The bond market is doing the tightening the Fed isn't

This week's story is a genuinely global long-end repricing:

- U.S. 10-year at the highest since early 2025; the 30-year at its highest since **2007**
- Long-end yields in **Japan and Germany** at multi-decade highs
- Drivers stacked on top of each other: sticky inflation, fiscal deficits, and a heavy wave of corporate issuance meeting thin August liquidity

The U.S. Treasury attempted an intervention this week — a debt buyback plan that produced a one-day relief rally on August 18-19 and then fully unwound by August 20. **That failure is the week's real information.** When a buyback announcement buys you 48 hours, the market is telling you the problem is supply and inflation expectations, not plumbing.

For equity investors the translation is mechanical: a 4.75-4.80% risk-free rate raises the discount rate on every long-duration cash flow, compresses the multiple the market will pay for growth that arrives in 2030, and rewards businesses that generate cash *this year*. That is the single most important input into today's stock selection.

## Geopolitical Factors

### Iran is the macro variable, not a headline risk

This has moved from tail risk to primary driver. The sequence:

- The mid-June Iran-U.S. ceasefire **broke down**, reversing Gulf supply flows
- Brent traded as high as **$105/bbl on July 23**
- Prices dipped toward $80 on renewed diplomatic hope, then rebuilt: Brent has risen **~14%** from that trough
- Treasury Secretary Bessent, Thursday: the U.S. will impose **"some of the toughest sanctions in history"** on Iran, with details Monday August 26 — explicitly framed as collapsing Iran through economic pressure
- Strait of Hormuz traffic is described as **stagnating**

Brent at **$93.96**, WTI at **$86.94**, oil up more than 5% this week and headed for a second consecutive weekly gain.

**Why this matters more than a typical geopolitical premium:** roughly 20% of global oil supply transits Hormuz. Sanctions designed to remove Iranian barrels from the market are, by construction, bullish crude — and an adversary whose export revenue is being eliminated has a diminishing incentive to keep the strait open. The asymmetry is unpleasant: successful sanctions raise oil, and retaliation raises oil more.

Modeled macro cost: Brent sustained at $80 depresses H1 global GDP growth by ~0.6 percentage points annualized. We are $14 above that.

### Other live risks

- **Trade and tariffs** remain a two-way variable. Note that Ross Stores' quarter included a ~$0.60/share *tariff refund* — a reminder that tariff policy is now large enough to swing individual retailer EPS by a third.
- **Fiscal credibility** is now a global equity risk, not just a bond story. When Japanese, German and U.S. long ends sell off together, the marginal buyer of duration is repricing sovereign borrowing capacity everywhere at once.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Industrials, Energy, Materials & Picks-and-Shovels

This is the sector that the 2026 macro regime was designed for, and the market has figured it out — but unevenly, which is where the opportunity is.

**The structural case has three legs.**

**Leg one: the AI bottleneck moved from silicon to physical infrastructure.** The constraint on data center buildout in 2026-2032 is electricity, transmission, cooling and uptime — not compute availability. The numbers are large enough to reshape industrial order books: global data-center electricity consumption is projected to rise from ~600 TWh in 2026 to **more than 1,000 TWh by 2030**, and hyperscaler expansion is now limited by grid power availability rather than chip supply. Wall Street's 2026 capex estimates for the five largest hyperscalers have **more than doubled since June, from $361B to $733B.** Meanwhile NVIDIA has begun investing directly in power — a $3B stake in Lancium — which is what it looks like when the chip vendor concludes that its customers' binding constraint is megawatts.

The consequence: a contractor pouring foundations, a distributor supplying switchgear, and a pipeline moving gas to a combined-cycle plant all now have AI-linked revenue growth with none of the AI multiple. Names at this physical layer are posting near-triple-digit growth in AI-exposed segments at 18-35x forward earnings while the semiconductor leader trades at 47x.

**Leg two: energy inflation is a revenue line for this sector.** Brent at $94 and up ~50% YTD is a cost for the S&P 500 and a top line for E&P, oilfield services and midstream. In a year where the Fed's own inflation forecast is being driven by energy supply shocks, owning the shock is the cleanest available hedge.

**Leg three: rising discount rates favor short-duration cash flow.** With the 10-year near 4.75-4.80% and the 30-year at 2007 levels, a dollar of 2026 free cash flow is worth materially more relative to a dollar of 2032 free cash flow than it was in January. Industrials, energy and materials are the shortest-duration cash flows in the market.

### Artificial Intelligence

The AI trade is intact at the fundamental level and being repriced at the multiple level — which is exactly what a 4.75% ten-year does.

**Spending is accelerating, not decelerating.** Hyperscaler 2026 capex consensus for Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle has gone from $361B to **$733B since June**. The historical base rate argues the number is still low: consensus implied ~20% capex growth for both 2024 and 2025, and actual growth exceeded 50% in both years. NVIDIA's Q4 FY2026 data center revenue grew 75% YoY, with full-year FY2026 data center revenue near **$93.7B**, and the company captures roughly **41.5 cents of every hyperscaler AI hardware dollar**.

**The interesting development is vertical integration into power.** NVIDIA's $3B Lancium investment is a compute company buying into electricity supply. When the marginal seller starts financing its customers' power, the bottleneck has been publicly identified — and that identification is worth more to the industrial complex supplying that power than to the chip vendor.

**The trade this creates:** AI-linked revenue growth is now available across the physical layer — electrical distribution, transmission contracting, cement and aggregates, gas pipelines — at industrial multiples. Owning AI through a switchgear distributor at 22x rather than a GPU designer at 47x is not a bearish AI view. It is the same view expressed at a lower discount-rate sensitivity.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

Semis sold off with rates this week but the supply-side picture is as tight as it has been in this cycle.

**Foundry:** TSMC holds an estimated **73% of the global foundry market**, up from 69% in Q4 2024, and has reportedly **sold out its entire 2nm capacity for 2026** — with 2nm priced at a **10-20% premium to 3nm**. Selling out a node before it ramps, at a premium price, is the definition of pricing power, and capex is rising again in 2026 with a significant share directed at advanced nodes.

**Equipment:** rising TSMC advanced-node capex flows directly to ASML as the effectively sole supplier of EUV lithography, and through to the broader capital-equipment complex.

**Memory is the tightest link in the chain.** Memory prices surged through 2025 and are expected to rise further in 2026 as AI demand for HBM and high-capacity NAND compounds. Memory has genuinely re-rated from commodity to scarce input — the pricing power now sits with the supplier rather than the buyer, which is a structural change in how that segment should be valued.

**The discipline point:** the semiconductor fundamental picture is excellent and the sector's multiples reflect it. In a week where the 30-year Treasury printed 2007 highs, paying 47x for the leader is a rate bet as much as a growth bet. Nothing here needs to be sold; nothing here is cheap.

Light Materials **-9% to $881.4M** on weaker gypsum wallboard volume and pricing. Earnings declined at the consolidated level, which is why the multiple is where it is — and which is what creates the entry. Capacity projects at Laramie, Wyoming (cement) and Duke, Oklahoma (wallboard) are on track, with full expensing tax benefits.

**Competitive Moat:** Cement is the best moat in materials and it is pure geography — the product is too heavy relative to its value to ship far, so each plant holds a genuine regional franchise inside its freight radius, and permitting new capacity in the U.S. is a decade-long undertaking. That is why announced price increases stick. Aggregates carry the same logic. This is a business protected by the laws of physics and the zoning process rather than by patents.

**Key Risk:** Housing. Wallboard is levered to residential construction, and with mortgage rates pressured by a 4.75-4.80% ten-year, the weak half could get weaker faster than the cement half strengthens. If the bond rout extends toward 5%, the wallboard drag extends with it — and consolidated earnings could decline for longer than the 15.7x multiple currently anticipates.

**Rating: Buy**

Secondary risks: at 3.8x net debt/EBITDA this is a leveraged entity, so a further leg higher in long rates raises refinancing cost directly, and 2.5% near-term EBITDA growth leaves little cushion if a major project slips.

**Rating: Accumulate**

## Risk Factors

**Ranked by severity for the coming week.**

**1. The long-end bond rout extends toward 5% — SEVERE**
The 30-year is at its highest since 2007, the 10-year at a 19-month high, and long-end yields in Japan and Germany are at multi-decade highs simultaneously. The Treasury already tried to intervene this week and the effect lasted 48 hours before fully unwinding — the most important tell of the week. A 5% ten-year is now openly discussed as the level that would force official attention. This is the top risk because it is not a shock but a process: it compresses every equity multiple, raises mortgage rates into the housing complex, lifts refinancing costs for leveraged issuers (KMI at 3.8x among them), and it does so regardless of how earnings come in.

**2. Iran sanctions escalation and Hormuz — SEVERE**
The sanctions package is detailed **Monday, August 26**. Brent is already at $93.96 having traded to $105 in July, Hormuz traffic is stagnating, and roughly 20% of global oil supply transits the strait. The asymmetry is what makes this dangerous: sanctions that succeed in removing Iranian barrels raise crude, and an Iran with no export revenue has less to lose from disrupting the strait. A move to $105+ would simultaneously worsen the inflation print, harden the FOMC hawks, deepen the bond rout, and squeeze the consumer — the only single event on the calendar that can hit all four channels at once.

**3. A hawkish Fed surprise, with Jackson Hole as the venue — HIGH**
Three FOMC members already dissented in favor of a hike in July, markets price ~30% odds of a hike by year end, and the Fed's own inflation forecast sits at 2.7%. **Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first Jackson Hole keynote Friday, August 28** — a new chair's inaugural framework speech is inherently high-variance, and it follows Wednesday's PCE print. Any signal that the committee is willing to hike into energy-driven inflation would reprice the front end and hit the small-cap and levered-industrial complex hardest, since the entire small-cap rally was built on rate-cut optimism that has already been withdrawn once.

**4. Consumer deterioration broadening from staples into discretionary — HIGH**
Walmart posted its slowest U.S. comp growth in six years (2.6% vs. 3.7% expected) and is cutting prices to defend share, with commentary tying weakness directly to elevated gasoline prices. When the world's most efficient retailer chooses margin sacrifice over volume loss, that is a demand signal rather than an execution problem. Ross Stores' +10% traffic-driven comps confirm the same thesis from the trade-down side. Watch **Tuesday's Consumer Confidence** and **Wednesday's Personal Income & Spending** for confirmation — this risk is what turns a rate-driven multiple compression into an earnings-driven one.

**5. Complacent equity volatility into a dense calendar — MODERATE but mispriced**
VIX at 16.01 is inconsistent with a 2007-high 30-year, a war premium in crude, a sanctions announcement Monday, PCE Wednesday, and a new Fed chair's debut keynote Friday. The risk is not that 16 is wrong about the world; it is that protection is unusually cheap relative to a calendar this concentrated, and that a market positioned for calm has further to travel when it is surprised.

**6. Hyperscaler capex digestion — MODERATE, but directly relevant to today's picks**
Consensus 2026 hyperscaler capex has doubled since June to $733B. The historical base rate says estimates have been too *low* two years running, so this is not a near-term call. But it is the shared dependency behind WCC's data center segment and KMI's gas backlog, and any hyperscaler signaling a pause would hit the entire physical-layer AI trade at once — including the parts of it trading at industrial multiples.

## Bottom Line

The bond market, not the AI trade, is setting prices right now, and it is telling you to own short-duration cash flow: a 30-year Treasury at 2007 highs and a failed Treasury intervention are not a dip to buy in long-duration growth. Today's basket buys the same AI and energy secular stories through cement, switchgear distribution, pipelines, government engineering and Delaware Basin barrels at 9.7x to 22x — because the physical layer of the AI buildout is compounding at 45-70% while trading at industrial multiples, and because at $94 Brent you want to own the energy shock rather than pay for it.

Respect next week's calendar. Sanctions detail Monday, PCE Wednesday, and a new Fed chair's first Jackson Hole keynote Friday is a lot of variance to carry into a VIX of 16 — scale into positions rather than chasing them, and note that Walmart just told us the consumer is already paying for the oil price.
