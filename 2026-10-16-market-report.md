## Geopolitical Factors

**The Iran conflict is now the dominant inflation input.** This has moved well beyond a risk premium into measurable physical supply disruption:

- **Strait of Hormuz:** roughly **nine tankers attacked** in and around Hormuz over the past week. Iran has threatened further escalation in response to the **U.S. blockade of its ports**.
- **Saudi infrastructure:** Houthi attacks on Saudi oil facilities and regional shipping continue to add to the premium.
- **Partial offset:** the IEA reports Middle East Gulf crude exports **rose 4.4 million bpd in September**, to roughly **75% of prewar levels** — real supply recovery, and the main reason Brent sits near $105 rather than well above it.
- **Diesel:** the IEA expects the war-driven **global diesel shortage to persist**, with inventories unlikely to recover until **the second half of 2027**. Diesel feeds freight, agriculture and construction costs — this is the channel through which the conflict becomes core, sticky inflation rather than a transitory energy spike.
- **Policy response:** the U.S. is weighing an easing of sanctions on "shadow fleet" vessels to suppress prices — a notable pragmatic reversal, and a bearish crude catalyst if enacted.

Analysts are genuinely split on whether crude is overextended, with one economist calling current levels "somewhat lofty compared to market realities" while others argue the Hormuz risk is under-priced. **The honest position is that this is unforecastable**, which is itself the argument for owning businesses whose economics do not depend on the outcome — a principle that shapes today's pick slate.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Enterprise Software & SaaS

*(Rotation math: day-of-year 289; 289 mod 6 = 1 → Enterprise software & SaaS.)*

**The setup: a sector that was left for dead, then quietly re-rated, and is now being bought outright.**

To understand why this is the most interesting bucket in the rotation right now, you have to hold three facts simultaneously.

**Fact one — the de-rating was real and brutal.** The 2026 "SaaSpocalypse" took the software index down **more than 26% from late January to its April low**. The thesis was specific and intellectually serious: if enterprises deploy AI agents that reduce headcount, then **per-seat pricing collapses**, and with it the recurring revenue that justified the multiples. Software price-to-sales ratios compressed from roughly **9x to 6x** — levels last seen in the mid-2010s.

**Fact two — the panic overshot, and the market has partially admitted it.** By **October 6**, U.S. software stocks had reached **fresh 2026 highs** as earnings expectations rose and AI-disruption fears faded. JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs analysts argued the selloff had gone too far. Valoir's Rebecca Wettemann put it plainly: the SaaSpocalypse "didn't happen anywhere near as fast as some of the people on Wall Street thought it would." Note the careful wording — *not that it won't happen*, but that the timeline was wrong. That distinction is the entire investment question.

**Fact three — acquirers are voting with balance sheets, and they are taking out the mid-cap tier wholesale.** This is the part the market is under-weighting. Over the course of 2026, the following names have been removed or agreed to be removed from the public mid-cap software universe:

**The correction I have to make to the obvious narrative.** It would be easy — and wrong — to call this a record software M&A boom. **It is not.** PitchBook data shows U.S. PE software deal value at roughly **$16.24 billion over the first five months of 2026**, pacing toward about **a quarter of 2025's record $156 billion**. Platform buyouts fell to a **decade-low share** of PE software deal value. Only **seven software platform deals cleared $100 million** by late June. CBH puts Q2 software deal value down roughly **66% year over year**; EY has tech-focused PE deal value down about **50%** while non-tech rose.

So the correct reading is narrower and more useful: **deal *volume* collapsed, but the deals that are happening are concentrated in high-quality mid-caps and are being struck at de-rated prices.** Take-privates cleared at a median of about **11.6x EBITDA**, *below* the ~13.9x paid in large corporate deals. Jamf went at **3.3x ARR** — a humiliating multiple for a category-leading franchise with 40% security ARR growth. Acquirers are not paying up in a frenzy; they are picking off good businesses cheaply because the public market de-rated the whole group indiscriminately.

**That is the actual opportunity, and it is a valuation opportunity, not a momentum one.**

**The analytical key: not all SaaS revenue is equally exposed to AI.** The per-seat disruption thesis is directionally sound but applies very unevenly, and the market has largely failed to discriminate. Revenue sitting on these bases is structurally more defensible:

1. **Consumption / transaction / asset-based pricing** — revenue scales with activity or assets, not headcount. AI agents that *increase* transaction volume are accretive, not dilutive.
2. **Embedded payments and fintech attach** — monetizes dollars flowing through the platform, entirely decoupled from seat count.
3. **System-of-record with regulatory or audit weight** — switching costs are legal and procedural, not merely technical.
4. **Identity, governance and security** — AI agents *multiply* the number of entities requiring credentials and oversight. This is the clearest case where AI is a direct structural tailwind rather than a threat.

Conversely, the genuinely exposed cohort is **pure per-seat knowledge-worker software with weak data moats** — and the tape is already discriminating here. Today, **KeyBanc downgraded Workday (WDAY) to Sector Weight** and **Goldman Sachs cut Klaviyo (KVYO) to Neutral with a $20 target**. Both are seat-and-contact-based models. That is not a coincidence, and it is a warning to anyone treating "software is cheap" as an undifferentiated call.

**One more live signal worth flagging:** BlackLine management disclosed that several large deals **slipped past quarter-end because AI-related customer review has lengthened enterprise sales cycles**. This is the most honest description of the near-term risk I have seen from a management team. AI is not yet destroying software revenue — it is *delaying* it, as buyers pause to ask whether they still need the seat count they are renewing. That delay compresses near-term growth without necessarily impairing terminal value, which is exactly the condition that creates mispricing.

### Artificial Intelligence

**The AI trade broke this week on its balance sheet, not its income statement.** This distinction matters enormously, and most commentary is getting it wrong.

The fundamentals are, if anything, accelerating. TSMC reported fiscal Q3 revenue **up 51% to NT$1.49 trillion**, beating forecasts, and guided Q4 to as much as **+61% year over year**. ASML warned it **may not be able to fully meet demand in 2027** — a supply constraint, not a demand problem. These are not the numbers of a bursting bubble.

What broke was the **financing structure**:

- **CoreWeave** fell **15.5%** on October 14 and a further **4.1% premarket today**. Its **bonds fell below 80% of face value** — conventionally the threshold for distressed debt — with some yields approaching **15%**.
- **Nvidia's own 10-Q** discloses guarantees covering SB Energy leases for an Ohio campus hosting its compute for OpenAI, with exposure **capped at $105 billion** across roughly **4.25 gigawatts** of IT load under 20-year leases. The obligation amortizes as OpenAI makes lease payments. A chip vendor underwriting its customer's real estate is **vendor financing**, and vendor financing at this scale is a structural feature that deserves far more scrutiny than it receives.
- **Broadcom** has reportedly arranged **more than $50 billion** for OpenAI's custom chips, with a separate **$60 billion** chip financing package benefiting Anthropic among others.
- **Morgan Stanley** estimates AI infrastructure requires **$1.5 trillion of external financing through 2028** — while noting **September was the second-lowest issuance month of the year**. Required financing is rising as available financing falls. That gap is the whole story.
- **Credit quality:** AIR Platforms, a small AI-driven ratings startup, published unsolicited ratings of **BB+ for OpenAI** (below investment grade) and **BBB for Anthropic**. Forbes separately reports that bankers are lobbying S&P and Moody's for investment-grade treatment of AI borrowers. *(Treat an unsolicited rating from a startup as a data point, not an authority.)*
- **The arithmetic problem:** the Financial Times estimates data center owners would need **$8.5 trillion of revenue by end-2030** to cover costs and earn reasonable returns, against consumer and enterprise AI spending expected to reach **at most $3.6 trillion**. Even allowing for wide error bars, a gap of that magnitude does not close quietly.
- **Sentiment markers:** OpenAI has **postponed its IPO to next year**, and Anthropic is reported to show **slower quarterly revenue growth**.

**My read:** this is a **cost-of-capital repricing, not a demand repricing**. The buildout's economics were underwritten at a discount rate that no longer exists — with the 10-year above 5% and the Fed possibly hiking, every one of these 20-year lease structures is worth materially less than at signing. Demand for compute remains real; the *capital structure* financing it was built for a 3% world. That is why TSMC can print +51% and still fall. Investors should separate **companies selling into the buildout with pricing power and clean balance sheets** from **companies whose equity is a levered call option on the buildout being financed on schedule.**

### 1. BSY — Bentley Systems *(Infrastructure engineering software — AEC/ owner-operator)*

- **Thesis:** Bentley is the system of record for the design, construction and operation of physical infrastructure — roads, rail, water, grid — and its revenue is tied to **global infrastructure capital spending**, not to enterprise headcount. In a world where AI agents compress white-collar seat counts, revenue indexed to bridges and transmission lines is a different and better asset. The stock has drifted while ARR compounds steadily.
- **Valuation metrics:** Price ≈ **$35.06** (Oct 13; $34.75 on Oct 5). Market cap ≈ **$10–11B**. **Trailing P/E ≈ 40.1**; **forward P/E ≈ 25.0** per StockAnalysis. *Conflicting sources:* Trefis showed trailing 32.0 / forward 19.5 against a June 25 price of $28.88; Macrotrends showed 30.69 on June 12. The forward multiple is therefore somewhere in the **20–25x** band depending on date and estimate basis. **P/S ≈ 6.6x** on $1.60B TTM revenue. EV/EBITDA and PEG not reliably sourced — **I am not going to fabricate them.**
- **Growth profile:** Q1 2026 revenue **$424.2M, +14.5%** (+11.9% constant currency); Q2 2026 revenue **$410.73M, +12.8%**; TTM revenue **$1.60B, +12.85%**. **ARR $1,494.5M.** Trefis models FY2026 revenue ≈$1.7B (+9.3%) and FY2027 ≈$1.9B (+10.4%) — note this implies *deceleration*, which is the main bear argument.
- **Competitive moat:** Infrastructure projects run on 30-to-50-year asset lifecycles, and Bentley's models become the as-built digital record. Switching means abandoning the design history of assets that must be maintained for decades. Engineering-firm training and credentialing deepen the lock-in. This is a genuine data moat, not a UI moat.
- **Key risk:** **Government budget dependence.** With deficits under pressure and rates above 5%, public infrastructure funding is squeezed precisely when borrowing costs rise. A municipal capex pullback hits Bentley with a lag. Secondarily, the Trefis deceleration path (to ~9–10% growth) would not support a 25x forward multiple.
- **Rating:** **Accumulate** — scale in; the multiple is fair rather than cheap, and the deceleration question is unresolved.

### 2. APPF — AppFolio *(Vertical SaaS — real estate operations with embedded payments)*

- **Thesis:** The cleanest expression of the "revenue that isn't a seat" principle on this slate. AppFolio monetizes **units under management and payment volume flowing through the platform**, so an AI agent that lets a property manager handle more units with fewer staff **increases** AppFolio's revenue base while reducing its customer's costs. The AI-disruption fear is close to inverted here, and the stock is priced as though it isn't.
- **Valuation metrics:** Price **$197.45** (Oct 8). Market cap ≈ **$6.99B**. **Trailing P/E 44.89; forward P/E 25.43** per StockAnalysis. *Conflicting source:* GuruFocus put trailing P/E near **39.04x** on a $5.8B cap post-Q2 — the gap reflects different dates and earnings bases. **P/S ≈ 6.6x** on >$1B TTM revenue. PEG ≈ **1.4** on the forward multiple against ~18% growth *(my calculation, not a sourced figure)*.
- **Growth profile:** Q2 2026 revenue **$281M, +19%**; Q1 2026 **$262M, +20%**; FY2025 **$950.82M, +19.72%**. TTM revenue crossed **$1B for the first time**. FY2026 guidance **raised** to **$1.117–1.127B (≈18%)**. Profitability is inflecting hard: Q2 GAAP operating income **+31% to $53M (18.8% margin)**; non-GAAP operating income **+76M, +24% (27.1% margin)**.
- **Competitive moat:** Property managers run their entire operation — leasing, maintenance, accounting, tenant screening, payments — inside AppFolio. The embedded payments attach makes it a financial rail, not just software, and ripping it out means re-plumbing rent collection for thousands of tenants mid-lease. Very high practical switching cost.
- **Key risk:** **Units under management grew only 8%** against 19% revenue growth, meaning the majority of growth is **price and attach-rate expansion, not customer expansion**. That is a finite lever. If unit growth stays at 8% and attach saturates, revenue growth converges toward single digits and a 25x forward multiple does not survive. This is the single most important number to watch, and it is the reason this isn't a Strong Buy.
- **Rating:** **Buy**

### 3. SAIL — SailPoint *(Identity security & governance)*

- **Thesis:** The one name on this slate where **AI is a direct structural tailwind rather than a headwind to be survived**. Every autonomous AI agent deployed in an enterprise is a **non-human identity** requiring provisioning, entitlement, least-privilege enforcement and audit. Agent proliferation multiplies SailPoint's addressable unit count. Management is already monetizing it: **AI-driven ARR has passed $70M and represents over 30% of net new ARR.**
- **Valuation metrics:** Price ≈ **$19.30** (Oct 14, MarketBeat). *Conflicting source:* Morningstar showed **$20.77** on Oct 9. Analyst consensus target **$22.20**. Market cap ≈ **$11.8B**, implying roughly **9.6x ARR** and **~10x TTM revenue** *(my calculation)*. This is the richest multiple on the slate and the pick most dependent on growth delivering.
- **Growth profile:** Fiscal Q2 2027 **ARR $1.231B, +25%**; **SaaS ARR $847M, +36%**; revenue **$309M, +17%** (reported revenue lags ARR because the higher SaaS mix defers recognition — a real accounting headwind, not a demand problem). - **Key risk:** **The guidance credibility gap.** The stock **fell 16.4% in March 2026** despite ARR crossing $1B, because guidance disappointed. - **Competitive moat:** BlackLine automates the financial close and account reconciliation — a **SOX-audited, auditor-blessed control environment**. Replacing it means re-certifying internal controls, which CFOs avoid absent a compelling reason. The switching cost is regulatory, not technical, which is the most durable kind.
- **Key risk:** **Growth is genuinely decelerating, and the AI question is open.** ARR at +6% is weak, and management explicitly disclosed that **large deals slipped past quarter-end because AI-related customer review lengthened sales cycles**. The bear case is coherent: if AI agents can perform reconciliation natively inside the ERP, BlackLine's core function gets absorbed by SAP or Oracle. I do not think that happens quickly — the audit-trail and controls layer is the hard part, not the matching logic — but **this is the pick most likely to be wrong**, and the cheap multiple is compensation for exactly that risk, not a free lunch. Next earnings: **early November 2026.**
- **Rating:** **Accumulate** — a value position sized for the possibility that the bears are right about terminal value.

### 5. ZM — Zoom Communications *(Unified communications & collaboration)*

- **Thesis:** A **fortress balance sheet** trading at a single-digit multiple of enterprise free cash flow. With roughly **$7.19B of net cash** against a ~$27B market cap, the operating business is available at approximately **$20B for ~$1.9B of annual free cash flow — about 10.5x EV/FCF** *(my calculation)*. The market is pricing a terminal decline that the cash flows are not showing; meanwhile management is shrinking the share count aggressively.
- **Valuation metrics:** Price ≈ **$90–97** depending on source and date. Cash **$7.25B** against debt of just **$60.27M** → **net cash $7.19B**. TTM FCF **≈$1.92B (+4.4%)**. **P/FCF ≈ 15x; FCF yield ≈ 7.35%**; **EV/FCF ≈ 10.5x** adjusting for net cash. Trefis notes FCF equal to **6.7% of market value** versus a 4.4% S&P median. *Conflicting views:* GuruFocus rates it **modestly overvalued** at a GF Value of $80.64, while a Simply Wall St DCF suggests ~**32% undervalued** — a spread wide enough that both should be discounted as model outputs rather than evidence.
- **Growth profile:** This is the weak point and I will not dress it up. Revenue grew **~4.9% in Q2 FY27**. The 5-year FCF CAGR is only **~2.9%** (3-year ~19%, flattered by the pandemic base). Q2 FY27 FCF of **$472M** funded aggressive buybacks. **This is a cash-return story, not a growth story** — the thesis requires only that revenue stay roughly flat, not that it accelerate.
- **Competitive moat:** Honestly, the moat is the **weakest on this slate** — Microsoft Teams is bundled into an enterprise agreement most Zoom customers already pay for. What Zoom has instead is brand-level verb status, a genuinely superior core product experience, an installed base that persists on preference, and **a balance sheet that gives it a decade of runway to find a second act**. I am underwriting the cash and the optionality, not a durable competitive fortress.
- **Key risk:** **Terminal value.** If Teams bundling grinds Zoom's revenue into sustained decline, a 10x EV/FCF multiple on a shrinking base is a value trap, and buybacks merely accelerate the march. Margin compression was already flagged as a concern in recent results. The offset is that at this valuation, a *flat* business is sufficient for an attractive return — but flat is not guaranteed.
- **Rating:** **Accumulate**
- **Required disclosure:** one opinion source argues the stock is a Strong Buy partly on the basis of a reported **$3.13B stake in Anthropic**. Because I am an AI assistant made by Anthropic, **I have deliberately excluded that stake from this thesis entirely** — every figure above stands on Zoom's own cash flows and balance sheet. Readers who find the stake credible should treat it as upside optionality I have not counted; I note it only so that its omission is transparent rather than silent.

## Risk Factors

**1. A Federal Reserve hike on October 28 — Severity: High.** Markets assign roughly 35% probability and are positioned for a hold. Core PPI at +0.7% MoM against +0.3% expected, and headline PPI at 6.3% against 5.4%, are not consistent with a Fed that is finished. A hike would hit long-duration equities hardest — including every name on today's slate — and would arrive six days before midterm elections, compounding the move with political volatility. **This is the risk most asymmetrically mispriced right now.**

**2. AI financing seizure — Severity: High.** The gap between the $1.5 trillion of external financing Morgan Stanley estimates is required through 2028 and September's second-lowest issuance month of the year is the market's central structural problem. CoreWeave bonds below 80 cents with yields near 15% is a live distress signal, not a sentiment indicator. If AI capex gets financed at 15% instead of 5%, a large share of announced buildout plans are not economic, and the revenue forecasts embedded across semis, power, networking and data centers deflate together. Nvidia's $105B lease guarantee means the credit risk does not stay contained within the borrowers.

**3. Oil as a persistent inflation engine — Severity: High.** With Brent near $105, nine tankers attacked near Hormuz in a week, a U.S. blockade of Iranian ports, and an IEA diesel shortage projected to persist into **H2 2027**, energy is no longer a transitory CPI line item. It is feeding the core through freight and production costs. Critically, **this is the inflation the Fed cannot fix with rates** — which raises the risk of over-tightening into a supply shock, the classic policy error.

**4. Enterprise AI timing disappointment — Severity: Medium-High.** The thesis behind Barclays' simultaneous downgrade of Dell, HPE and NetApp, and the same force lengthening BlackLine's sales cycles. If enterprises keep *deferring* AI commitments while vendors keep building capacity, the revenue arrives late across both hardware and software. This risk is unusual in that it hits the AI buildout and the software incumbents **at the same time, from the same cause** — the usual hedge of owning one against the other does not work here.

**5. Credit contagion from AI into broad risk assets — Severity: Medium.** Thursday's 12.8bp rally in the 10-year was explicitly attributed to a flight from AI-related corporate credit into sovereigns. That is the mechanism by which a sector problem becomes a market problem. Watch the data center bond complex and CoreWeave spreads as the leading indicator, not equity prices — **credit is where this will show up first, and it already has.**

---

## Bottom Line

The defining feature of this market is that **strong fundamentals are no longer sufficient**, because the cost of capital is repricing faster than earnings can compound — TSMC grew revenue 51%, guided to +61%, and its stock fell anyway. In that regime, the highest-quality setup is a business with **durable cash flows, a clean balance sheet, and revenue indexed to something other than the enterprise headcount AI is about to compress** — which is precisely the screen that produced today's slate. Enterprise software offers this at a discount because the market de-rated the entire group on a disruption thesis that is real in direction but badly wrong in timing and completely indiscriminate in application; the acquirers taking out Varonis, Clearwater, Confluent, DoubleVerify and Jamf at de-rated multiples are the ones currently being paid for that distinction.

**Positioning:** accumulate deliberately rather than chase, keep dry powder for the October 28 FOMC, and watch **credit spreads in the data center complex** — not equity indices — as the signal that tells you whether this is a rotation or the first leg of something larger.
