# Daily Market Report — Friday, August 7, 2026

## Executive Summary

Equities closed out their best week since April on a session defined by a genuinely strange divergence: the July employment report was outright bad — payrolls *fell* 23,000 against consensus of roughly 83,000–95,000, with 103,000 of downward revisions to May and June — and stocks rallied anyway. The S&P 500 added 0.33%, the Nasdaq Composite gained 0.86%, and the Dow eked out 0.09%, while the Russell 2000 slipped 0.58% in a clear rejection of the "bad news is good news" trade at the small-cap level. The proximate driver was rate-cut repricing layered on top of continued optimism around a negotiated reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, which has kept a bid under risk assets all week and a lid on crude.

The tell worth watching is the internal split. Mega-cap tech and long-duration growth took the payroll miss as a discount-rate gift; small caps, which are the most levered to actual domestic demand, read the same number as a demand warning and sold off. Both cannot be right. Today's report leans into the rotating spotlight — **fintech, payments and financial data** — precisely because that group sits at the intersection of the two readings: payment volumes are a real-time proxy for the consumer and B2B spend that the jobs data just called into question, and the sector has been de-rated hard enough that several names now discount a recession that hasn't shown up in their volumes.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The July employment report — the day's dominant input

This was a materially weak report, and the weakness was broad rather than concentrated in one quirk:

- **Headline payrolls: −23,000**, versus consensus expectations in the 83,000–95,000 range. This is the first outright monthly decline in some time.
- **Government employment: −53,000**, with local government education alone shedding 50,000 positions.
- **Retail trade: −19,000. Leisure and hospitality: −40,000.** Healthcare grew, but more slowly than its recent run rate — notable because healthcare has been carrying a disproportionate share of job creation for over a year.
- **Revisions: −103,000 combined.** May was revised down 66,000 to a gain of 63,000; June was revised down 37,000 to a gain of just 20,000. The three-month trend is now close to stall speed.
- **Unemployment rate: 4.1%**, *lower* — but for the wrong reason. The **labor force participation rate fell to 61.4%**, a level not seen in more than five years. The rate fell because people left the labor force, not because they found work.
- **Average hourly earnings: +3.2% year over year**, the softest reading since May 2021.

The wage number is the one that matters most for policy. A 3.2% wage print is broadly consistent with 2% inflation given trend productivity — it removes the labor-cost channel as an inflation argument almost entirely. That is what let the front end rally and equities rip despite an ugly headline.

### The Fed's problem

The target range currently sits at **3.50%–3.75%**, and the Fed has not cut at all in 2026. Prior to today, market pricing had drifted genuinely hawkish — from expecting cuts, toward pricing some probability of *hikes* by year-end, with a meaningful 30–40% chance of no cut at all in 2026 and a central case of just one more 25bp cut to 3.25%–3.50%.

Today's report puts real pressure on that framing. But the Fed is boxed in by the other half of its mandate: inflation remains elevated relative to the 2% goal, and the reason is largely **energy and supply shocks tied to Middle East tensions** — precisely the kind of supply-side inflation that monetary policy handles worst. Cutting into an oil-driven price shock risks un-anchoring expectations; not cutting into a contracting labor market risks the recession the Fed spent two years trying to avoid.

Complicating matters further, new Chair **Kevin Warsh** has established task forces to evaluate Fed operations and the policy framework, with findings expected later in the year. The plausible institutional path is a Fed that stays on pause until that review concludes — which means today's data may not translate into policy action as quickly as the equity rally implies.

**What to watch next week:** CPI is the single most important release. If core comes in soft, the September cut becomes close to fully priced and today's rally extends. If energy pass-through pushes core higher while the labor market deteriorates, the market has to confront genuine stagflation pricing for the first time this cycle — and that is an environment where neither the growth-multiple trade nor the small-cap trade works.

## Geopolitical Factors

### The Strait of Hormuz remains the defining macro variable of 2026

The conflict-related disruption in the Strait triggered what has been characterized as the largest oil market shock in history. The scale is worth restating plainly: roughly **21 million barrels per day** transit the chokepoint — nearly 20% of global petroleum consumption and about 30% of all seaborne traded oil. The market faced an estimated **3.7 mb/d deficit in Q2 2026**, and Brent traded to $105 at the peak of the March closure.

**The current state is negotiation, not resolution.** Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent signaled earlier this week that a deal with Iran was in sight, and that headline is doing enormous work in current market pricing — it drove Tuesday's record highs and has capped crude all week. Oil "wavered" today as traders weighed the odds.

Consensus scenarios for 2026 Brent now sit in a **$95–$115/bbl** range, roughly 10–35% above the prior baseline. The bear-case-for-markets scenario is not a re-closure but a **prolonged stalemate**: intermittent military escorts, sporadic exchanges, and a sustained risk premium holding oil above $100 for an extended period. That scenario keeps headline inflation sticky, keeps the Fed frozen, and slowly grinds down consumer discretionary spending — a much worse outcome for equities than a sharp, resolved shock.

**The under-priced risk here is asymmetry of positioning.** The market has rallied to record highs partly *on* deal optimism. A deal that gets signed is substantially priced in; a deal that collapses is not. That is a poor risk/reward setup for adding beta into the headline.

### Second-order effects worth tracking

- **Travel and cross-border commerce disruption.** Middle East travel disruption is showing up directly in company results — Shift4 flagged a ~$20 million Q2 revenue drag from it. This is a real, measurable earnings channel into the payments complex, not an abstraction.
- **Shipping asset dislocation.** Vessels repositioned around the chokepoint have not fully normalized, keeping freight and insurance costs elevated across unrelated trade lanes.
- **Trade policy.** WTO-related delays continue to reshape cross-border payment flows, adding friction that benefits infrastructure providers with established multi-corridor licensing while hurting single-corridor operators.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Fintech, Payments & Financial Data

*(Rotation: day-of-year 219, 219 mod 6 = 3 → Fintech, payments & financial data)*

This is the most interesting sector in the market right now, and the reason is a valuation dislocation that has persisted long enough to become an opportunity rather than a trap.

#### The setup: a sector priced for a recession that its own data doesn't show

Payments companies are, structurally, a levered bet on nominal GDP with an embedded software margin. Over the past eighteen months the group has been de-rated on three distinct fears: (1) consumer spending rolls over, (2) stablecoins disintermediate the incumbent rails, and (3) AI compresses the value of the software layer sitting on top of the rails. All three are legitimate. None of them is currently visible in the volume data.

The volume data says the opposite. **Corpay's Corporate Payments spend volume surged 67% to $81.43 billion**, with segment revenue up 42%. **Shift4 is guiding to 26–31% growth** in gross revenue less network fees. **Euronet's digital revenue grew 31% year over year** and now contributes 26% of total revenue, with digital accelerators up 35% year to date — ahead of the framework management laid out at its Investor Day. **Remitly crossed 10 million quarterly active customers for the first time** and beat its own EBITDA guidance by 30%.

Meanwhile the multiples have compressed to levels that assume none of this continues. This gap between fundamental trajectory and multiple is the entire thesis for today's spotlight.

#### Structural driver 1: B2B is where the actual growth is

Consumer payments is a mature, share-shift business. **Corporate/B2B payments is not** — it is still substantially paper-and-check, and the digitization runway is measured in decades rather than quarters. Corpay's 67% corporate spend volume growth against 10% consolidated organic growth is the clearest illustration in the sector of where incremental dollars are actually going.

The important nuance for investors: B2B payment relationships are far stickier than consumer ones. They involve ERP integration, multi-year contracting, treasury workflow embedding, and compliance infrastructure. The switching cost is not the payment rail — it is the accounts-payable process rebuild. This is why B2B payments deserves a higher multiple than consumer acquiring, and why the market's tendency to lump them together creates the mispricing.

#### Structural driver 2: stablecoins — threat and opportunity, and the market has the sign wrong

The **GENIUS Act**, signed July 18, 2025, established the federal framework for USD-backed payment stablecoins — issuance, reserve backing, redemption, supervision, and AML obligations. The OCC issued its implementing proposed rulemaking in February 2026. Stablecoin transaction volumes reportedly hit **$33 trillion in 2025**, and forecasts have stablecoins representing roughly **3% of all US dollar payments in 2026, rising toward 10% by 2031**.

The reflexive market read is that this is terminal for cross-border payment incumbents. That read is too simple, for three reasons:

1. **Settlement is not the expensive part.** For most cross-border flows, the cost sits in compliance, licensing, FX spread management, last-mile cash-out, and customer acquisition — not in the movement of value between two ledgers. Stablecoins compress the cheapest link in the chain.
2. **Regulated distribution is the scarce asset.** A stablecoin still has to become spendable local currency in the recipient's hands. The companies with licenses, banking relationships, and physical/digital cash-out networks in dozens of jurisdictions are the ones that capture that. Euronet's REN infrastructure and Remitly's corridor-level disbursement networks are exactly this kind of asset.
3. **Incumbents are adopting, not resisting.** Shift4 has been building a stablecoin platform. Large banks are moving into wholesale settlement and tokenized deposits. The pressure the GENIUS Act creates on traditional finance is a pressure to *integrate*, and integration favors whoever already owns the customer relationship.

The honest counter-argument, which I do not dismiss: for pure P2P remittance corridors with high-volume, price-sensitive, crypto-literate users, stablecoins genuinely do compress take rates. That risk is real and specific, and it is why Remitly carries the lowest conviction rating in today's picks despite the best growth profile.

#### Structural driver 3: financial data and electronic trading — the quiet compounder

The exchanges-and-data complex delivered a solid Q1 2026: **MarketAxess** revenue of $233.4 million, +11.9% year over year and ahead of consensus; **Morningstar** revenue of $663.2 million, +9.6%, beating on both EBITDA and EPS; **Tradeweb** revenue of $558.9 million, +9% and in line.

The appeal here is mix. These businesses earn a blend of transaction fees (volatile, but positively correlated to exactly the market stress that hurts everything else in a portfolio) and data subscriptions (recurring, high-margin, price-escalating). In a genuinely uncertain macro environment, a business that earns *more* when volatility rises is a structurally valuable portfolio component.

The specific caution — and it is a real one — is **MarketAxess's credit market share**. Growth outside US credit ran around 20% on record emerging-market and Eurobond volumes, which is excellent. But the core US credit franchise is showing signs of share stagnation as competing electronic protocols mature. This is the single most important variable for the name, and it is why the rating below is Accumulate rather than Buy.

#### Structural driver 4: core banking software — the most defensive corner

Core processing for regional and community banks is close to a utility. Contracts run five to seven years, switching costs are severe (a core conversion is a multi-year, career-risking project for a bank CIO), and revenue is largely recurring.

**Jack Henry** is the cleanest expression. FY2026 guidance calls for revenue of $2,521–$2,533 million, operating margin of 24.7–24.9%, and EPS of $6.78–$6.87, with a trailing-twelve-month ROIC of 22.2% and an expanded buyback. The signing of **Woodforest National Bank** — a multi-state institution with over $9 billion in assets, described as Jack Henry's largest new core signing to date — matters more than its immediate revenue contribution, because it demonstrates the cloud-native platform can win upmarket. That is the bear case (that Jack Henry is trapped serving shrinking community banks) directly refuted.

The structural headwind is genuine: bank consolidation shrinks the customer count every year. The offset is that deconversion fees (raised to $37 million for FY26) partially monetize the very consolidation that reduces the client base, and surviving banks are larger and buy more modules.

#### What the jobs report means for this sector specifically

A weakening labor market is a direct negative for consumer payment volumes and for credit-exposed fintech. It is a much smaller negative for B2B payments (corporate spend lags employment), close to neutral for core banking software (contractual), and arguably a *positive* for exchange and data businesses (uncertainty drives trading volume).

This is why today's picks are deliberately weighted toward B2B, infrastructure, and data rather than consumer credit or discretionary-spend-levered acquirers. If the labor market is genuinely rolling over, that positioning is where the sector's defensiveness actually lives.

### Artificial Intelligence

The dominant AI story remains the financing structure of the buildout rather than the technology itself.

**The Nvidia–OpenAI backstop.** Nvidia and OpenAI have been in discussions over a backstop of **up to $250 billion** that would let OpenAI raise debt against Nvidia's credit to fund a 10-gigawatt data center campus in Pike County, Ohio. When this was reported in late July, AI hardware names sold off hard — Nvidia fell 5%, AMD dropped 8%, Intel fell 4%, Dell fell 4%.

That market reaction was the correct instinct, and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed as noise. When a supplier finances its customer's purchase of the supplier's own product, revenue quality degrades. The accounting may be clean; the economics are circular. The relevant question for every AI infrastructure holding is what proportion of demand is funded by end-customer cash flow versus vendor-supported credit. That proportion has been rising.

**The capex numbers remain staggering.** Hyperscaler 2026 capital expenditure plans total roughly **$660–690 billion**: Amazon ~$200 billion, Alphabet $175–185 billion, Meta $115–135 billion, Microsoft $120 billion or more, Oracle ~$50 billion.

**And critically, the revenue is now showing up.** Q2 2026 settled the biggest open debate on Wall Street — hyperscalers are converting AI infrastructure spend into accelerating cloud revenue. **AWS reached $42.23 billion growing 37%**, the fastest pace in 18 quarters, at a 39.4% operating margin. **Google Cloud accelerated to 82% growth**, with nearly 90% of the Fortune 100 using Gemini.

This is the crux of the current AI debate. The capex is real, the cloud revenue acceleration is real, and the financing structures are getting more aggressive at the same time. Those facts are not contradictory — they describe a boom in its late-middle phase, where genuine demand and financial engineering coexist and become difficult to separate. Investors should own the demand and avoid paying for the engineering.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

Semiconductors posted their **strongest four-day rally since 2020** earlier this week, and the underlying data supports the enthusiasm even if the price action got ahead of itself.

**Industry volume.** Global semiconductor sales reached a record **$120.6 billion in May 2026, up 104.1% year over year** — the fifteenth consecutive monthly record. A doubling of industry-wide sales year over year is a genuinely extraordinary datapoint and reflects both unit growth and substantial ASP inflation from AI-driven mix shift.

**Foundry.** TSMC raised its 2026 capex budget by **$8 billion to $62 billion** at the midpoint and lifted its full-year USD revenue growth target to **over 40%**. Leading-edge utilization is near 100% for 3nm and 5nm, and **3nm capacity is fully allocated for the next 18–24 months**. TSMC has confirmed wafer price increases for 2027, with mature nodes (12nm, 16nm, 28nm) facing hikes of **up to 10%**.

That mature-node pricing detail is underappreciated. Raising prices on trailing-edge capacity — historically the commoditized, competitive part of the business — signals that the capacity crunch has propagated well beyond leading-edge AI accelerators into the analog, power management, and microcontroller content that goes into everything else. It is good for TSMC's margins and bad for the input costs of every fabless company and every industrial OEM.

**Memory.** HBM4 is in high-volume shipment for the lead AI accelerator platform. Micron delivered Q3 FY26 revenue of **$41.46 billion, up 345.7% year over year**, beating consensus of $35.25 billion by 17.6% — and the stock still closed at $874.66 on July 30, **down 24.21% over the prior month**. Morgan Stanley has Micron as its top semiconductor pick for 2026.

A stock falling 24% in a month after beating a consensus estimate by 17.6% on 345% revenue growth is the most important sentiment signal in the semiconductor complex right now. It says the market is no longer paying for reported results; it is paying for its own forecast of where the memory cycle peaks. When that dynamic sets in, the fundamental news flow stops working as a catalyst — and that condition typically persists for several months.

### Software & SaaS

The software sector is bifurcating along a clean line, and the aggregate numbers hide the divergence.

**Median 2026 revenue growth has risen to 13.2%**, which sounds healthy. Underneath: **DevOps, ITOps and developer/automation platforms are growing 21.9%**, while **CRM, sales, marketing, CX, collaboration, productivity and creative software have fallen into single digits**.

That is the whole story. Software that sits close to the AI infrastructure layer — orchestration, observability, developer tooling, automation — is accelerating. Software that sells seats to knowledge workers is decelerating, because the AI narrative cuts directly against per-seat pricing. If agents do work that headcount used to do, seat-based revenue models face a structural, not cyclical, headwind.

**AI monetization is nonetheless real at the leaders.** Salesforce's Agentforce ARR reached approximately **$800 million, up 169% year over year**, with the combined Agentforce and Data Cloud bundle crossing **$2.9 billion ARR, up over 200%**. ServiceNow closed **35 deals worth $1 million or more** for its Now Assist AI suite.

Enterprises are reportedly spending **15% more on software in 2026** because of AI. The catch is distribution: that incremental spend is concentrating in relatively few vendors, and it is partly funded by cutting spend on the long tail. The sector aggregate looks fine while the median vendor struggles.

**One caution flag:** IBM's warnings on AI capital expenditure have made cloud software buyers more cautious. When a large, diversified vendor signals hesitation about its own AI capex returns, it tends to precede a broader procurement slowdown by a quarter or two.

### 1. CPAY — Corpay, Inc.

**Thesis:** Corpay just posted an all-time record quarter — revenue $1.34 billion (+21%, $45 million ahead of expectations) and cash EPS of $7.00 (+36%) — and raised full-year adjusted EPS guidance, yet the stock finished the session down 0.55% at $394.34 and was flat after hours. ## Risk Factors

**1. The labor market may be genuinely rolling over — severity: high, and newly elevated as of today.**
A −23,000 payroll print with 103,000 of downward revisions and participation at a five-year low of 61.4% is not a single noisy month; it is a trend that has been revised into visibility. The three-month average is now near stall speed. Equity markets treated this as a rate-cut catalyst, which works only if the Fed cuts *and* the economy stabilizes. If the labor market deteriorates further while the Fed remains constrained by energy-driven inflation, current earnings estimates across cyclicals are too high. The Russell 2000's 0.58% decline against the S&P's 0.33% gain suggests parts of the market already believe this.

**2. Stagflation risk from a prolonged Hormuz stalemate — severity: high.**
The market has rallied to record highs partly on the expectation of a negotiated reopening. Brent scenarios of $95–$115 sit well above baseline, and the prolonged-stalemate case keeps crude above $100 indefinitely. Supply-driven inflation combined with a weakening labor market is the one macro configuration in which the Fed cannot help — and it is the configuration current pricing assigns the least probability to. Positioning is asymmetric: a deal is largely priced, a collapse is not.

**3. Circular AI financing degrading revenue quality — severity: high, and rising.**
A vendor backstopping up to $250 billion of its largest customer's debt to fund purchases of that vendor's product is a structure that works while asset values rise and unwinds badly if they do not. The market's initial reaction — Nvidia −5%, AMD −8% — was the correct instinct. The $660–690 billion hyperscaler capex figure is genuinely supported by accelerating cloud revenue (AWS +37%, Google Cloud +82%), so this is not a mirage. But the marginal dollar of AI infrastructure demand is increasingly credit-supported rather than cash-flow-funded, and that shift is not reflected in current multiples.

**4. "Good is not good enough" — a market-wide sentiment regime shift — severity: medium-high.**
SanDisk and Western Digital sold off on solid results. Micron fell 24% in a month after beating consensus by 17.6% on 345% revenue growth. When strong fundamental news stops producing positive price reaction, the market has moved from discounting results to discounting its own forecast of the cycle peak. That regime historically persists for months and makes long positions in cyclical technology dependent on multiple expansion that is unlikely to arrive.

**5. Seat-based software facing structural, not cyclical, pressure — severity: medium.**
The gap between DevOps/ITOps at 21.9% growth and CRM/collaboration/creative in single digits is not a temporary spending rotation. If AI agents perform work previously done by headcount, per-seat pricing models face permanent impairment. Aggregate software spending rising 15% masks a widening divergence in which a few AI-adjacent vendors capture nearly all the growth. IBM's caution on AI capex returns is an early warning that procurement scrutiny is tightening.

## Bottom Line

The market spent Friday celebrating a bad jobs report, and the celebration was narrow — mega-cap growth up, small caps down, which is a market pricing lower rates rather than better growth. That trade works until it collides with an oil-constrained Fed, and next week's CPI is where the collision either happens or gets deferred another month. I would not add broad beta into record highs that are partly built on an unsigned Hormuz agreement.

I would add selectively where fundamentals and multiples have genuinely disconnected, and today that is the fintech and payments complex. Corpay printing record earnings at ~14x its own raised guidance, Euronet compounding digital revenue at 31% while trading near 7x, and Shift4 at ~6.2x forward EBITDA are not momentum trades — they are businesses whose reported volumes contradict the recession their multiples already discount. Pair them with Jack Henry's contractual revenue and MarketAxess's structural long-volatility exposure, and the result is a basket that does not require the soft landing to work.
