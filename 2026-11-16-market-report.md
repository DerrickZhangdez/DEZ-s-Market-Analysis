Our posture today is constructive on the physical layer of the buildout and cautious on anything that requires cheap credit or a near-term Fed pivot. The five positions below sit in engineering services, merchant power, storage hardware, interconnect components and thermal management — deliberately one step removed from the GPU itself.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The hike nobody positioned for

The single most important repricing of the last ten days has nothing to do with Nvidia. The federal funds target band stands at **3.75–4.00%**, and markets have moved to effectively full conviction on an increase at the **December 9** FOMC meeting:

- **CME FedWatch:** ~89% probability of a 25 bp hike, ~11% probability of a 50 bp move.
- **Kalshi:** 87% for 25 bp, 12% for a larger move, 3% for no change.
- **Reuters economist poll:** **all 101 respondents** expect a hike to 4.00–4.25%, which most characterize as the second and final increase of the cycle.

This is not a close call anymore. The market is debating *size*, not *direction*.

### What forced the Fed's hand

October CPI printed **+0.4% month over month**, with **core CPI +0.3% for the third consecutive month**. A third straight 0.3% core reading annualizes to roughly 3.7% — comfortably above target and, critically, showing no deceleration. October PPI for final demand rose 0.3% m/m; sources differ on the year-over-year rate (6.5% in one account, 5.7% versus a 5.3% consensus in another), but both readings point the same direction.

Governor **Christopher Waller** has said he leans toward 25 bp in December and explicitly floated that a 50 bp move *should be considered* if core inflation holds at 0.3% or higher — which it just did. Kansas City Fed President **Schmid** polled an audience and found no support for a cut. The dovish wing of the committee has gone quiet.

### Why this matters more than the AI narrative

A hiking Fed changes the discount rate on every long-duration cash flow in the market, but it does something more specific and more dangerous to the AI complex: it raises the cost of the debt that is now funding the buildout. When hyperscaler capex was financed from operating cash flow, rates were a second-order concern. At 93% of operating cash flow — with the marginal dollar coming from bond issuance and private credit — the policy rate becomes a direct input into project economics.

## Geopolitical Factors

### Oil: a risk premium at war with a supply glut

Crude is caught between two forces pulling hard in opposite directions, and the result has been directionless chop rather than a clean trend.

**Bearish:** The IEA and EIA both see surplus. Saudi output has increased. Goldman Sachs projects Brent averaging **$88/bbl in 2027, falling to $78 by year-end 2027** as geopolitical premiums normalize, and has warned of a sizable 2027 surplus. Traders have been pricing de-escalation after the U.S. signaled it would not immediately resume hostilities against Iran, and crude headed for a second consecutive weekly decline.

**Bullish:** The physical risk is not theoretical. **Two supertankers were disabled by explosions after striking naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz** — a waterway that carries a substantial share of global seaborne crude. Mines in Hormuz are a materially different risk category from rhetoric; they raise insurance costs and re-route tonnage regardless of diplomatic signaling.

**Price:** Brent was near **$92.39** on November 12, with a November 16 report putting it closer to **$95.60**, and a separate headline claiming sub-$90. The EIA's November outlook projected Brent near $100 for the remainder of 2026.

> *These figures do not reconcile. The spread across sources is roughly $6/bbl, which is too wide to attribute to timing alone. Treat the level as "low-to-mid $90s, unusually uncertain" and verify against a primary feed before acting on any oil-levered position.*

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: AI Infrastructure & Data Centers

*(Rotating spotlight: day-of-year 320 mod 6 = 2 → AI infrastructure & data centers — power, cooling, networking, interconnect, memory.)*

This sector has spent six weeks being sold as a single undifferentiated block, and that is the opportunity. The market is treating a merchant power producer with twenty-year contracted cash flows, a mechanical contractor with a signed $17.8 billion backlog, and an unprofitable neocloud borrowing at 16% as though they carry identical exposure to one variable: whether hyperscalers keep spending. They do not.

#### The demand signal is still accelerating — in the order book

The most reliable evidence in this sector is not analyst commentary but contracted backlog, which is auditable and forward-looking:

| Company | Backlog | Growth |
|---|---|---|
| Comfort Systems (FIX) | **$17.81B** (Sept 30) | +90% YoY; +26.7% QoQ (from $14.06B at Jun 30, $9.38B a year prior) |
| Powell Industries (POWL) | **$1.8B** (Mar 31) | +33% YoY; plus a post-quarter **$400M+** single data center order, its largest ever |
| AAON | **$1.9B** | +40.5% YoY |
| Vertiv (VRT) | **$15B** at last disclosure | More than doubled — *company has since stopped reporting orders and backlog* |

Vertiv's decision to **stop disclosing orders and backlog in 2026** is the single most important governance datapoint in this sector and deserves more attention than it has received. When a company discontinues the metric that drove its re-rating, the burden of proof shifts.

The underlying demand curve remains intact: the IEA estimates data center consumption at roughly **485 TWh in 2025, nearly doubling to ~950 TWh by 2030**. That is a physical constraint requiring generation, transmission, switchgear, cooling and interconnect — none of which can be software-substituted.

#### The financing signal is deteriorating

Against that, the funding picture has turned materially worse in six weeks:

- **CoreWeave's bond yield topped 16%** on November 6. Jefferies' Christopher Wood characterized the risk as "the credit card being taken away."
- **Goldman:** $296B hyperscaler debt issuance in 2026 → an estimated **$440B in 2027**.
- **Bank of America:** AI-related credit issuance of **$685B in 2027**, up ~31%, with growth concentrated in **private credit** — the least transparent corner of the market.
- **JPMorgan:** AI capex reaching **~93% of hyperscaler operating cash flow in 2026**, versus 33% in 2023.
- Spending has outpaced operating cash flow for nine consecutive months — reportedly the first such stretch since 2011. Negative free cash flow at Alphabet, Amazon and Meta drove part of the early-November selling.
- Credit default swap levels are rising. Rating agencies issued cautious notes on November 4 but have not downgraded.

**The synthesis:** demand for AI infrastructure is real and contracted; the *equity* risk has migrated from "will they buy?" to "how is it financed, and who holds the paper when the cycle turns?" The correct response is to own the contracted, cash-generative, investment-grade layer of the stack and avoid the leveraged, pre-revenue layer entirely. Every pick below clears that bar.

#### Sub-sector triage

**Mechanical & electrical construction** — The cleanest exposure. These firms bill against signed contracts, carry negative working capital, and do not own the assets. Valuation dispersion here is extreme: EMCOR at roughly 16–18x forward versus Comfort Systems at 22–30x and Quanta near 30–36x, for broadly similar end-market exposure. EMCOR has fallen ~17% in a month and Comfort Systems ~26.8%.

**Merchant power** — The most mispriced sub-sector. Independent power producers signing long-dated data center PPAs trade at **13–16x forward earnings** while selling an output whose scarcity value is rising. Vistra at ~14x forward and Talen at ~14.8x are priced as commodity generators, not as owners of scarce, dispatchable, interconnection-queue-advantaged capacity.

**Storage & memory** — Nearline HDD is structurally tight. Seagate shipped 789 exabytes in FY2026 on $12.2B revenue at a 46% gross margin and $3.2B net income, with nearline exabytes rising to 695 from 497. Capacity is **committed via purchase orders through calendar 2026**, with long-term agreements extending through 2027. Seagate's HAMR products reached ~40% of nearline exabyte run-rate by fiscal year-end. Both majors trade around 17–23x forward.

**Interconnect & optical** — Bifurcated. Amphenol at ~22–26x forward is defensible against 23% projected FY2027 revenue growth. Pure-play optics are not: Lumentum at ~41–43x forward against a decade-average multiple near 19x, and Fabrinet at ~37–38x against a three-year average of 28–32x, are priced for flawless execution. We own the diversified component maker and avoid the pure-plays.

**Thermal management** — Consolidating in real time. Samsung is reportedly in talks to acquire **Modine**, which sent the stock up ~29% on November 12. That removes a comparable from the independent universe and re-rates what remains.

### Artificial Intelligence

**Nvidia is the entire story, and the story is governance, not silicon.**

Jensen Huang **resigned as CEO on November 12** following reports of an affair with a subordinate and a relationship with a woman U.S. officials feared could be connected to Chinese intelligence. CFO **Colette Kress** was named interim CEO. An independent board investigation remains open.

As of this morning, the *Financial Times* reports Huang is discussing a **comeback** with allies, who have organized a lobbying effort — including an open letter signed by figures such as Elon Musk — to pressure the board. Nvidia's board states that **no formal process has been initiated**. Nvidia has not confirmed, and neither has Huang. Shares are lower pre-market on the report.

Read that price reaction carefully. The stock is falling *on news that the founder might return*. That is the market pricing in prolonged governance instability, a contested boardroom, and the likelihood of congressional testimony — not expressing a view on Huang's operational value. A founder returning to a company whose board forced him out eight days earlier is not a clean resolution; it is a second crisis.

**Tuesday's print:** Nvidia guided to **$108B ± 2%**. Consensus is ~**$109.08B revenue and $2.47 EPS** (LSEG); Barchart carries $2.49. The stock is **down ~20% in November** and roughly **30% from its highs**. The asymmetry here is unusual: a beat-and-raise delivered by an interim CEO into a leadership controversy may not be rewarded, while a miss would be punished across the entire complex. We would not express a view on this event through any position, long or short.

**Anthropic's IPO** is expected on Nasdaq this week, though no confirmed pricing date or offering terms have surfaced. Michael Burry has publicly framed the Anthropic, OpenAI and SpaceX offerings as "selling into peak euphoria." Analysts flag a **potential IPO delay as a bearish catalyst** for AI sentiment broadly — a successful debut would do more for the complex this week than anything in Nvidia's release.

Separately, a reported push among AI leaders for **slower frontier-model development** produced a notable rotation: SaaS names rallied while chips fell. If that becomes policy rather than posture, it compresses the compute-intensity assumptions underpinning the entire infrastructure trade — a genuine tail risk to the spotlight sector.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

Friday's Nasdaq underperformance was explicitly a **chip-sector selloff**. Nvidia fell ~13% over the prior week. AMD and Broadcom have been consistent decliners; in October, AMD lost 6%, Intel 5.8% and Broadcom 3.6%.

Two crosscurrents are worth isolating:

2. **Storage is decoupling from logic.** HDD and NAND suppliers are operating under multi-quarter allocation with contracted pricing, while logic faces both the China file and the post-Huang uncertainty. This is the most defensible place to sit in hardware today, and it is why our semiconductor-adjacent exposure is expressed through storage rather than through processors.

### Software & SaaS

Enterprise software is the one corner of technology that **benefits** from the AI financing squeeze. Software companies are asset-light, cash-generative, and do not require $440 billion of 2027 debt issuance to grow. The rotation already showed up once this month: on one session, ServiceNow jumped 7.4% while Intuit, Adobe and Salesforce gained 4.7–5.5%, and Intel, Micron, Broadcom and AMD each fell more than 4%.

The counterargument — agentic AI compressing seat-based pricing — remains unfalsified, which is precisely why multiples have not recovered. We are not adding software exposure today: our last two rotations already allocated there, and the sector's central question is a two-to-three-year debate, not a November one.

### 1. EME — EMCOR Group, Inc.
*Sub-sector: Mechanical & electrical construction services*

**Thesis:** The cheapest listed way to own data center construction. EMCOR builds and services the electrical and mechanical systems inside mission-critical facilities, and its shares have fallen roughly **17% in a month** on fears of post-midterm regulatory restrictions on data center construction — a sentiment-driven de-rating that has not touched the signed backlog. It trades at a meaningful discount to directly comparable peers doing the same work.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **P/E (trailing):** ~19.0x (MarketBeat, Nov 13, at $658.92)
- **P/E (forward):** **~16.3x** on the most recent Zacks reading; sources range 16.3x–23.1x depending on as-of date
- **Peer comparison:** Comfort Systems ~21.8x–29.8x forward; Quanta ~29.8x–35.9x forward — EMCOR is the clear value name in the group
- **EV/EBITDA:** Not confirmed in sources consulted; flagged as a gap
- **PEG:** ~1.3x using 16.3x forward against ~12.4% consensus EPS growth (2026→2027)
- **52-week range:** $564.92 – $951.96 | **Consensus target:** $902.88

**Growth Profile:** Q2 2026 revenue **+19.8% YoY to $5.15B**. FY2026 revenue guidance raised to **$18.50–19.25B**. Consensus EPS of **$33.04 (2026) → $37.13 (2027)**, roughly +12.4%.

**Competitive Moat:** Skilled-trade labor scarcity is the binding constraint on data center delivery schedules, and EMCOR is one of a handful of contractors able to staff large mission-critical projects nationally. Prefabrication capability and multi-decade hyperscaler relationships make it very hard to displace mid-project. Importantly, roughly half the business is recurring building services — maintenance and retrofit — which cushions a new-construction downturn.

**Key Risk:** Customer concentration in hyperscaler capex. If the post-midterm regulatory restrictions on data center construction materialize at scale, new bookings slow even though backlog converts. Secondary risk: skilled labor cost inflation compressing fixed-price contract margins.

**Rating: Strong Buy**

### 2. VST — Vistra Corp.
*Sub-sector: Independent power producer / merchant generation*

**Thesis:** The market is pricing Vistra as a cyclical commodity generator while it signs long-dated power purchase agreements with data center counterparties for an output whose scarcity value is structurally rising. At roughly **14x forward earnings**, it is one of the few ways to own AI-driven electricity demand without paying an AI multiple. Shares were down ~5.7% YTD at last reading despite the thesis strengthening.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **P/E (trailing):** ~25x
- **P/E (forward):** **~14x** (one source cites 15.9x versus Talen's 14.8x — call it 14–16x)
- **PEG / EV-EBITDA:** Not reliably sourced; IPP EV/EBITDA is distorted by asset mix and hedge books, and I would not quote a figure I could not verify
- **Credit:** **Fitch upgraded Vistra to investment grade in late May 2026** — materially lowering cost of capital precisely as peers face credit stress
- **Targets:** Average ~$217.42 (vs. ~$151.72 at the referenced quote, ~43% implied upside); Scotiabank Street-high **$298**; one third-party model dissents at $140.83

**Growth Profile:** Earnings growth is driven by PPA repricing rather than volume. The demand backdrop — IEA data center consumption roughly doubling from ~485 TWh (2025) to ~950 TWh (2030) — tightens reserve margins in Vistra's core ERCOT and PJM markets, where it owns dispatchable capacity that cannot be replicated quickly.

**Competitive Moat:** Interconnection queues and permitting timelines are the real barrier to entry in power. Existing, interconnected, dispatchable generation is a genuinely scarce asset — a new combined-cycle plant takes four to six years and a nuclear unit far longer. Vistra's nuclear and gas fleet sits inside the two markets with the heaviest data center load growth, and its retail arm provides a natural hedge.

**Key Risk:** Commodity and regulatory. A sustained decline in power prices, an adverse ERCOT/PJM market-design ruling, or state-level pushback on data center load growth and ratepayer cost allocation would compress the PPA repricing opportunity. Merchant generators also carry real weather and outage risk that contracted utilities do not.

**Rating: Strong Buy**

### 3. WDC — Western Digital Corporation
*Sub-sector: Data storage hardware / memory*

**Thesis:** AI inference generates and retains enormous volumes of data, and nearline hard disk capacity is sold out. Industry nearline capacity is **committed via purchase orders through calendar 2026**, with long-term agreements running into 2027 — a visibility profile more like a contracted industrial than a cyclical component maker — yet WDC trades around 17–22x forward with meaningful capital returns.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **P/E (forward):** **~17.5x–22x** depending on source and date; WDC screens cheaper than Seagate in most comparisons (one pairing: WDC 17.45x vs. STX 19.32x; another: WDC 21x vs. STX 23x)
- **P/E (trailing):** ~18x in one account
- **P/S / EV-EBITDA / PEG:** Not reliably sourced across the material consulted; flagged as a gap rather than estimated
- **Industry economics (Seagate as read-through):** FY2026 revenue ~$12.2B, **46% gross margin**, $3.2B net income, 789 exabytes shipped; nearline exabytes 695 vs. 497 prior year

**Growth Profile:** Growth is capacity-led and contracted rather than price-speculative. The structural driver is that no economically viable substitute exists for bulk cold and warm storage at scale — flash remains far too expensive per terabyte for the archival tiers AI workloads generate.

**Competitive Moat:** A two-player rational duopoly with Seagate. Both have demonstrated supply discipline through this cycle — allocating capacity rather than chasing share — which is the single most important variable in HDD profitability. Capital intensity and decades of accumulated head and media IP make new entry effectively impossible.

**Key Risk:** A cyclical hyperscaler capex reset is the clear threat; storage orders are discretionary at the margin and would be cut alongside compute. Technology risk also runs in the background — WDC must keep pace with Seagate's HAMR roadmap, which reached ~40% of Seagate's nearline exabyte run-rate by fiscal year-end.

**Rating: Buy**

### 4. APH — Amphenol Corporation
*Sub-sector: Electronic components & interconnect*

**Thesis:** Amphenol sells the connectors, cables and interconnect assemblies that every AI rack requires regardless of which vendor wins the compute socket — a genuinely vendor-agnostic toll on the buildout. At ~22–26x forward against roughly 23% projected FY2027 revenue growth, it is priced far more reasonably than the pure-play optics names expressing the same theme at 37–43x.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **P/E (forward):** **~21.8x** (GuruFocus, Oct 22) / **22.67x** (Yahoo Finance) / ~26x (Investing.com) / 26–30x (Muffett) — call it **22–26x**
- **Industry median forward P/E:** ~17.5x, so APH carries roughly a 25% premium
- **Historical context:** Yahoo's historical readings for APH range ~27x–38x, so the current multiple sits **below** its own recent range
- **PEG:** **~1.0x** using ~23% FY2027 revenue growth against a ~22–26x multiple — the most attractive growth-adjusted valuation in this slate
- **Note:** A **2-for-1 split took effect September 3, 2026**; pre-split per-share figures in screeners are not comparable

**Growth Profile:** Consensus FY2027 revenue growth of **~23.3%**, with roughly **55% YoY growth expected in the upcoming quarter** — though that figure is inflated by the **CommScope acquisition** and is not organic. Investors should underwrite the low-20s organic number, not the headline.

**Competitive Moat:** Extreme product breadth (hundreds of thousands of SKUs across aerospace, defense, automotive, industrial and IT), design-win stickiness — once a connector is qualified into a platform it rarely changes — and a serial-acquisition model that has compounded capital effectively for two decades. Diversification across end markets means a data center slowdown is cushioned by defense, auto and industrial demand, which is precisely the characteristic the pure-plays lack.

**Key Risk:** Acquisition integration, specifically CommScope, which is a large and operationally complex deal that flatters reported growth. Valuation leaves modest room for error, and a broad AI capex deceleration would compress both the multiple and the IT datacom segment simultaneously.

**Rating: Buy**

## Risk Factors

**1. A 50 basis point December hike — SEVERE, and the most underpriced risk on this list.**
Markets assign roughly 11–12% probability to a half-point move. But Waller has explicitly said 50 bp should be considered if core CPI holds at 0.3% — and it just printed 0.3% for the third consecutive month. If Tuesday's retail sales come in hot, that probability repriced sharply higher would hit long-duration equities hard and would arrive *before* the market has digested Nvidia. The consensus view that December brings "the second and final hike of the cycle" is itself an assumption, not a commitment.

**2. AI credit contagion — SEVERE.**
CoreWeave borrowing above 16% is not an isolated data point; it is the marginal cost of capital for the leveraged tier of the buildout. With $440B of hyperscaler issuance projected for 2027 and BofA expecting $685B of AI-related credit — increasingly through opaque private credit channels — a single failed financing or a genuine rating downgrade would transmit to equities faster than any earnings miss. CDS levels are already rising. This is the risk most likely to turn a correction into something worse.

**3. Nvidia governance and Tuesday's print — HIGH.**
An interim CEO reporting into an open board investigation, a founder publicly lobbying for reinstatement, a potential Senate Banking subpoena, and the Treasury Secretary pressing on China policy — all before the numbers. The stock is already down ~20% this month and ~30% from highs. Because Nvidia's index weight is enormous, an adverse reaction is not containable to a single name.

**4. Data center construction regulation post-midterms — MODERATE but rising.**
Sell-side commentary already attributes part of the recent construction-name weakness to anticipated restrictions on data center permitting. Local and state friction over power draw, water use and ratepayer cost allocation is the most plausible mechanism by which the physical buildout slows for reasons entirely unrelated to AI demand. This is the specific risk to EME and AAON.

**5. Oil and the Strait of Hormuz — MODERATE, with fat tails.**
The consensus is comfortably bearish crude on surplus. That consensus is being expressed while naval mines are disabling supertankers in the world's most important oil chokepoint. The *distribution* here is badly skewed: a modest further decline is likely, but a disorderly escalation in Hormuz would deliver a supply shock into an economy the Fed is already tightening into — the worst possible macro combination.

**6. Anthropic IPO reception — MODERATE.**
A strong debut would meaningfully repair AI sentiment this week. A pulled or poorly received deal — with Burry already framing these offerings as "selling into peak euphoria" — would confirm the funding-window-closing thesis and pressure the entire complex.

## Bottom Line

The market has spent two weeks correctly identifying that AI infrastructure has a financing problem and incorrectly concluding that everything touching AI infrastructure shares it. Contracted backlogs at the physical layer are growing 40–90% year over year, nearline storage is allocated through 2026, and merchant power with twenty-year data center PPAs trades at 14x forward — while the leveraged tier borrows at 16%. That dispersion is the trade: own the contracted, investment-grade, cash-generative layer and leave the credit-dependent layer to someone else.

Respect the macro, though, because it is the larger force. A Fed that is hiking into a third consecutive 0.3% core print is a materially different environment than the one most portfolios were built for in 2026, and the 11% probability assigned to a 50 basis point December move looks too low. Tuesday brings retail sales in the morning and Nvidia at night; we would let both pass before adding risk, and we would not express a view on either through a position.
