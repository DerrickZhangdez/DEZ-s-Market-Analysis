## Macro & Monetary Environment

**The Fed is no longer debating how fast to cut. It is debating whether to hike.** That reframing is the single most important thing for investors to internalize right now. The federal funds target has sat at 3.50%–3.75% since December 2025. At the July 29 meeting the Committee voted 9–3 to hold — and critically, all three dissenters wanted a 25 basis point *increase*. J.P. Morgan Wealth Management now expects a 25 bp hike at the September 15–16 meeting, which also carries updated projections.

The case for tightening rests on two legs. First, the inflation mix is awkward rather than alarming: headline cooled to 3.5% in June, which by itself argues for patience, but core at 2.6% remains above target and has proved sticky. Second — and this is the leg that has strengthened materially in the last month — the Iran conflict is producing sustained energy and supply-chain cost pressure, exactly the sort of shock that a central bank worried about its own credibility cannot look through indefinitely. Add investor doubt about the Fed's resolve following the July hold and changes in Fed leadership, and the bar for a September hike has fallen considerably.

**Today's data:** the ISM Manufacturing PMI is released on the first business day of the month at 10:00 a.m. ET, putting it on the tape today. The read-in from S&P Global's flash was mildly softening — 53.2 in August against 53.9 previously, undershooting expectations of 53.9. Still expansionary, but decelerating, which is precisely the uncomfortable combination for a Fed contemplating tightening: growth cooling at the margin while cost pressure builds.

**What to watch this week:** the ISM prints and their prices-paid subcomponents (the cleanest early read on whether crude is leaking into goods inflation), any Fed communication ahead of the pre-meeting blackout, and the long end of the curve. A 10-year that keeps grinding toward 5% does more damage to long-duration equity valuations than the front end does.

## Geopolitical Factors

The Hormuz theater is now the dominant macro variable, not a background risk.

Overnight into September 1, U.S. forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island after detecting preparations to lay mines in the waterway. Iran responded with missile and drone attacks on U.S. facilities in Jordan and on targets in the UAE. A supertanker caught fire after striking two naval mines in the southern Strait, per Iranian state media, and two further tankers — one Saudi, one South Korean-owned — were hit by projectiles Monday night. President Trump has extended military threats to Kharg Island, Iran's principal oil export terminal.

Three points matter for portfolios:

1. **This is a resumption, not a new war.** Hostilities are roughly six months old and appear locked in a stalemate, with periodic escalation punctuating relative calm — the last quiet stretch ran about a month before breaking overnight. Markets have repeatedly priced a Hormuz reopening and been disappointed; each disappointment resets crude higher.
2. **The Kharg Island threat is the genuine tail risk.** Strikes on rocket launchers are noise. A strike on Iran's main export terminal, or successful mining of the Strait, would take Brent well beyond current levels and force a wholesale repricing of inflation expectations — and therefore of the Fed path.
3. **The equity transmission channel is rates, not just energy.** Elevated oil raises bond yields, which compresses the multiples of long-duration assets. That is why the Nasdaq is down twice as much as the Dow today. Investors hedging this risk through energy equities alone are hedging half of it.

Today's rotation reflects all of the above: Chevron +1.7%, Exxon Mobil +1.5%, Occidental +1.8%, ConocoPhillips +1.3%, Halliburton +2.5%, SLB +1.7%, with defensive utilities also bid, while technology and communication services lag.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Healthcare Technology, Medical Devices & Revenue-Generating Biotech

*(Rotating spotlight, day-of-year 244 mod 6 = 4)*

This is the most interesting sector in the market right now, and the reason is a mispricing born of a policy scare that has since been resolved.

**The setup: a sector sold for reasons that no longer apply.** Healthcare entered 2026 as the market's designated victim of policy risk. XLV was down roughly 7% year-to-date in early 2026 while SPY was up about 5% — a spectacular failure of healthcare's supposed defensive character, and an unusually painful one for anyone who owned it *for* that character. The proximate causes were the Medicare drug-price negotiation regime and the threat of pharmaceutical import tariffs.

Both have now largely resolved, and resolved better than feared:

- On January 1, 2026, the first tranche of negotiated Maximum Fair Prices took effect on ten blockbuster drugs, with cuts of 38% to 79%. Severe headline numbers — but most affected manufacturers had already reset long-term guidance and R&D plans years in advance. The worst case was in the price before it was in the P&L.
- Trade agreements have capped pharmaceutical import tariffs at 15%, and several large manufacturers (AbbVie, Merck among them) secured exemptions from tariffs and price controls in exchange for U.S. manufacturing investment.

The result is a sector where the overhang has lifted while the de-rating has only partly reversed — XLV is down about 3% year-to-date against a 14% gain over the trailing twelve months, having clawed back from the earlier trough as allocators (State Street among them) moved healthcare from neutral to bullish. Sector M&A has run to roughly $284 billion in deal value in 2026, which is the market's own vote on where value sits.

**Medtech specifically: valuations reset, innovation did not.** Bernstein's framing is the right one — multiples have compressed while the underlying drivers remain intact. Sector earnings growth is forecast at roughly 9.6% annualized through 2029, and many medtech names still trade below historical averages on lingering macro caution rather than deteriorating fundamentals. Q1 2026 earnings drew harsh investor reactions across the group; that is usually where the entry points get made.

The M&A confirmation is unambiguous. Boston Scientific agreed on January 15, 2026, to acquire Penumbra for approximately $14.5 billion in cash and stock — a strategic buyer paying up for high-growth interventional capability, which is the clearest possible statement that industry insiders think public-market medtech valuations are too low. Note carefully what this does to a screener-driven process: **Penumbra is no longer investable as a fundamental long, and neither is Catalyst Pharmaceuticals**, which Angelini Pharma is acquiring at $31.50 per share for $4.1 billion. Catalyst screens beautifully — sub-14x earnings, 20%+ promoted-product growth, $756 million of cash and no funded debt — and is precisely the sort of name a valuation screen would surface today. It is a merger-arb position, not an investment thesis, and it is excluded from our picks for that reason.

### Artificial Intelligence

The AI trade has entered its "show me the cash flows" phase, and the debate has shifted from whether demand is real to whether the *financing structures* are real.

The centerpiece remains the OpenAI–NVIDIA arrangement to deploy at least 10 gigawatts of NVIDIA systems, millions of GPUs, with NVIDIA investing up to $100 billion progressively as each gigawatt comes online and the first gigawatt landing in the second half of 2026 on the Vera Rubin platform. Concretely, NVIDIA is backing up to $105 billion of financing for an OpenAI data center in Ohio — an initial 4.25 GW with an option on a further 3.75 GW, built and managed by SB Energy at the PORTS-Pike Technology Campus under a 20-year lease to OpenAI.

Two objections have gained traction, and both deserve to be taken seriously rather than dismissed:

**The circularity objection.** A chip vendor financing its customer's purchases of its own chips is a structure that flatters near-term revenue quality. Wall Street is genuinely split on this, and the split is the reason NVDA is up "only" about 20% year-to-date in a year when its bookings suggest more.

**The scale objection.** When the Ohio deal was finalized $145 billion below earlier reported figures, it was read as evidence of artificial demand signaling rather than as ordinary negotiation. Whether or not that read is fair, the market's willingness to reach for it tells you how much benefit of the doubt has been withdrawn.

The competitive picture is also shifting. OpenAI's public position that its Broadcom custom silicon is "a big advance" is the most consequential AI development of the past week for anyone holding merchant silicon. Custom ASICs at hyperscaler volume do not need to beat NVIDIA on performance per watt; they need only be good enough to shift negotiating leverage. That is a margin story for NVIDIA long before it is a volume story.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

Semiconductors are in the middle of a violent unwind that has now decoupled almost completely from the underlying pricing data — which is exactly the sort of dislocation that eventually matters.

**The damage:** chip stocks have shed roughly $1.5 trillion in market value since June 25. Memory has been the epicenter and has entered a bear market. Micron dropped 5.1% premarket Monday to $929.50; SK Hynix fell more than 15% in a record decline as investors took profits following last week's Nasdaq debut. Samsung and SoftBank have been dragged along. AMD, Intel, and Micron all extended losses through late August, and the slump pushed the Nasdaq toward correction territory.

**The disconnect:** third-quarter DRAM contracts are settling 20% to 30% *higher* this month. Google and Meta have signed five-year contracts locking in both price and volume. Analysts do not expect meaningful new supply before 2028.

Reconcile those two paragraphs and you have the central question in semis right now. Contract prices up 20–30% with multi-year hyperscaler commitments and no new supply for two years is not the fundamental backdrop of a bear market — it is the backdrop of a cycle peak that has not arrived yet. What is actually being repriced is the *multiple* investors will pay for peak-cycle memory earnings, and that repricing is a function of the discount rate and of AI-capex credibility, not of DRAM contracts.

I am not making a memory pick today — today's mandate is the healthcare spotlight, and memory names have appeared in recent reports. But the divergence between physical market tightness and equity market pricing is the single most interesting setup on the board, and it goes on the watchlist explicitly.

### Software & SaaS

Software is living through the most fundamental business-model challenge of its public-market history, and the numbers are stark.

Median SaaS EV/Revenue stood at 3.4x as of March 2026. Public SaaS revenue multiples compressed from a Q3 2025 peak of 6.1x to 5.1x–5.5x by March. For the first time ever, software as a group trades at a *discount* to the S&P 500. The trigger has a date: the launch of agentic AI office tooling in January 2026 kicked off what the market named the "SaaSpocalypse," erasing on the order of $1 trillion of enterprise SaaS market capitalization in weeks.

The mechanism is worth stating precisely, because it determines which names survive. The threat is not that AI writes software better. It is that per-seat licensing — the pricing architecture underpinning essentially all modern SaaS — breaks if enterprises deploy agents that reduce headcount. Fewer seats, less recurring revenue, and the growth rate that justified a 6x revenue multiple simply is not there. Companies are now guiding to sub-10% growth for 2026.

The market is bifurcating rather than derating uniformly, and correctly so. AI-native companies command 40%–80% valuation premiums over comparable traditional SaaS. Vendors supplying the "intelligence layer," or able to demonstrate measurable productivity gains, keep premium multiples; legacy SaaS is being repriced as a utility — stable, low-growth, with real downside if seat counts actually fall. One genuine bright spot: EBITDA margins are expanding across the group even as revenue growth stays muted, so the cash flows are holding up better than the multiples.

The practical implication for a valuation-driven process is that cheap software is a trap unless you can answer one question: is this company's revenue tied to seats, or to something an agent cannot displace? That question is why our healthcare-tech exposure today sits in Omnicell — hardware-anchored pharmacy automation with a device installed base — rather than in a per-seat vertical SaaS name.

**Rating: Accumulate** — build the position into the drawdown rather than chasing; the Q3 bookings print is the moment of truth.

### 5. FMS — Fresenius Medical Care AG
*(Dialysis care delivery and renal products)*

**Thesis:** A P/E of 10.86 and a PEG near 0.13 on a business whose FME25+ transformation program is delivering real, quantified margin expansion — €67 million of savings in Q2 alone — against essential, non-discretionary demand. This is the deepest-value name in the slate and the most defensive: dialysis patients do not defer treatment because Brent crossed $91.

**Valuation Metrics:** ADR price $22.26 · **Trailing P/E 10.86x** · **PEG ~0.13** · 2026 revenue guidance broadly flat against €19,628 million in 2025, implying P/S well under 0.5x · Group operating income margin guidance 10.5%–12.0%, with operating income projected to move between positive and negative mid-single-digit percentages. Worth flagging the disagreement in the data: one valuation model (GF Value) reads FMS as roughly 4.8% *over*valued, while sell-side work argues the undervaluation "is getting clearer." I side with the earnings-multiple and margin-trajectory evidence, but investors should know the case is contested.

**Growth Profile:** This is explicitly *not* a revenue growth story — guidance is flat. It is a margin-recovery and cash-conversion story. FME25+ delivered €67 million of Q2 savings; margin reached 11.7% in Q3 2025 on the back of the program plus pricing improvements; the 10.5%–12.0% full-year target implies continued expansion from a depressed base. Earnings grow through cost structure and price, not volume.

**Competitive Moat:** Vertical integration is close to unique — Fresenius both operates the dialysis clinic network and manufactures the machines, dialyzers, and consumables that run in it, capturing margin at both stages and giving it a cost position competitors cannot match. Clinic networks are effectively local-monopoly assets constrained by nephrologist relationships, payer contracts, and certificate-of-need regimes. Demand is as inelastic as it gets in healthcare.

**Key Risk:** Flat revenue is a real constraint, not a rounding error — a margin story has a finite runway, and once FME25+ is fully harvested the earnings growth has to come from somewhere. Treatment-volume pressure (visible in both Q1 and Q2 as "volume pressure") is the thing to watch: if it persists, cost savings are offsetting decline rather than compounding on a stable base. Add U.S. reimbursement policy exposure and, for ADR holders, EUR/USD translation risk.

**Rating: Accumulate**

## Risk Factors

**1. A September Fed hike into a supply shock — severity: high, probability: rising.** The market has spent 2026 positioned for an easing bias that no longer exists. Three FOMC members already voted to hike in July, J.P. Morgan expects 25 bp on September 16, and the meeting carries updated projections that could reset the whole 2027 path. A hike delivered while manufacturing decelerates (S&P Global PMI 53.2, down from 53.9 and below expectations) is a policy-error scenario, and equity multiples have not discounted it. This is the risk that hurts every long-duration asset simultaneously.

**2. Hormuz escalation to export infrastructure — severity: high, probability: moderate.** Brent at $91.28 already reflects tanker strikes and mining attempts. It does not reflect a strike on Kharg Island — Iran's principal export terminal, which the U.S. President has now explicitly threatened — or successful closure of the Strait. Either would push crude far higher, feed directly into core inflation with a lag, and force the Fed's hand regardless of growth. The pattern of the last six months is a stalemate punctuated by escalation, with each hoped-for reopening disappointed; positioning for de-escalation has been a losing trade five times running.

**3. Rates transmission to equity multiples — severity: high, probability: already happening.** The 10-year at 4.79% and rising is doing visible damage: today's 1.36% Nasdaq decline against 0.66% for the Dow is pure duration, not news. Software already trades at a discount to the S&P 500 for the first time in history, with median SaaS EV/Revenue at 3.4x, down from a 6.1x peak. If the 10-year takes out 5%, there is no valuation support level anyone can point to with confidence for the highest-multiple cohort.

**4. AI capex credibility and circular financing — severity: high if it breaks, probability: low near-term.** Roughly $1.5 trillion of semiconductor market value has already evaporated since June 25 while DRAM contract prices *rose* 20%–30% — which tells you the equity market is repricing the durability of AI capex, not current demand. The vendor-financing structures (NVIDIA backing up to $105 billion for its own customer's Ohio build) are the pressure point: they flatter revenue quality and invite exactly the "artificial demand" reading that greeted the $145 billion downsizing. Concurrently, OpenAI's endorsement of its Broadcom custom silicon signals margin pressure on merchant chips well before volume pressure arrives.

**5. Healthcare-specific policy and reimbursement drift — severity: moderate, probability: moderate.** The big overhangs have resolved favorably: MFP cuts of 38%–79% took effect January 1 and were pre-absorbed into guidance, and pharmaceutical import tariffs are capped at 15% with exemptions available for U.S. manufacturing commitments. But the negotiation regime expands in future tranches, the 15% cap is a policy choice rather than a law of nature, and provider-side reimbursement pressure flows through to hospital capital budgets — a direct risk to OMCL's bookings and an indirect one to MDT's capital-equipment lines. FMS carries specific U.S. renal reimbursement exposure.

## Bottom Line

The market is being repriced by the bond market, not the earnings tape, and that distinction is where today's opportunity lives: multiples are compressing across a market whose fundamental news flow — Medtronic's raise, DRAM contracts up 20%–30%, expanding SaaS EBITDA margins — is mostly fine. Own the discount rate risk deliberately rather than by accident, which today means healthcare at 10x–17x forward earnings instead of long-duration growth at multiples that require a friendly Fed.

Our five picks are all in that spotlight sector for a reason that is documented rather than thematic: the policy scare that de-rated healthcare in the first half of 2026 has resolved better than feared, insiders are paying $14.5 billion and $4.1 billion cash premiums for public medtech and rare-disease assets, and the equity multiples have only partly caught up. Barbell the exposure — MDT and FMS as ballast, HRMY and OMCL for return, GMED in between — and keep dry powder for the memory dislocation, where a $1.5 trillion drawdown against rising contract prices and no new supply before 2028 is the most interesting unresolved argument on the board.
