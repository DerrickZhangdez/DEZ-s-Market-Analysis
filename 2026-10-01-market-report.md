# Daily Market Report — Thursday, October 1, 2026

## Executive Summary

October opened with a market that is rallying *in spite of* its bond market rather than because of it. US equities pushed higher as technology strength — led by Micron's sixth consecutive record quarter — outweighed a deepening global bond rout that drove the 10-year Treasury yield to **5.33%, its highest level since April 2002**, and the 30-year to 5.67%, the highest since July 2002. The dissonance is the story: the S&P 500 traded around 7,696 (+0.58%) while UK 30-year gilts touched 6% for the first time since 1998 and French and Japanese yields hit multi-decade highs. September itself was a month of violent internal rotation rather than aggregate movement — the Nasdaq Composite gained 1.8% and set a record on September 22, while the Dow fell roughly 4.3% and the S&P 500 slipped 0.4%, leaving the index about 1.9% below its August record.

The macro backdrop is genuinely unusual, and investors should resist pattern-matching it to 2022. Inflation is *decelerating* — August PCE came in at 3.4% headline and 3.0% core, both below expectations — and yet long rates are rising anyway, driven by term premium, sovereign supply and an oil price carrying a rebuilt geopolitical risk premium rather than by inflation expectations alone. The Federal Reserve has moved to a tightening posture with the funds rate at 4.00% and a September median dot of 4.1% for 2026, implying one more hike is live. Today's spotlight sector — healthcare — is one of the few places where a long-duration cash flow stream is *not* being repriced by this, because the sector's valuations never participated in the multiple expansion that is now unwinding elsewhere.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The Fed has pivoted to fighting, not easing

The policy picture has inverted relative to consensus expectations held earlier in the cycle. The federal funds rate stands at **4.00%**, and the September 15–16 FOMC meeting produced a median projection of **4.1% for end-2026** — a figure that is only coherent if at least one more hike is on the table before year-end. Recent Fed communication has been framed around a commitment to the inflation fight, including a unanimous 12–0 hike vote.

The Summary of Economic Projections tells a sobering story about the path:

| PCE Measure | 2026 (Fed proj.) | 2027 (Fed proj.) | Actual (August 2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headline PCE | 3.7% | 2.3% | **3.4%** |
| Core PCE | 3.4% | 2.5% | **3.0%** |

Both 2026 figures were **revised upward** from the June estimates. More striking: policymakers do not see a return to the 2% target until **2029**. That is a three-year admission that the inflation problem is structural rather than transitory, and it is the single most important input into every long-duration valuation in the market.

### But the incoming data is *better* than the projections

Here is the tension that defines today's tape. August PCE printed at **3.4% headline against a 3.7% expectation**, and **core at 3.0% against 3.3% expected**. Both undershot. Inflation is decelerating faster than the Fed's own September projections assumed.

If disinflation is running ahead of the Fed's forecast, why are long yields making 24-year highs?

The answer is that this is **not an inflation-expectations move — it is a term-premium and supply move.** The drivers identified across sources are persistent inflation *plus* massive government borrowing *plus* strong economic growth, with rising oil as an accelerant. Note the ordering: sovereign debt issuance is doing as much work here as inflation. The confirming evidence is the global synchronization — UK 30-year borrowing costs at 6% for the first time since 1998, and French and Japanese yields at multi-decade highs. A purely American inflation story does not move JGBs and OATs. A global repricing of sovereign credit and term premium does.

### What to watch this week

1. **The 5.50% level on the 10-year.** There is no technical structure between here and the early-2000s range. A disorderly move through 5.50% would almost certainly break the VIX-16 complacency.
2. **Follow-through on the inflation undershoot.** If September data confirms the August deceleration, the Fed's 4.1% dot becomes very hard to justify and the front end should rally.
3. **Treasury issuance and buyback policy.** Buyback operations have been publicly reinforced as a liquidity tool. The execution details matter more than usual right now.
4. **Oil's passthrough.** With crude feeding directly into breakevens, the energy complex is currently the marginal driver of the long end.

## Geopolitical Factors

Energy markets in 2026 have been driven by geopolitics rather than by incremental supply-and-demand shifts, and that remains true today — crude's renewed climb was explicitly cited as a driver of the yield move.

**The Iran / Strait of Hormuz complex.** Tensions between the US and Iran have re-entered the pricing mechanism of oil and gas markets, rebuilding a structural risk premium across global energy benchmarks. The Strait of Hormuz — through which roughly **a quarter of seaborne oil trade** passes — has returned to the center of global energy security concerns. Several Middle Eastern producers have cut output on security threats, some facilities have been attacked, and transport operations have been disrupted.

**Where prices sit.** Brent is forecast to average **$86/bbl in 2026**, but the realized path has been violent: the benchmark briefly exceeded **$110** and approached **$117** during the early-March escalation. The World Bank's and consensus escalation scenario puts 2026 Brent in a **$95–$115** range if hostilities re-escalate or pipeline export constraints persist.

**The positioning tell — and why it matters.** Commodity trading advisers are carrying **91% short positions in Brent futures and 73% short in WTI**. That is extraordinary one-sidedness. The systematic community is betting that fundamental oversupply ultimately dominates the geopolitical premium. The asymmetry this creates is important and underappreciated: *any* genuine supply disruption now detonates into a crowded short, which is precisely the configuration that produces disorderly spikes rather than orderly repricings. For equity investors, this is the cleanest tail risk on the board — an oil spike into 5.33% ten-year yields would be a genuinely hostile combination.

**Trade and policy.** Tariff and drug-pricing policies revealed by the current administration have proven **manageable for drugmakers**, which is directly relevant to today's spotlight sector and has contributed materially to improved biotech sentiment through 2026. This is one of the few places where policy risk has resolved *favorably* versus expectations.

#### The setup: a sector that already had its drawdown

Healthcare in 2026 presents something rare in this market — a sector where the *valuation reset has already happened* and the *policy overhang has already cleared*. While software is mid-derating and semis are mid-cycle-debate, healthcare has spent years as the market's unloved corner and is only now being re-rated.

The performance picture is genuinely strong but bifurcated:

Biotech has roughly doubled the market's return. But the leadership is **rotating inside the sector right now**: in the most recent week, large-cap healthcare outperformed with XLV gaining 1.4% while **XBI fell 1.4%** — the speculative, equal-weighted end is losing steam after a hot run.

**This rotation is the actionable insight for today's picks.** Momentum is draining from clinical-stage speculation and moving toward names with *actual cash flows*. In a 5.33% ten-year environment, that is exactly what should happen: pre-revenue biotech is the longest-duration asset in public markets, and it cannot survive a sustained term-premium shock. Companies with real revenue, real margins and low multiples can. Today's entire pick list is built on that distinction, which is also why the stated bucket is "biotech **with real revenue**" rather than biotech generally.

#### The policy overhang has cleared — and that is underpriced

For most of the past several years, the bear case on healthcare was government pricing intervention. The current reality is that tariff and drug-pricing policies have proven **manageable for drugmakers**. Consensus framing for the balance of 2026 is that the industry's momentum carries forward *as long as strong clinical data continue to be rewarded and companies can bring drugs to market without major government pricing regulation* — and that condition is currently being met.

An overhang that resolves favorably is worth more than it appears, because the discount it imposed was applied to the *multiple*, not to the earnings. Removing it is a re-rating event, not an earnings event, and re-ratings happen faster than earnings compound.

#### Sub-sector by sub-sector

**Drug delivery and royalty models — the structurally advantaged corner.** The most interesting business model in healthcare right now is not drug discovery but **drug delivery tolling**. Halozyme's ENHANZE platform converts intravenous biologics into subcutaneous injections and collects royalties from the partner's franchise. The economics are remarkable: 2026 royalty revenue guided to **$1.220–1.245B, up 41–43%**, inside total revenue guidance of **$1.835–1.910B (+31–37%)**, producing adjusted EBITDA of **$1.225–1.280B — up 86–95% year over year**. Q2 2026 revenue grew 48% and royalties grew 50%. A business converting roughly two-thirds of revenue into EBITDA, growing royalties at 40%+, is a toll road, not a pharmaceutical company. The market is valuing it as the latter.

**Commercial oncology — the de-risked middle ground.** Exelixis occupies the space between speculative biotech and big pharma: a marketed franchise funding the next one. Cabozantinib global net product revenues rose approximately **13% year over year to $806M in Q2 2026**, with US revenues of $573M (+10%). FY2026 revenue guidance of **$2.525–2.625B** against $2.123B in 2025 implies roughly 19–24% growth. Critically, the next franchise is already at the FDA: **zanzalintinib's NDA for previously-treated metastatic colorectal cancer was accepted with a target action date of December 3, 2026**, with the company targeting $5B in annual revenue by 2033. That is a dated, binary, near-term catalyst attached to a company already trading at roughly 14–15x earnings.

**Rare disease and pulmonary — a trough with a very large option attached.** United Therapeutics is the sector's most interesting debate today. The core business is *shrinking*: Q2 2026 revenue fell 2% to $783.3M, and total Tyvaso revenue declined 4% to $452.6M, with nebulized Tyvaso down 18% to $126.0M against 4% growth in Tyvaso DPI to $326.6M — management attributes the pressure to competitive therapies. **Medical devices — where the actual value is.** This is the most dislocated sub-sector in healthcare. Blue-chip medtech names are trading **47–48% below 52-week highs while posting 13–29% revenue growth, with forward P/Es compressed to 15–22x**. That is a textbook disconnect between price and fundamentals. At the deeper-value end, Integra LifeSciences trades around **$16.65 against reaffirmed 2026 adjusted EPS guidance of $2.40–2.50** — under 7x forward adjusted earnings — having swung from a $(6.31) prior-year quarterly loss to a $0.06 GAAP profit. ### Artificial Intelligence

The AI capital cycle has reached a scale where it is a macroeconomic variable rather than a sector story. Global AI investment is tracking toward approximately **$1 trillion in 2026**, with roughly **$581 billion in the US — about 10% of all US private fixed investment**. Hyperscaler buildout estimates for the year run **$775–800 billion**. When a single capex theme accounts for a tenth of national private fixed investment, it is no longer possible to analyze it separately from GDP, rates, or the energy complex.

**The financing architecture is where the risk now lives.** Nvidia and OpenAI signed a letter of intent for at least **10 gigawatts** of AI data centers with Nvidia committing up to **$100 billion progressively as systems deploy**, with the first gigawatt expected online in 2H 2026 on the Vera Rubin platform. Nvidia separately backed **$105 billion** in financing for an OpenAI data center in Ohio — and then **scaled back that guarantee to cover only the first phase**.

That retrenchment deserves more attention than it received. Nvidia has also partnered with six major financial institutions to launch compute-financing platforms targeting over **$500 billion in third-party capital**. Read together, the direction of travel is clear: the chip vendor is working to move buildout risk *off its own balance sheet and onto external capital*. That is rational corporate finance, but it means the marginal AI data center is increasingly funded by third-party credit — in a market where the 10-year yields 5.33%. The cost of capital for this buildout has risen materially, and the entity best positioned to see the demand curve is reducing, not increasing, its own guarantee exposure.

Nvidia also announced a **$150 billion share repurchase**, bringing its total program to **$235 billion**, and gained 1.7% on the news. A buyback of that size is a statement about free cash flow confidence — and also a reminder that the company is choosing to return capital rather than underwrite more customer financing.

**A notable shift in tone.** The industry's posture moved visibly toward risk management in late September: Nvidia introduced software to keep AI agents contained, OpenAI scrapped the release of GPT-6.1 Astra over safety concerns, and Anthropic framed Sonnet 5.5 as improving existing tasks rather than pushing the frontier. The framing moved "from need for speed to seatbelts." For investors, a slower frontier-capability cadence with steadier deployment is arguably *better* for the names selling into real production workloads and *worse* for valuations predicated on discontinuous capability jumps.

### Software & SaaS

Software is experiencing **the industry's largest non-recessionary drawdown in more than 30 years**, and the numbers are historic:

- Roughly **$2 trillion** of software market value erased
- Software's S&P 500 weighting has fallen from **12% to 8.4%**
- Forward P/E has collapsed from **84.1x at the 2020–2022 peak to 22.7x by March 2026** — **below the S&P 500's own multiple for the first time in history**
- Price-to-sales compressed from **9x to 6x**, levels last seen in the mid-2010s

The bear thesis is **seat compression**: if one AI agent performs the work of several employees, enterprises cut license counts, and per-seat SaaS pricing models face structural revenue decay rather than cyclical softness. This narrative has swept indiscriminately across enterprise software, vertical market software, information services and online classifieds.

**My read: the narrative is directionally real but is being applied without discrimination.** Much of the selloff has been narrative-driven rather than grounded in deteriorating fundamentals. Per-seat pricing in knowledge-work categories genuinely is impaired. But software that prices on transactions, regulated workflows, or systems-of-record where the AI agent is a *consumer* of the system rather than a replacement for it should not carry the same discount. Software trading below the market multiple for the first time ever is the kind of statistic that, historically, marks the zone where indiscriminate selling creates genuine mispricing — while also being exactly the zone where value traps are most abundant. The discipline required here is to distinguish *pricing-model risk* from *demand risk*, and the market is currently charging the same penalty for both.

The parallel to today's spotlight is direct: this is why **Phreesia** — priced at ~8x EV/EBITDA on a workflow embedded in patient registration and payment, not on knowledge-worker seats — screens differently from a generic SaaS name at a similar multiple.

### 1. HALO — Halozyme Therapeutics, Inc. *(Drug-delivery platform & royalty streams)*

**Thesis.** Halozyme is not a drug developer — it is a toll road on the industry-wide conversion of intravenous biologics to subcutaneous administration, collecting royalties on partners' franchises without bearing their clinical or commercial risk. It is growing royalties at 40%+ with EBITDA nearly doubling, and trades at a single-digit-to-low-teens earnings multiple.

**Valuation Metrics.**
- **Forward P/E: 11.3x** (one provider) / **12.3x** (another); **GAAP trailing P/E 9.17x** (a third). *Providers disagree; the range is 9–12x on any measure.*
- **EV/EBITDA: 8.36x** (one provider) / **11.5x** (another) — both below historical averages and sector peers
- **P/S: ~5.5–6x** *(derived from EV implied by the 8.36x EV/EBITDA figure against 2026 revenue guidance — treat as approximate)*
- **PEG: ~0.25–0.3** *(derived from forward P/E against guided EBITDA growth — directionally very low, not a precise figure)*
- Reference price: ~$110.40

**Growth Profile.** FY2026 revenue guidance **$1.835–1.910B, +31–37% YoY**. Royalty revenue guidance **$1.220–1.245B, +41–43%**. Adjusted EBITDA guidance **$1.225–1.280B, +86–95%**. Q2 2026 revenue +48% YoY; Q2 royalty revenue +50% to $308M. Guidance has been **raised**, not trimmed — the multi-year outlook was raised as well.

**Competitive Moat.** ENHANZE is protected by patents and, more durably, by **switching costs that are effectively absolute**: a partner that has run its pivotal trials and secured approval on a subcutaneous formulation cannot change delivery platforms without repeating clinical development. Every new approval on the platform compounds a royalty annuity with near-zero incremental cost to Halozyme. Approximately two-thirds EBITDA conversion on revenue is the arithmetic proof of the model.

**Key Risk.** **Patent cliff concentration.** Royalty streams terminate on defined schedules, and the single largest contributor rolling off would create a visible revenue step-down that growth from newer partners must outrun. The low multiple is partly the market pricing exactly this. Investors must underwrite the *replacement* pipeline of new partner approvals, not merely extrapolate current growth. Secondary risk: partner-franchise concentration means Halozyme inherits the commercial fate of drugs it does not control.

**Rating: Strong Buy.**

### 5. PHR — Phreesia, Inc. *(Healthcare IT — patient intake, access & payments)*

**Thesis.** A healthcare workflow platform at roughly **8x EV/EBITDA** that has just delivered **two consecutive profitable quarters and raised its 2026–2027 outlook** — priced as though it carries full SaaS seat-compression risk despite being embedded in patient registration and payment workflows that AI agents consume rather than replace.

This is the **highest-risk and smallest position** on today's list and should be sized accordingly.

**Rating: Accumulate.** *Speculative.*

## Risk Factors

**1. A disorderly continuation of the global bond rout — SEVERITY: HIGH.** This is the dominant risk and it is not US-specific. The 10-year at 5.33% and 30-year at 5.67% are 24-year highs, the UK 30-year has touched 6% for the first time since 1998, and French and Japanese yields are at multi-decade highs. The drivers named are persistent inflation, **massive government borrowing** and strong growth. Sovereign supply is not a problem that a central bank pause solves. There is no technical structure between current levels and the early-2000s range. Every equity valuation in this report is a discounted cash flow, and the discount rate is moving against all of them simultaneously.

**2. The VIX-16 complacency gap — SEVERITY: HIGH.** Equity volatility at 16.34 alongside a historic global bond repricing is a divergence, not an equilibrium. Either the bond market is overstating the problem or equity markets are underpricing it. The base rate on this configuration favors the bond market. The practical implication is that **equity hedges are currently cheap relative to the realized risk in the system** — this is the rare moment where protection costs little precisely when it is most warranted.

**3. An oil spike into a crowded short — SEVERITY: MEDIUM-HIGH.** Commodity trading advisers hold **91% short positions in Brent and 73% in WTI**, betting on fundamental oversupply. Meanwhile the Strait of Hormuz — ~25% of seaborne oil trade — is back at the center of energy security concerns amid US-Iran tensions, with escalation scenarios putting 2026 Brent at **$95–115** against an $86 base case. A supply disruption into that positioning does not produce an orderly repricing; it produces a short squeeze that feeds directly into breakevens and the long end of the curve. **Risks 1 and 3 are correlated, which is what makes this combination dangerous rather than merely unpleasant.**

**4. The AI capex cycle's financing structure — SEVERITY: MEDIUM.** AI investment at ~$1 trillion globally and ~$581 billion in the US — roughly 10% of US private fixed investment — is now macroeconomically significant. The warning sign is not demand but **financing**: Nvidia scaled back its Ohio data center guarantee to cover only the first phase, while simultaneously organizing six financial institutions to raise $500B+ in third-party capital. The buildout is being shifted onto external credit at the exact moment credit costs are making 24-year highs. If that third-party capital becomes more expensive or more selective, the capex curve bends — and it is currently holding up a meaningful share of index earnings growth.

**5. Peak-cycle risk in memory and semiconductors — SEVERITY: MEDIUM.** Micron beat on every metric, posted an 87% gross margin, raised FY2027 guidance — and the stock fell. After a 284% year-to-date run with HBM sold out for 2026, there is no upside surprise left to harvest, and the explicit market concern is that the memory cycle is nearing a peak. The SOX index has already demonstrated the downside: a 29% drawdown from its June record. A sector that round-tripped once this year can do it again.

**6. Indiscriminate software derating — SEVERITY: MEDIUM.** Forward software P/E has gone from 84.1x to 22.7x, below the S&P's own multiple for the first time ever, on a seat-compression thesis applied uniformly across business models. The risk cuts both ways: the narrative may still have room to run and take genuinely good businesses lower, *or* it may already have created real mispricing. For portfolio purposes, the operative risk is that **nobody currently knows which software businesses are structurally impaired**, and the market is charging all of them the same penalty until it finds out.

## Bottom Line

Today's market is rallying on a 5.33% ten-year, and that is not a sustainable arrangement — one side of that trade is wrong, and the VIX at 16 suggests equities have not yet done the work of figuring out which. The constructive reading is that inflation is genuinely decelerating faster than the Fed's own projections assumed, which would make the long-end move a term-premium and sovereign-supply event rather than an inflation event, and therefore something that resolves without an earnings recession. The honest caveat is that term-premium shocks are harder to forecast than inflation shocks, not easier.

Against that backdrop, today's rotating spotlight lands somewhere genuinely useful. Healthcare is one of the few sectors where the de-rating already happened, the policy overhang has cleared favorably, and leadership is actively rotating from speculative clinical-stage names toward companies with real cash flows — exactly the migration a 5.33% discount rate should force. **Halozyme at a single-digit-to-low-teens multiple on a royalty stream growing 40%+ is the clearest risk-adjusted opportunity on this list**, with Exelixis offering a dated December 3 catalyst at a below-sector multiple. The deep-value and small-cap entries (IART, PHR) are rated Accumulate rather than Buy for a reason: they carry real execution risk, and in a rate shock they will correlate hard.

The position that requires no forecast at all: with the VIX at 16 against the most stressed bond market in 24 years, **portfolio protection has rarely been cheaper relative to the risk it hedges.** If you buy one thing today that is not on the list above, buy that.
