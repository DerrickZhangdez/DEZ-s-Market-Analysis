# Daily Market Report — Friday, September 18, 2026

## Executive Summary

Markets closed out a genuinely difficult week on a split note. Two days after the Federal Reserve delivered its first interest-rate hike since 2023 — a unanimous 12-0 move to a 3.75%–4.00% target range — equities finished Friday with the Dow leading (+0.66%), the S&P 500 barely positive (+0.19%), and the Nasdaq 100 slightly negative (-0.17%). That divergence is the story: beneath flat headline indices, a violent single-name rotation played out, with Intel, AMD, Arm and the memory complex ripping higher on supply-shortage commentary while crowded mega-cap and momentum names bled. Quadruple witching — September 18 is the quarterly expiry of index futures, index options, single-stock options and single-stock futures — amplified the churn and makes today's tape a poor guide to next week's.

The dominant macro fact is that the cost of money is now rising rather than falling, and the dot plot points to one more hike before year-end to a median 4.1%. This inverts the assumption embedded in most 2026 equity models. The second fact is that a retreat in the 10-year Treasury yield, which had touched its highest level since 2007 earlier this month, was what allowed risk assets to stabilize at all this week. Oil remains the transmission mechanism: Brent near $104 on Middle East supply risk is why the Fed is hiking into a decelerating economy, and the one-month correlation between WTI and the 10-year yield has climbed to 0.96.

Today's rotating sector spotlight falls on **Fintech, Payments & Financial Data** — a group that is being mispriced wholesale because the market is treating "fintech" as a single rate-sensitive bucket when the Fed's pivot to tightening is in fact a *tailwind* for a specific subset of it: float-holding payment processors, exchange and market-infrastructure businesses that monetize volatility, and non-discretionary core banking software. That distinction is the basis of today's picks.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

**The hike is real, and the guidance is harder than the hike.** The FOMC raised the federal funds target by 25 basis points to 3.75%–4.00% on Wednesday, September 16 — the first increase in more than three years — on a unanimous 12-0 vote. The statement said plainly that "inflation remains elevated." Chair Kevin Warsh was blunter in the press conference: inflation has been "too high … for too long," and "we must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed."

The projections matter more than the move. The Fed's median core PCE projection for year-end 2026 rose to **3.4%**, up from 3.3% at the June meeting — an upward revision, in a tightening cycle, is an admission that the committee has been behind. The dot plot shows a median terminal rate of **4.1%** for this year, implying one more hike, with four members penciling in two more. Warsh explicitly cited Middle East tension as a contributor to the decision, which is an unusual and important framing: the Fed is telling markets it will respond to an energy-driven supply shock with demand-side tightening rather than looking through it.

**Why the market bounced anyway.** Wednesday's initial reaction was negative — the S&P 500 fell just over 0.4% on the decision. Thursday reversed hard, with the S&P 500 up 1.14% as investors re-thought the FOMC reaction, and Friday held those gains. The proximate cause was the 10-year yield backing off its highest level since 2007. That is a fragile foundation. The equity market is not rallying on improving fundamentals; it is rallying on a bond market that stopped getting worse for forty-eight hours.

**The historical base rate is unfriendly.** Over the last 36 years, the five quarter-point rate hikes the Fed has undertaken were followed by S&P 500 losses one month later **100% of the time**. That is a small sample and should not be traded mechanically, but it argues against treating this week's bounce as an all-clear.

**What to watch next week.** Earnings are a non-factor — there were no noteworthy reports after Thursday's close and none scheduled for Friday, with only nine and one small-cap prints respectively. That leaves the calendar dominated by macro: inflation prints, Fed speakers elaborating on the dot plot, and the path of crude. With the quarterly expiry now behind us, positioning resets and early next week's tape will be a cleaner read on real demand than today's was.

## Geopolitical Factors

**The Middle East is the macro.** The 2026 Middle East conflict — military action involving Iran, disruption risk around the Strait of Hormuz, and attacks on regional energy infrastructure — produced one of the largest oil shocks in recent history before ceasefire negotiations restored partial confidence in supply routes. Brent near $104 embeds a meaningful risk premium rather than a demand signal. U.S. strikes on Iranian targets earlier this month (September 1) are a reminder that de-escalation is not linear.

**The transmission math is unforgiving.** Oil volatility during elevated geopolitical-risk regimes runs roughly **twice** the level of calm periods, and a geopolitically driven 1% decline in oil production pushes prices up by an average of **11.5%**. Spillovers into other commodities run about 50% larger than normal: a 10% geopolitical oil shock lifts natural gas roughly 7% at peak and fertilizer prices over 5%. This is the mechanism by which a regional conflict becomes a Fed decision, and it is why the energy premium is now an equity-duration problem.

**The stagflation tail.** The genuine risk is not a single oil spike but the combination the Fed is now navigating: energy-driven headline inflation, an economy showing signs of deceleration, and a central bank that has committed rhetorically to fighting inflation. Either a Hormuz transit curtailment or further damage to regional production infrastructure would force the Fed to choose between its mandate halves in public. That is the single scenario most likely to break the current equity range.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Fintech, Payments & Financial Data

This is the sector where the Fed's pivot creates the widest gap between narrative and cash flow, and it is where I am concentrating today's capital.

**The lazy trade is to sell all of it.** "Rates up, fintech down" is a 2022 reflex. It was correct then because the fintech complex was dominated by unprofitable consumer lenders funded with cheap capital, and because the sector's valuation was pure duration. Both conditions have changed. Broad screens now put U.S. fintech at roughly **24.6% to 59.2% below fair value** on a discounted basis, and the sector is carrying a de-rating that was earned by a cohort — money-losing neobanks and buy-now-pay-later lenders — that is no longer representative of the whole group.

**The correct trade is to separate the sector into three buckets by rate exposure.**

**Bucket one — rate-hurt: consumer credit and funding-cost-sensitive lenders.** Anything whose unit economics depend on cheap wholesale funding or on consumer credit performance gets worse from here, not better. Rising rates compress origination volume and raise loss-given-default simultaneously. The 2022 analogue is apt for this bucket alone, and the market is right to discount it. I am picking nothing here.

**Bucket two — rate-*helped*: float-holding processors and volatility-monetizing market infrastructure.** This is the mispriced bucket and the source of most of today's picks. Payment processors that hold customer funds in transit earn interest on that float. When the Fed was cutting, float revenue was a *headwind* — Corpay explicitly quantified a **180 basis point drag on Corporate Payments organic growth from float revenue compression** in its most recent quarter. A hiking cycle mechanically reverses that drag into a tailwind, and it does so with essentially no incremental cost, no incremental capital, and no execution risk. The market has not repriced this. Separately, electronic trading venues monetize *volatility and volume*, and a Fed that has surprised the market by hiking, with an upward-revised inflation projection and a contested terminal rate, is a machine for generating fixed-income trading volume.

**Bucket three — rate-indifferent: non-discretionary financial software.** Core banking processing, regulatory compliance and back-office infrastructure are contractual, multi-year, and effectively impossible to defer. Community banks do not stop processing transactions because the funds rate went up 25 basis points. These businesses trade at premium multiples for good reason, and they are where you hide if the historical base rate on post-hike drawdowns asserts itself.

**Where valuation actually sits.** An equally weighted basket of 15 leading financial exchange and data companies now trades at a forward P/E of **23.1x — marginally below the S&P 500, the first time that has been true since the first half of 2012.** Read that again. The market is pricing the toll-booth businesses of the financial system at a discount to the index, at the exact moment that rate volatility is rising and their float income is inflecting. That is the single most compelling structural observation in this report.

### Artificial Intelligence

The AI capital cycle continues to escalate in scale and to change in character — from equity-funded to debt- and vendor-financed, which is the part investors should be watching.

Nvidia is backing approximately **$105 billion in financing** for an OpenAI data center at the PORTS-Pike Technology Campus in Pike County, Ohio, supporting an initial 4.25 gigawatts with an option for 3.75 gigawatts more, built and managed by SB Energy under a 20-year lease to OpenAI. This sits alongside the previously announced Nvidia–OpenAI letter of intent to build **at least 10 gigawatts** of AI data centers with Nvidia committing up to **$100 billion** progressively as systems deploy, with the first gigawatt expected online in the second half of 2026. Talks have also been reported for a backstop facility of up to $250 billion.

Aggregate hyperscaler capital expenditure — Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, Amazon and OpenAI — is estimated at up to **$527 billion this year**, with 2026 and 2027 estimates of **$748 billion and $869 billion**, representing 56% and 16% year-over-year growth. Nvidia, the first $4 trillion company, has described a backlog in the range of **$500 billion**.

**The analytical point:** when the dominant chip supplier is financing its own customers' purchases, the revenue quality of the sector changes. This is vendor financing, and vendor financing historically inflates reported demand late in a capital cycle. It is not evidence of fraud or imminent collapse — the underlying compute demand is real — but it does mean that a rising cost of capital now hits the AI complex through a second channel beyond valuation: the financing structures themselves become more expensive to roll. A Fed hiking to 4.1% is a more serious problem for a debt-financed buildout than for an equity-financed one. That deserves more weight than it is getting.

Counter-pressure is also emerging from within the industry: Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's call to slow AI development was sufficient to rotate capital out of semiconductors and into enterprise software in a single session this week, with the IGV software ETF up 2% while QQQ fell 2%.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

Today was a semiconductor day, and the driver was supply, not demand.

Intel rose sharply — reported between **+7.7% and +9.23%** depending on the measurement window — leading a broad chip rally that included **AMD +6.5%, Arm +8.6%, Micron +5.5%, SanDisk +6.2%, Marvell +4.8%, and Nvidia +2.5%**. The catalysts were specific: Intel's CEO highlighted **severe CPU supply constraints** and warned that the **memory shortage could worsen next year**, while reports surfaced of exploratory talks between **Intel and SK Hynix** — a combination that speaks to both U.S. manufacturing capacity and memory supply security. Citi separately boosted its chip sector outlook. Tigress Financial maintained Intel at Buy and raised its target from $118 to $145.

**The thesis worth holding onto:** the constraint in this cycle has migrated from logic to memory and from chips to power. A genuine multi-year memory shortage is a profit-margin event for memory makers and a cost event for everyone downstream who buys HBM and DRAM. That has asymmetric consequences the market prices in fits and starts — today was one of the fits. Investors should be careful not to read a supply-shortage rally as a demand-strength signal; they are different trades with different endings.

### Software & SaaS

Enterprise software is the most interesting setup in technology right now because the damage is already in the prices.

Salesforce is **down 33% year-to-date**, ServiceNow is **off 45% over the past year**, and Workday is **down 30% year-to-date**. These are not speculative names; they are profitable, cash-generative platform businesses that have been de-rated on the thesis that generative AI will disintermediate seat-based software. This week saw a sharp rebound — ServiceNow +5%, Adobe +4%, with Salesforce ticking up — as the "SaaSpocalypse" narrative drew pushback, including directly from Salesforce's CEO. Oracle gained roughly 8% earlier in September.

The reassessment underway is that AI *needs* robust software systems — systems of record, permissions, workflow and data governance — rather than replacing them. That argument has merit, but it is not yet proven in seat counts or net revenue retention, and one quarter of recovery does not resolve a structural question. I would rather own the financial-infrastructure software in today's spotlight, where the demand is contractual and regulatory rather than discretionary, than pay for an unresolved AI-disruption debate in horizontal SaaS.

### 1. CPAY — Corpay, Inc. *(B2B corporate payments & spend management)*

**Thesis.** Corpay is the cleanest expression of the rate-reversal insight in this report: management quantified a **180 basis point drag on Corporate Payments organic growth from float revenue compression** during the easing cycle, and a hiking Fed mechanically turns that drag into a tailwind at zero incremental cost. You are buying a business compounding cash EPS at 28% for roughly 16x forward earnings, with a new and unpriced margin tailwind attached.

**Valuation Metrics.** Forward P/E approximately **16.1x** (as of mid-August); trailing P/E **19.09x** in Q2 2026, up from 16.83x in Q1. PEG ratio is well below 1.0 against a 28% cash EPS growth guide — the central attraction. EV/EBITDA and P/S not independently verified in this session; sizing should be confirmed against a current data terminal.

**Growth Profile.** Q2 2026 revenue of **$1.248 billion, up 20.7% year-over-year**, beating estimates, with full-quarter revenue growth accelerating to 20.36%. Cash EPS of **$7.00, up 36%** — an all-time company record. Full-year 2026 revenue guidance raised to **$5.31 billion at the midpoint (+17%)** and cash EPS guidance raised to **$27.35 from an initial $26, implying 28% growth**. Corporate Payments organic spend rose **43% to $95 billion**. The Alpha acquisition contributes approximately $300 million of incremental 2026 revenue.

**Competitive Moat.** Corpay's defensibility comes from embedded workflow in corporate accounts-payable and fleet/spend systems, where switching requires re-plumbing a customer's treasury operations. Closed-loop network economics in fuel and fleet, plus a growing cross-border book, create a data advantage in underwriting and interchange that scale competitors cannot easily replicate. The float balance itself is a structural asset that competitors must build transaction by transaction.

**Key Risk.** Serial acquisition is the engine and the hazard — Corpay is actively simplifying its portfolio, which is the right direction but concedes that the complexity was real. An integration stumble on Alpha, or a corporate-spend recession that shrinks organic spend growth from 43% toward flat, would break the EPS algorithm faster than the float tailwind can offset it.

**Rating: Strong Buy.** Growth at a market multiple with an identified, unpriced catalyst is the best risk/reward in today's spotlight.

### 3. MKTX — MarketAxess Holdings, Inc. *(Electronic fixed-income trading & market infrastructure)*

**Thesis.** This is the contrarian position in the report: a high-margin, capital-light electronic bond-trading venue that has been de-rated for five years and has fallen roughly 34% over the past twelve months, precisely as the Fed enters a hiking cycle with a contested terminal rate — the exact macro condition that generates fixed-income trading volume. ## Risk Factors

**1. The Fed is tightening into a decelerating economy — severity: high.** This is the dominant risk and it is structural, not a headline. The committee raised rates 12-0, revised its core PCE projection *up* to 3.4%, and signaled a median terminal rate of 4.1% with four members wanting more. Meanwhile the underlying economy shows "undeniable signs of deceleration." The historical record is stark: over 36 years, all five prior quarter-point hikes were followed by S&P 500 losses one month later, 100% of the time. Positioning for a resumption of the easing-cycle playbook is the single most likely way to lose money over the next quarter.

**2. Middle East escalation and the oil-to-yield transmission — severity: high.** With Brent near $104 and the one-month WTI/10-year yield correlation at **0.96**, oil is currently the most important input to U.S. monetary policy. Geopolitical oil volatility runs at roughly twice normal levels, and a 1% geopolitically driven production loss moves prices 11.5% on average. A Hormuz disruption or renewed infrastructure attacks would push headline inflation higher, force the Fed further, and create the stagflationary scenario in which both equities and bonds fall together.

**3. AI capital-cycle financing quality — severity: medium-high.** Hyperscaler capex estimated at $527 billion this year rising toward $748 billion next is increasingly supported by vendor financing — Nvidia backing $105 billion for an OpenAI Ohio facility, committing up to $100 billion against a 10-gigawatt letter of intent, and reportedly negotiating a backstop of up to $250 billion. Rising rates make these structures materially more expensive to roll. The demand is real; the financing is the fragility. A single failed facility or a delayed gigawatt would reprice the entire AI-adjacent complex, including the power and server names that led today.

**4. Post-expiry positioning reset — severity: medium.** Today was quadruple witching, and the flat-index-plus-violent-single-name-rotation pattern is characteristic of expiry mechanics rather than fundamental repricing. Dealer hedging flows that pinned the tape unwind next week. Do not extrapolate Friday's breadth, and be skeptical of any thesis built on this session's price action.

**5. Valuation compression in high-multiple software and momentum — severity: medium.** Salesforce -33% YTD, ServiceNow -45% over twelve months and Workday -30% YTD show the de-rating is already well underway, and this week's bounce (ServiceNow +5%, Adobe +4%) rests on an unresolved question about whether AI disintermediates seat-based software. A rising discount rate compounds the disruption narrative. Names on high multiples with unanswered structural questions carry two risks priced as one.

## Bottom Line

The market spent this week learning that the cost of money is going up rather than down, and it has not finished repricing that fact — Friday's flat indices concealed a violent rotation into supply-constrained hardware and out of crowded momentum, on an expiry day that should not be extrapolated. The highest-conviction opportunity is not in the AI complex at all but in financial infrastructure, where an equally weighted basket of leading exchange and data companies now trades **below the S&P 500's forward multiple for the first time since 2012**, at the precise moment that rising rates convert float-income headwinds into tailwinds and a contested terminal rate generates trading volume.

Own the fintech that gets *better* when rates rise — float-holding processors, volatility-monetizing venues, contractual core banking software — and avoid the consumer-credit fintech that the market is lazily bundling with it. If the 100%-of-the-time post-hike drawdown statistic asserts itself, that basket is where you want the capital.
