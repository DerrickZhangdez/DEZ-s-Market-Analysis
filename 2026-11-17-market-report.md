## Executive Summary

The market enters Tuesday with the Nasdaq Composite in a formal correction — **10.9% below its October 6 record** — after Monday's 2.49% decline, the third consecutive down session and the worst of the three. The selling was not indiscriminate: it concentrated precisely where leverage to the AI capital cycle is highest, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index off more than 4% after the Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule on or about November 15 requiring licenses for advanced AI chips bound for parts of Southeast Asia and Brazil. AMD fell roughly 4–5%; Broadcom, Micron and Intel followed. That the rule is a licensing regime rather than a blanket ban mattered less than the signal it sent about how much of the demand curve runs through jurisdictions Washington now treats as diversion risk.

Two macro facts frame everything else. First, the Federal Reserve is expected to **raise** rates on December 9 — prediction markets put a quarter-point hike near 89% as of November 16, with the target range currently 3.75%–4.00% after September's first hike in three years — following hot October CPI. Second, oil has broken decisively lower, with WTI around $85 and the IEA now forecasting a fourth-quarter *surplus* of roughly 1.7 million barrels per day, reversing its earlier deficit call. A market that is simultaneously de-rating growth multiples on rising discount rates and getting a disinflationary impulse from energy is a market in which the *composition* of returns matters far more than the direction of the index.

Today's rotating spotlight — **fintech, payments and financial data** — is unusually well positioned for exactly this configuration. A Fed that hikes rather than cuts is a direct earnings tailwind to brokers who hold customer cash, to market makers who monetize volatility, and to processors whose economics are volume- rather than credit-linked. Meanwhile the sector's valuation damage was inflicted in 2025–early 2026 by a stablecoin-disintermediation narrative (an IMF working paper estimated US stablecoin payments legislation cut listed incumbent payment firms' market value by 18%) that has not, so far, shown up in the incumbents' actual revenue lines. That gap between narrative and reported numbers is where today's five positions live.

Our posture: constructive on fee- and float-driven financial infrastructure, cautious on anything requiring cheap credit, a near-term Fed pivot, or a continuation of AI capex at its current trajectory. We take **zero direct exposure to the AI complex** today, by design, ahead of tonight's Nvidia print.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The December 9 hike is now the base case

The single most important repricing of 2026 is nearly complete: the market has accepted that this is a **tightening** cycle.

- **Current target range: 3.75%–4.00%**, reached after the September hike — the Fed's first in three years. The October meeting held rates steady, with **Governor Cook and Cleveland's Hammack dissenting in favor of higher rates** — a dissent pattern that is the mirror image of 2024's.
- **December 9 odds:** Kalshi and Polymarket both put a quarter-point hike near **89%** as of November 16, with a hold at roughly 3%. CME FedWatch readings cited in secondary coverage are above 85%. One report citing a 100% probability is an outlier and should be discounted.
- **A 50bp move is not the base case**, but it is live. Governor Waller has said a half-point hike should be considered if core CPI prints around 0.3% month-over-month or higher.
- **The inflation problem is real, not a forecast artifact.** The Fed's own projections put 2026 headline PCE at **3.7%** — nearly double the target. October CPI came in hot and was the proximate cause of the odds repricing.
- **2027 is not a pivot year in current pricing.** Forbes' reading of market-implied paths has the FOMC continuing moderate tightening through 2027.

### What this does to equity math

A hiking Fed compresses the terminal multiple on long-duration growth assets and simultaneously **raises the earnings power of financial intermediaries that hold customer cash**. This is the cleanest fundamental asymmetry available in the market today, and it is the organizing principle behind three of today's five picks. Interactive Brokers disclosed that a 25bp move in fed funds changes annual net interest income by roughly **$83 million**, with an additional ~$35 million of sensitivity to comparable non-US rate moves. A December hike alone is worth something close to $118 million of annualized pre-tax income at a 78% pre-tax margin business. That is not a narrative; it is a disclosed sensitivity.

The counterweight is equally mechanical: higher rates raise the discount rate on the 2028–2030 cash flows that justify AI infrastructure valuations, at the same time that hyperscaler capital expenditure consumes roughly 93% of operating cash flow (versus 33% in 2023) and Goldman counts $296 billion of hyperscaler debt issuance this year rising toward $440 billion in 2027. The AI trade now needs cheap credit at precisely the moment the Fed is removing it.

### The energy disinflation offset

The most underrated macro development of November is on the supply side of oil, discussed in full below. If Brent holds below $100 and the IEA's Q4 surplus call proves right, the energy contribution to headline CPI flips from a 2026-long headwind to a 2027 tailwind. That is the single most plausible path to the Fed stopping after December — and nothing in current pricing reflects it. It is the main upside risk to this report's defensive posture.

## Geopolitical Factors

### Oil: the risk premium has drained faster than the conflict has resolved

This is the month's most important and least appreciated macro shift.

- **Prices have broken down.** The EIA's November Short-Term Energy Outlook records **Brent falling below $100/bbl on November 4**. Trading Economics reported crude at **$85.06** on November 16, **down 3.95% on the day**. Earlier in the month Brent fell nearly 20% in a single week on de-escalation pricing. Source estimates for Brent over the past week range from roughly $91 to $97 — treat any specific level as approximate.
- **The IEA has flipped its balance.** It now forecasts **105 million bpd of Q4 supply against demand roughly 1.7 million bpd lower** — a surplus call reversing its prior deficit forecast.
- **Why supply recovered:** ship-to-ship transfers past Oman's coast have restored flows around the Hormuz chokepoint.
- **Why demand fell:** the EIA notes consumption dropped materially during the prolonged disruption, **particularly in China**, which blunted the price impact of lost supply. This is the demand-destruction mechanism working as textbooks describe — and it does not reverse instantly when supply returns.

### The conflict itself has not de-escalated

The price move reflects logistics and demand, **not peace**. Tankers remain ablaze in the Gulf, oil spills are washing ashore, and drone and missile strikes on regional infrastructure continue. Freight rates remain elevated and are putting a floor under crude. President Trump has stated the US "no longer needs a deal with Iran," which removes the diplomatic path that part of the November rally had priced.

**The asymmetry is uncomfortable.** Spot prices now embed a near-normalized risk premium while the physical risk is substantially unchanged. A single successful strike on export infrastructure re-prices crude violently upward from $85 — and the market is no longer positioned for it. For equity investors this argues against adding energy exposure here (we picked that theme on November 13 and are not revisiting it) and in favor of recognizing that the disinflation tailwind is contingent and reversible.

### Chip export controls as geopolitics

The BIS interim final rule of roughly November 15 extends licensing requirements for advanced AI chips to parts of **Southeast Asia and Brazil**, aimed at diversion to China through third markets; reporting links it to concerns about Megaspeed International, a Singapore-based firm. It is explicitly **not** a blanket ban. Market reaction was nonetheless severe: SOX −4%+, AMD −4–5%, with Asian suppliers selling off while **SMIC rose 7%** — the clearest possible market verdict on who benefits from forced technological decoupling. Note that the specific country list appears in one outlet but not all; verify against the Federal Register notice before trading on the detail.

### Watch items

- **APEC and G20, November 18–19** — trade and chip policy headlines are likely.
- **Stablecoin regulation is going global.** Brazil's BCB Resolution 561/2026 took effect October 1 and prohibits "stablecoin sandwiching." Thailand's SEC has proposed a five-million-baht daily cap per licensed operator. The UK House of Lords approved the draft Cryptoassets (Miscellaneous Amendments) Regulations 2026 on November 11. South Korea is weighing rules requiring banks to hold 50% plus one share of won-stablecoin issuing consortiums. The direction of travel is toward **bank- and incumbent-favorable** regimes — directly relevant to today's spotlight.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Fintech, Payments & Financial Data

**The thesis in one paragraph.** This sector was de-rated in 2025 and early 2026 on a disintermediation narrative — stablecoins, account-to-account rails, real-time payments — that has an academic imprimatur: an IMF working paper found US legislation supporting stablecoins in payments reduced listed incumbent payment firms' market value by **18%**. What the narrative has not yet done is show up in the incumbents' revenue lines. Meanwhile the macro regime has inverted in the sector's favor: a Fed that hikes pays brokers and float-holders directly, and a market with a VIX near 23 and a Nasdaq in correction pays market makers directly. The result is a group where the multiple reflects a 2029 threat while the earnings reflect a 2026 tailwind.

#### Sub-sector 1: Merchant acquiring — maximum valuation damage, intact cash flow

**Global Payments (GPN)** is the cleanest statement of the thesis. Q3 2026 (reported November 5) delivered adjusted net revenue of **$3.18 billion** and adjusted EPS of **$3.58, up 10%**, with adjusted free cash flow of **$870 million** — roughly **92% conversion** of adjusted net income. Management reaffirmed FY2026 adjusted EPS of **$13.60–13.80** and raised the Worldpay run-rate expense synergy target to **$650 million by 2028** from $600 million, describing integration as ahead of schedule. Net leverage closed at **3.4x** against a target of ~3.0x by end-2027.

Against that, the stock carries a **forward P/E of roughly 6.2–6.3x** — one of the lowest multiples on any profitable large-cap in the US market. Trailing P/E is *negative* (−22x on MarketBeat, with StockAnalysis showing −$1.29B net income) because of Worldpay-related amortization and impairment. **This is the single most important screening trap in the sector today**: every value screen keyed on trailing GAAP earnings mechanically excludes GPN, which is precisely why the forward multiple is 6x.

**Fiserv (FI / FISV) is the cautionary counter-case, and we are deliberately not picking it.** The superficial setup looks similar — P/E of 9–10 after a decline of more than 70% over the past year, Michael Burry publicly arguing for $100–120 buyout value against a recent price near $63, a Q3 beat, and a cost-savings target doubled to $1 billion with a $500 million buyback. But the fundamentals underneath are genuinely impaired: **organic revenue guidance of −1% to flat**, adjusted operating margin collapsing to **31.2% from 37.0%** year over year, **three CEOs since May 2025** (Takis Georgakopoulos is the current one), **two guidance cuts in 2026**, and reported DOJ scrutiny of 2025 earnings guidance plus a securities class action — claims we could only source thinly and which should be verified before acting on either side. Low multiple plus declining organic revenue plus governance turnover is not value; it is a value trap. GPN's revenue grows; Fiserv's does not. That is the whole difference, and it is why one is in the book today and one is in this paragraph.

#### Sub-sector 2: Latin American processing — the growth the US names don't have

**Evertec (EVTC)** posted Q3 2026 revenue **up 24% to $283.0 million** (≈21% constant currency) with adjusted EBITDA **up 19% to $110.5 million** and adjusted EPS **up 16% to $1.07**. The engine is Latin America Payments and Solutions: **revenue +59%, adjusted EBITDA +77%** (≈52% constant-currency revenue growth). Management raised FY2026 guidance to **$1.088–1.096 billion** of revenue (**+16.7% to +17.6%**) with adjusted EPS of **$4.00–4.08**.

Two honest qualifications. Some Q3 Payment Services growth came from a **Puerto Rico vehicle-fine amnesty program that ended in July and will not recur**. And Business Solutions revenue **fell 5% to $58.6 million**, largely on a **10% contractual discount to Banco Popular** — a reminder that Evertec's Puerto Rico franchise rests on a single anchor relationship. The company also closed its **BBChain** acquisition, adding blockchain and tokenization capability — a defensive move against exactly the stablecoin threat de-rating the sector.

The problem is price: a forward P/E in the **low-to-mid 20s** with a consensus "Hold" and average targets clustered roughly **$290–330**, which sit *below* where the stock has been trading. Note also that **VIX options ADV grew only 3%** in Q3 while the VIX sat in a 14–20 range; the franchise's torque arrives only when volatility actually breaks out, as it began to in late October and again Monday. Excellent business, insufficient margin of safety today. We would revisit on a meaningful de-rating.

**StoneX (SNEX)** screens beautifully on growth — fiscal Q2 2026 net operating revenues **+70% to $829.1 million** with **26.5% ROE**, fiscal Q3 **+47% to $719.7 million**, and the R.J. #### Sub-sector 4: Brokerage — the purest expression of the December hike

**Interactive Brokers (IBKR)** grew customer accounts **35% to 5.58 million** in Q3 2026 while net interest income rose **19% to $1.15 billion** and commissions rose **12% to $602 million**. Securities-lending net revenue including cash-collateral interest was an estimated **$426 million, up 36%**. Pre-tax margin held at **78%** — a number almost unmatched in financial services. GAAP EPS was **$0.69** ($0.67 adjusted) against $0.59 a year ago, on $1.91 billion of GAAP net revenue.

The one blemish deserves emphasis because it is the bear case: **net interest margin compressed to 1.99% from 2.16%**, because interest paid on customer cash rose faster than interest earned. Account growth and balance growth are more than offsetting it, but a firm whose NIM falls while rates rise is passing economics through to clients — which is, not coincidentally, why accounts grow 35%.

#### Sub-sector 5: Regulatory and financial data — the uncorrelated leg

**Donnelley Financial Solutions (DFIN)** crossed a genuine threshold this quarter: **software solutions are now 51.5% of total revenue**, with management targeting **60% by 2028** and planning to accelerate the migration of traditional services to SaaS in 2027. Q3 revenue was roughly **$185 million, up 5.5%**. Q2 showed the model's operating leverage clearly — net earnings of $36.4 million ($1.44 diluted), adjusted EBITDA of **$82.3 million at a 36.7% margin, up ~170bp** year over year, with **software revenue +8%** against **print and distribution −15%**. The declining-print drag is the whole reason the multiple is low and the whole reason the mix shift is worth owning.

**Euronet Worldwide (EEFT)** was a serious candidate and did not make the cut. The positives are real — Ria Digital revenue growth of **42%** in Q1, digital accelerator revenue **+31%** in Q2, a **~9.6x trailing P/E**, and consistent buybacks ($50 million and ~690,000 shares in Q3). Cheap, deteriorating, and guiding down. Pass.

#### What we are avoiding in the spotlight sector

**Consumer credit fintech** — Upstart, Pagaya, Oportun and the buy-now-pay-later complex — is the wrong place to be with the Fed hiking into a 3.7% PCE print. Rising funding costs compress the spread while the credit cycle turns against the asset. **Payoneer (PAYO)** illustrates a subtler version of the problem: customer funds of **$7.5 billion** grew just **6%** year over year in Q3, down from 10% in Q2 and 15% in Q1, revenue ex-interest growth decelerated to **5%** in Q3 from 10% in Q2, and interest income is falling ($52.1 million in Q2 versus $58.3 million a year prior) despite management hedging **$4 billion, or 53%**, of held funds plus rate floors on $2.2 billion. A float business whose float stops growing is not a rate play.

### Artificial Intelligence

**Everything today is positioning for one print.** Nvidia reports fiscal Q3 2027 after the close, guiding to **$108 billion ± 2%** of revenue at ~**74% ± 0.5pt** adjusted gross margin, against consensus near **$109 billion** and **$2.47–2.49** adjusted EPS. Data Center revenue was **$89.0 billion, up 117%** in the prior quarter. The November 20 at-the-money straddle implies a **~10.3% move**, with a weekly range near $140–172; the stock fell roughly **12.5% last week**.

The complicating variable has no precedent at this scale: **Jensen Huang resigned on November 12**, and **CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO** while remaining CFO until a permanent successor is named. A founder-CEO transition announced five days before the most-watched earnings report in the market is a governance event as much as a financial one. The question tonight is not whether the quarter is good — guidance and channel evidence suggest it will be — but whether guidance delivered by an interim CEO carries the same weight with investors as guidance delivered by the person who built the franchise.

**The financing layer remains the real fault line.** Hyperscaler capex now consumes roughly 93% of operating cash flow (versus 33% in 2023); CoreWeave's bonds yielded north of 16% on November 6; Goldman counts $296 billion of hyperscaler debt issuance in 2026 rising toward $440 billion in 2027. In a cutting cycle that math is manageable. In a hiking cycle it is not obviously so, and it is the mechanism by which an AI demand story becomes an AI credit story. CNN's Monday coverage explicitly linked the selloff to Nvidia, chip-export restrictions **and** interest-rate concerns — all three at once.

**Our position: no AI exposure today.** Not a view that the technology disappoints, but a view that a 10.3% implied move on a binary event, into a correcting index, with an interim CEO, is not a risk a valuation-based process should take with fresh capital.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

The sector took the week's heaviest damage and for a specific, policy-driven reason.

- **The rule:** BIS interim final rule, issued on or about **November 15**, imposing licensing requirements on advanced AI chips bound for parts of **Southeast Asia and Brazil**, aimed at third-country diversion to China. Explicitly not a blanket ban.
- **The reaction:** **SOX −4%+** on November 16. **AMD −4% to −5%.** Broadcom, Micron and Intel all lower. Asian suppliers sold off, with one Nvidia partner down nearly 10%.
- **The irony:** **SMIC rose 7%.** Every tightening of export controls is a subsidy to the Chinese domestic foundry base. That is now a repeatedly observed pattern, not a one-off.
- **Company framing:** Nvidia's own filings have long warned that evolving export restrictions could limit sales, raise compliance costs, and push customers toward competitors not subject to the same rules. Benzinga's read is that Broadcom's direct exposure is smaller but sector sentiment carries the damage regardless.

**Our read:** export-control risk is now a permanent, non-diversifiable feature of semiconductor equity ownership, and it arrives without warning on regulatory timelines no analyst can model. It should be priced as a structurally higher discount rate on the whole group, not as a series of one-off headlines. We last took semiconductor exposure on November 16 through storage and interconnect, one step removed from the GPU, and that remains our preferred route into the theme.

### Software & SaaS

Enterprise software is caught in the index-level downdraft without the underlying exposure. The relevant question is whether rising rates compress SaaS multiples further. The honest answer is yes at the margin — a hiking cycle mathematically reduces the present value of 2030 cash flows — but the compression has substantially already happened, and it has happened indiscriminately.

The structural point worth making today: **the most defensible software businesses in a hiking cycle are the ones whose customers' spending is mandatory rather than discretionary.** Regulatory compliance software, financial reporting software and risk infrastructure are purchased because a regulator requires them, not because a CFO approved a growth initiative. That is the category DFIN occupies, and it is why a 5.5% grower earns a place in today's book alongside a 24% grower.

Note for context that our November 9 report took a full position in enterprise software (NTNX, CVLT, PEGA, FIVN, DOCU, VERX) and our November 11 report in payments. Neither slate is revisited here; today's spotlight reaches the fintech theme through entirely different sub-sectors — acquiring, Latin American processing, market making, brokerage and regulatory data — rather than through the branded-checkout and payments-software names picked six days ago.

### Portfolio Construction Notes

**Correlation structure.** These five names are not independent bets. **IBKR and VIRT share a market-activity factor** — both earn more when volumes and volatility rise — and will move together in a genuine panic *and* in a genuine melt-up, though in opposite directions from the index in the first case. **GPN and EVTC share a consumer-transaction-volume factor**, and both carry the stablecoin disintermediation overhang. **DFIN is the only position uncorrelated to all of the above**, which is a significant part of why it is in the book despite the weakest growth profile. A reasonable sizing framework weights the two rate/volatility beneficiaries together as a single thematic position rather than two independent ones.

**The deliberate macro bet.** Three of five positions benefit from the December 9 hike occurring. If the Fed holds — and the energy disinflation channel described above is the plausible path to that outcome — IBKR loses its primary catalyst and VIRT likely loses its volatility premium as the market rallies. **GPN, EVTC and DFIN are the hedge within the book**: their theses do not require the hike. Investors who believe the November oil breakdown will stop the Fed should weight toward those three.

## Risk Factors

**1. Nvidia's print tonight — severity: extreme, probability: certain to resolve within 12 hours.**
A ~10.3% implied move on a company of this index weight is, mechanically, roughly 70–100 basis points on the S&P 500 and considerably more on the Nasdaq. The binary is compounded by an **interim CEO delivering her first guidance five days after the founder's resignation**. Guidance of $108B ± 2% against $109B consensus means the headline is likely fine; the risk is entirely in the forward commentary and in whether investors extend the same credibility to Colette Kress that they extended to Jensen Huang. *Our book takes no position on the outcome.*

**2. The Fed hikes into a slowing economy — severity: high, probability: ~89% for the hike itself.**
A December 9 quarter-point move is near-fully priced, so the risk is not the hike but the **path**: markets expect continued moderate tightening through 2027 against 3.7% projected 2026 PCE. Waller's comment that a 50bp move should be considered if core CPI runs 0.3%+ is the tail nobody is positioned for. The mirror risk is that the November oil collapse delivers enough disinflation to stop the cycle, which would invalidate the rate-beneficiary leg of today's book — a loss we would happily take.

**3. AI capex financing stress migrates into credit — severity: high, probability: rising.**
Hyperscaler capex at ~93% of operating cash flow, CoreWeave bonds above 16%, $296B of 2026 hyperscaler debt issuance heading toward $440B in 2027 — all of it underwritten during an easing cycle and now being refinanced into a tightening one. The transmission mechanism from "AI demand is fine" to "AI credit is not fine" does not require demand to disappoint; it requires only that the cost of funding the buildout rises faster than the revenue it generates. Monday's decline hit precisely the data-center-levered cohort.

**4. Export controls escalate further — severity: medium-high, probability: moderate and rising.**
The November 15 BIS rule extended licensing to Southeast Asia and Brazil on diversion concerns. There is no structural reason that list stops expanding, and APEC and G20 meetings on November 18–19 provide an immediate forum for escalation. **SMIC's 7% gain on the news** is the market telling you who wins from each successive round.

**5. The oil risk premium re-prices upward — severity: medium, probability: low but badly underpriced.**
Crude at ~$85 with the IEA forecasting a 1.7 million bpd Q4 surplus reflects restored logistics and Chinese demand destruction, **not resolution**. Tankers are still burning, strikes on regional infrastructure continue, and Trump has stated the US no longer needs an Iran deal. The market has removed the war premium while the war continues. A successful strike on export infrastructure re-prices crude violently upward into a Fed already fighting 3.7% PCE — the single worst macro combination available.

## Bottom Line

The Nasdaq is in correction, the S&P is down 5.1% from its high, and the Russell 2000 still leads the market year-to-date — a configuration that says the market is repricing concentration risk rather than economic risk, and that the correct response is to rotate rather than to de-risk. With the Fed near-certain to hike on December 9 and the VIX near 23, the most defensible place to put new capital is in financial infrastructure that is *paid* by both of those conditions: brokers who earn $83 million of annual net interest income per 25 basis points, market makers running at record trailing earnings on 8.5x, and processors generating 92% free-cash-flow conversion at 6x forward earnings because their trailing GAAP numbers are negative.

We take no AI exposure today, by design, ahead of a binary event with a 10.3% implied move delivered by an interim CEO. That is not a bearish view on artificial intelligence; it is a refusal to express any view at all through fresh capital at this particular hour.

The honest caveat on today's book: three of five positions benefit from the Fed actually hiking, and the November collapse in oil is the most plausible reason it might not. If energy disinflation stops the tightening cycle, GPN, EVTC and DFIN carry the book and the rate leg disappoints. We think that is a risk worth taking at these multiples — but it is a risk, and it is the one we would be watching rather than the one everyone is talking about.
