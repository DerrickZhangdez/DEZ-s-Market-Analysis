## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The Fed pivoted — in the direction almost nobody positioned for

On **September 16, 2026**, the FOMC voted **12-0** to raise the federal funds target range by 25 basis points, from 3.50%–3.75% to **3.75%–4.00%**. This was the **first rate hike since 2023**, and the unanimity of the vote is as significant as the move itself: there was no dissent from the doves, which tells you the inflation data left no room for argument.

The data behind it:

- **Core PCE running near 3.4%** — materially above the 2% target and, critically, no longer falling.
- **Headline inflation revised higher to roughly 3.7%** — the revision matters more than the level, because it suggests the disinflation narrative of 2025 was partly a measurement artifact.
- The updated Summary of Economic Projections showed a **strong majority of officials viewing another hike as possible before year-end**.

The Committee's framing was that it intends to return inflation to target "at sufficient speed" — language that signals the Fed is no longer willing to let above-target inflation run while it waits for lagged effects.

### Why equities are rallying into a hiking cycle

This is the question that should occupy every allocator right now, and there are three defensible answers:

1. **The hike is being read as confirmation of nominal growth, not as a brake.** A Fed raising rates into 3.4% core PCE with equities at records implies an economy absorbing higher rates without cracking. Corporate revenue lines are nominal; sustained 3–4% inflation is not automatically hostile to earnings.

2. **The AI capex cycle is substantially rate-insensitive.** Hyperscaler infrastructure budgets are funded from operating cash flow at companies with net cash balance sheets. A 25bp move does not change a $60bn capex plan. This is the key reason the AI complex has decoupled from the rates complex in 2026.

3. **Falling oil and falling Treasury yields today are doing the easing the Fed will not.** Tuesday's session saw both decline simultaneously — a meaningful loosening of financial conditions that partially offsets the September hike.

The risk in this reasoning is that it is *conditional*. If another hike arrives and long yields rise with it rather than falling, the multiple compression that 2026 has so far avoided becomes very hard to dodge — and it would land hardest on exactly the long-duration software names that have just re-rated upward.

### What to watch this week

- **Treasury yield direction.** The 10-year pausing and then retreating is currently underwriting the equity bid. A renewed climb is the fastest route to a reversal.
- **Oil's path as Hormuz normalizes.** The disinflationary impulse from crude retreating from triple digits is the most plausible route to the Fed standing down.
- **The Trump–Xi capstone meeting.** Preliminary US–China talks concluded with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent characterizing them as "very successful," with specific hopes around AI cooperation. This is a live catalyst in both directions.

## Geopolitical Factors

### The Middle East supply disruption is the defining macro event of H2 2026

The scale here is genuinely historic and deserves to be stated plainly. The closure of the **Strait of Hormuz** and **Bab al-Mandab**, combined with the shutdown of the **East-West Crude Oil Pipeline on September 11, 2026**, disrupted approximately **39% of global trade and 31% of global shipments**. The International Energy Agency has characterized this as the **largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market**.

Price impact and current trajectory:

- Brent averaged **$91/bbl in August**, up $7 from July, as Middle East exports were constrained and regional production was shut in.
- The September escalation pushed crude sharply higher, with Brent trading above $100 and WTI in the mid-$90s.
- **Tuesday brought meaningful relief**: WTI fell roughly 3.5% to around $96.76 and Brent dropped roughly 2.9% to around $100.85, as flows through Hormuz and alternative routes gradually resumed.
- The EIA forecasts **Brent averaging near $90/bbl through H2 2026**, with some export constraints persisting through year-end.

### How to read this for portfolio purposes

The market's current interpretation is that the worst has passed — that the disruption peaked on September 11 and the path from here is gradual normalization. That interpretation is *probably* right and is clearly what a VIX near 15 embeds.

But the risk distribution is asymmetric and investors should be explicit about it. Oil retreating from $101 to $90 is worth perhaps a modest tailwind to margins and a helpful disinflationary nudge. Oil returning to $120 on a re-closure would simultaneously (a) reignite the inflation the Fed is already hiking against, (b) force the "another hike" scenario from possible to certain, and (c) compress equity multiples across the board. The upside from here is incremental; the downside is regime-changing. Positioning should reflect that asymmetry, and it is a significant part of why today's picks favor cash-generative, reasonably-valued businesses over high-multiple momentum.

### US–China: the constructive thread

Running counter to the Middle East risk, US–China negotiations concluded a preliminary round with notably positive characterization ahead of a Trump–Xi capstone meeting, with cooperation on artificial intelligence specifically cited as a hoped-for outcome. This was a direct contributor to Monday's rally. For semiconductor and enterprise software investors with China exposure, this is the single highest-leverage geopolitical variable of Q4.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Enterprise Software & SaaS

*(Rotating spotlight — day-of-year 265, 265 mod 6 = 1 → Enterprise Software & SaaS)*

This is the most consequential sector story of 2026, and today it is inflecting.

#### What actually happened: anatomy of the "SaaS Reckoning"

Between January and February 2026, enterprise software equities underwent a **broad structural repricing that erased roughly $1 trillion in aggregate market capitalization over six weeks**. The trigger was twofold: the launch of Anthropic's Claude Cowork on January 12, 2026, followed by ServiceNow's January 28–29 earnings report.

The bear thesis that drove it was coherent, which is why it did so much damage. It ran as follows: enterprise SaaS is overwhelmingly priced **per seat**. AI agents perform work previously done by seats. Therefore enterprise software revenue is structurally short the very technology it is selling. The vivid formulation circulating at the time — that ten AI agents can do the work of a hundred SDRs — captured the fear precisely. If true, every per-seat vendor faces permanent "seat compression": customers using *more* software while paying for *fewer* licenses.

The supporting evidence was real, not imagined:

- **Seat-based pricing fell from 21% to 15% of vendors in twelve months.**
- Projections suggest **at least 40% of enterprise SaaS spend shifts to usage-, agent-, or outcome-based pricing by 2030**.
- Zendesk had already moved first on outcome pricing back in August 2024, billing per Automated Resolution at $1.50 per committed AR and $2.00 pay-as-you-go — demonstrating the model was viable, not theoretical.
- **Hybrid pricing** (fixed base plus variable consumption) has become the dominant transition state for 2025–2026 renewals.

The critical analytical point, and the one the market got wrong in February, is that **repricing is not elimination**. The revenue *model* is changing; the revenue is not disappearing. A vendor that successfully migrates from per-seat to per-outcome can capture *more* value, because outcome pricing scales with the customer's benefit rather than with their headcount.

#### The reversal now underway

Today's tape is the clearest evidence yet that the February thesis overshot:

| **Salesforce (CRM)** | **+3.6%** | Extending a post-earnings recovery |

The fundamental data underneath the move is what makes it credible rather than a sentiment bounce:

- **Salesforce Q1 FY2027**: revenue **$11.13bn, +13.3% YoY**, with **Agentforce ARR at $1.2bn, up 205% YoY**. A billion-dollar agent business growing triple digits inside the company the bears said agents would destroy.
- **ServiceNow**: AI has crossed **$1bn in annual contract value**, with **agentic deployments up ninefold in nine months**.
- **Snowflake fiscal Q2 2027**: revenue **$1.55bn vs. $1.48bn expected, +35% YoY**; adjusted EPS **$0.62 vs. $0.45 expected**; full-year product revenue guidance raised to **$6.07bn**, explicitly citing AI-driven consumption including its CoCo AI coding agent. Shares jumped roughly **22%**.

Salesforce's CEO has been publicly dismissive of what he called "SaaSpocalypse nonsense," and — on the evidence of Agentforce's ARR trajectory — he has earned the right to be.

#### The dispersion is the opportunity

Here is the part that matters for stock selection. The rebound is emphatically **not** uniform, and the differentiator is not whether a company beat the quarter — in the late-August/early-September cluster, **Zoom, CrowdStrike, Salesforce and Snowflake all beat estimates**. What separated them was *forward guidance and AI attach*:

- **Snowflake**: consumption-priced, AI drives usage, guidance raised → **+22%**.
- **Zoom**: seat-priced, revenue growth of just **4.9%** — slowest of the group by a wide margin → **−7% despite a 12% EPS beat**.

That is the entire investment framework for this sector in one comparison. **A 12% EPS beat could not save a seat-priced vendor growing 5%. A consumption-priced vendor with AI-driven usage got a 22% re-rating.** The market is no longer paying for enterprise software as a category; it is paying for *pricing-model positioning* within the category.

#### Valuation context

Public SaaS multiples have reset to a **median around 8.5x EV/revenue**, with **6–12x forward revenue considered strong for a company growing >25% YoY** in mid-2026. The **Rule of 40** (revenue growth + FCF margin ≥ 40) has become a hard screening line rather than a guideline — companies below it are receiving a material discount. Premium multiples are accruing specifically to businesses with **durable growth, strong cash generation, and defensible AI capabilities**.

For valuation-driven investors, this combination — a sector that de-rated ~$1tn on a thesis that is now visibly overshooting, with a hard quantitative screen (Rule of 40) separating winners from value traps — is close to an ideal hunting ground.

### Artificial Intelligence

Monday's AI rally had an unusual trigger: **a consumer app**. Meta's **Muse AI assistant claimed the #1 free download spot on Apple's App Store**, sending **Meta shares up more than 11%** and pulling the entire AI complex with it.

This deserves more analytical weight than a typical app-store headline, because it addresses the AI bear case most directly. A consumer assistant topping the App Store is evidence of **demand pull at the top of the funnel**, which retroactively justifies infrastructure spend at the bottom. One upcoming event worth noting: Salesforce's Dreamforce keynote will feature Anthropic's Amodei on the same stage where Salesforce pitches AI agents as an *extension* of enterprise software. Given that Claude Cowork's January launch was the proximate trigger for the SaaS Reckoning, the symbolism of that pairing — the disruptor and the incumbent sharing a stage on a partnership framing — is itself a meaningful market signal.

### 1. PAYC — Paycom Software, Inc.
***(Human capital management & payroll SaaS)***

**Thesis.** Paycom has completed the transition from high-multiple growth story to a genuinely cheap compounder — **forward P/E near 18 against 26.9% earnings growth** — while sustaining software-industry-leading profitability that the market is currently ignoring. The bear case (AI compresses employee headcount, and Paycom bills per employee) is real but is already more than reflected in a multiple that has de-rated to roughly the market average for a business with 46% EBITDA margins.

**Valuation Metrics**
- **P/E (trailing):** ~25.4x
- **P/E (forward):** **~18.0x**
- **P/S:** ~6.0x *(est. — ~$12.5bn cap on ~$2.1bn FY26 revenue)*
- **EV/EBITDA:** ~13.5x *(est. — ~46% EBITDA margin; includes $900m drawn revolver)*
- **PEG:** **~1.2** *(18x forward ÷ ~15% forward EPS growth)*
- **Pretax earnings yield:** 8.2%

**Growth Profile.** Q2 2026 revenue **$531.2m, +10% YoY**, with **EPS +48%** on operating leverage and buybacks. Net earnings of **$107.4m**. FY26 guidance raised to **7–8% revenue growth** with adjusted EBITDA margin improving to **46%**. Earnings growth of **26.9%** substantially outpacing revenue growth is the signature of a business harvesting a completed investment cycle.

**Competitive Moat.** Payroll is the stickiest category in enterprise software — switching vendors means risking employees not being paid correctly, so churn is structurally low regardless of competitive pressure. Paycom's **single-database architecture** (one system of record across payroll, HR, time and talent) is a genuine technical differentiator against competitors running stitched-together acquisitions. **Beti**, its employee-driven self-service payroll model, inverts the labor model by pushing verification to employees — which, notably, makes Paycom a *beneficiary* of automation rather than a victim. Profitability confirms the moat: **83.7% gross margin, ~30.3% operating margin, 22.1% net margin, 26.2% ROE**.

**Key Risk.** **Per-employee pricing is the most direct seat-compression exposure in this basket.** If AI agents genuinely reduce enterprise headcount, Paycom's billing base shrinks mechanically — this is the Workday/Zoom risk in concentrated form. Secondary risk: the company **drew $900m on its revolving credit facility to fund buybacks**, adding leverage and financial risk to what was a pristine balance sheet, and reducing flexibility if SMB employment softens. A Fed in hiking mode raises the cost of that decision.

**Rating:** **Buy**

### 2. DOCU — Docusign, Inc.
***(Agreement management & intelligent agreement workflow)***

**Thesis.** Docusign is the clearest "cash-generative survivor repriced too far" in the sector: **forward P/E of ~14** on a business producing **over $1bn in free cash flow at a 33% margin** with **no debt**, while its IAM platform quietly inflects from 2.3% to 10.8% of ARR. The market is valuing this as a melting ice cube and being paid to be wrong.

**Valuation Metrics**
- **P/E (forward):** **~14.1x**
- **P/S:** ~4.1x *(est. — ~$13bn cap on $3.2bn revenue)*
- **EV/EBITDA:** ~31.4x *(GAAP-basis, materially depressed by stock-based compensation — the FCF multiple near 13x is the more honest lens for this business)*
- **PEG:** ~1.4
- **Price:** ~$67.05 *(+62% off its 52-week low)*

**Growth Profile.** FY26 revenue **$3.2bn, +8% YoY**; subscription revenue **+9%**. Q3 revenue **$818m (+8%)** with **billings $829m (+10%)** — billings outpacing revenue is a forward-looking positive. **First year ever exceeding $1bn in free cash flow, at a 33% margin** (up from 31%), with Q4 FCF of **$350m at a 42% margin, +25% YoY**.

The growth engine is **IAM (Intelligent Agreement Management)**: ARR rose from **2.3% to 10.8% of total**, generating **over $350m ARR in just 18 months**, with guidance targeting **over $600m ARR and 18% of total** next year. Note carefully what IAM *is* — it monetizes the agreement lifecycle (obligations, renewals, analytics) rather than the signature event. **That is a deliberate migration away from per-envelope/per-seat toward workflow value**, which is exactly the transition the SaaS Reckoning rewarded Snowflake for and punished Zoom for failing to make.

**Competitive Moat.** Docusign is the default verb in electronic agreement, with network effects compounding every time a signer becomes a sender. The **audit trail and legal defensibility** of its envelope architecture is the real lock-in — enterprises in regulated industries cannot casually swap a system holding years of legally-binding execution records. Capital discipline is excellent: **$215m repurchased in Q3 (largest quarterly buyback ever)**, roughly **$1bn cash, no debt**, and a **$2.6bn remaining authorization** — meaningful against a ~$13bn cap.

**Key Risk.** **The core e-signature business is mature and growing only 8%**, which means IAM must carry the entire growth story — and IAM is still under 11% of ARR. If IAM stalls short of the $600m target, this becomes a low-growth cash cow that deserves 14x. Competitive pressure is real: **Adobe and Microsoft can bundle e-signature into suites customers already buy**, and AI-native contract-intelligence startups attack the high-value analytics layer that IAM depends on for its premium.

**Rating:** **Buy**

**Thesis.** Elastic is the rare enterprise software company whose core asset — a search and vector-retrieval engine — becomes *more* valuable as AI proliferates, because every retrieval-augmented generation deployment needs exactly what Elasticsearch does. After years of inconsistent execution, FY27 opened with a decisive beat and a raise, yet the stock trades near **27x forward earnings** with a **21.5% free cash flow margin**.

**Valuation Metrics**
- **P/E (forward):** **~27x** *(~$89.71 ÷ $3.29–3.37 FY27 EPS guidance)*
- **P/S:** ~4.7x *(est. — ~$9.4bn cap on ~$2.0bn FY27 revenue guidance)*
- **EV/EBITDA:** ~30x *(est. — non-GAAP operating margin ~14.8%, improving)*
- **PEG:** ~1.6
- **Adjusted FCF margin:** **~21.5%** *(TTM operating cash flow $326.9m on just $5.1m capex → $321.8m FCF)*
- **Rule of 40:** ~15% growth + ~21.5% FCF margin ≈ **36** — below 40, but converging

**Growth Profile.** FY26 revenue **$1.739bn, +17% YoY**. Q1 FY27 delivered revenue **$478m (+15%)** and adjusted EPS of **$0.70 against $0.58 consensus** — a 21% earnings beat — with **sales-led subscription revenue growing 19%**, comfortably ahead of the total. Management **raised full-year guidance to ~$2.0bn revenue and $3.29–3.37 EPS**. One widely-followed DCF puts intrinsic value near **$140/share against ~$83–90 trading**, implying a ~40% discount; treat that as a directional signal rather than a target.

**Competitive Moat.** Elasticsearch is the **de facto standard for enterprise search and log analytics**, with an open-source-derived adoption model that embedded it in developer workflows years before any procurement conversation — the cheapest and most durable distribution advantage in infrastructure software. The strategic upgrade is **vector search**: as enterprises build RAG pipelines, Elastic is positioned as the retrieval layer, monetizing AI workloads through consumption rather than seats. **Collaborations with OpenAI and AWS** validate that positioning.

**Key Risk.** **Elastic fights on three fronts simultaneously** — Splunk and Datadog in observability, Microsoft and CrowdStrike in SIEM/security, and purpose-built vector databases such as Pinecone in AI retrieval. It is the clear leader in none of the three. Consumption-based revenue also cuts both ways: it captures AI upside but introduces quarter-to-quarter volatility that a subscription book does not have. Wall Street remains genuinely split on whether this execution is durable or another "head-fake" after several false starts.

**Sub-sector adjacency disclosure.** Elastic's observability segment **overlaps materially with Dynatrace (DT), picked September 10** and inside the one-month look-back. We are including ESTC because the thesis rests on the **search/vector-retrieval layer**, not the observability business, and the two companies compete only partially. Investors already holding DT should size ESTC with that overlap explicitly in mind rather than treating it as an independent position.

**Rating:** **Buy**

- **P/E (forward):** **~21.1x**

Consensus forecasts **revenue +7.8% annually and earnings +14% annually over three years**. The earnings-over-revenue growth spread is the entire thesis: this is a margin-expansion story, not a top-line story.

**Competitive Moat.** **Public-sector switching costs are the highest in all of enterprise software.** Replacing a county court case-management system or a municipal public-safety dispatch platform requires a multi-year procurement, statutory compliance validation, data migration spanning decades of legal records, and retraining of personnel who cannot suspend operations during the transition. Tyler holds dominant share across numerous county and municipal categories, and its customers are **tax-funded rather than cyclically-funded** — an underrated defensive characteristic with the Fed hiking into 3.4% core inflation. Critically for 2026, **none of this is seat-compression exposed**: a courthouse needs its case management system regardless of how many AI agents its clerks use.

**Key Risk.** **Growth has decelerated sharply — from a ~15% five-year average to roughly 8%** — and the market is entitled to ask whether the cloud transition is masking genuine share maturity in core markets. Municipal budget pressure is a live concern if higher rates strain local finances. And at **21x forward** the stock is *reasonable*, not *cheap* — the average forward P/E in US software is around 15x, so Tyler still carries a quality premium that a further growth disappointment would compress.

**Sub-sector adjacency disclosure.** Tyler's government revenue-administration products have **limited overlap with Vertex (VERX), picked September 16** on an indirect-tax-compliance thesis. The overlap is minor — Vertex sells corporate transaction-tax compliance, Tyler sells government-side administration — but it is disclosed for completeness.

**Rating:** **Accumulate**

### 5. OTEX — Open Text Corporation
***(Enterprise information management & business networks — deep value)***

**Thesis.** This is the deliberate deep-value sleeve of the basket: **a P/E near 12 on roughly 1.1x sales with a 4.5% dividend yield**, attached to an installed base of regulated-industry content and records that is extraordinarily difficult to displace. Nobody will call OpenText a growth story, and that is precisely why it is priced where it is. It is included because a valuation-driven report should own at least one position where the return comes from cash yield and multiple normalization rather than from growth.

**Valuation Metrics**
- **P/E (trailing):** **~11.9x**
- **P/E (normalized):** **~5.2x**
- **P/S:** **~1.10x**
- **Forward dividend yield:** **4.51%** *($0.28/quarter, paid September 18, 2026)*
- **3-year dividend growth rate:** **16.65%**
- **Consensus:** Hold, with a C$27.00 price target

**Growth Profile.** This is the honest weak point. Q4 total revenue **$1.35bn, +2.9% YoY**, with **cloud revenue +6.0%** and enterprise cloud bookings described as strong; GAAP net income surged with non-GAAP metrics improving. So: **low-single-digit total growth, mid-single-digit cloud growth, improving profitability, and a rapidly-growing dividend**. The investment case rests entirely on cash returns and the gap between a ~12x multiple and a ~5x normalized multiple — not on acceleration.

**Competitive Moat.** OpenText's defensibility is **archival inertia**. Enterprises in regulated industries store decades of content, records and compliance documentation in its systems; migrating that data creates legal discovery risk that most general counsels will refuse to authorize for a marginal cost saving. Its business network (EDI) franchise carries additional lock-in, since the switching decision involves every trading partner simultaneously, not just the customer. The **16.65% three-year dividend growth rate** against low-single-digit revenue growth confirms genuine free cash flow conversion.

**Key Risk.** **Value-trap risk is the honest characterization, and it is substantial.** A business growing 2.9% can stay at 12x indefinitely — cheap is not a catalyst. Two specific concerns: the company carries **meaningful leverage from the Micro Focus acquisition**, which is a materially worse position with the Fed hiking than it was in a zero-rate world; and unstructured content management is **plausibly among the categories most exposed to AI commoditization**, since large language models are extremely good at retrieving and summarizing document repositories. That is the bear case that keeps the multiple at 12x, and it is not unreasonable. Position accordingly — this is the smallest-conviction name in the basket, sized for yield rather than appreciation.

**Sub-sector adjacency disclosure.** OpenText's Carbonite data-protection line **overlaps partially with Commvault (CVLT), picked September 10**. The overlap is a minor segment of OpenText's revenue rather than its core ECM/business-network franchise, but investors holding CVLT should note it.

**Rating:** **Accumulate**

## Risk Factors

**1. A second Fed hike into a market priced for perfection — SEVERE**
The Fed hiked 25bp on September 16 with a unanimous 12-0 vote, core PCE near 3.4%, headline revised to 3.7%, and a majority of officials signaling another hike is possible before year-end. Equities are at record highs with the VIX near 14.9. The market is currently reconciling these facts by assuming the hiking cycle ends here. If it does not — and particularly if long yields rise alongside the next hike rather than falling as they did this week — the multiple compression that 2026 has so far avoided arrives all at once. **Long-duration software, including four of today's five picks, is the most exposed cohort.**

**2. Re-escalation of the Middle East supply disruption — SEVERE**
The September 11 closure of Hormuz, Bab al-Mandab and the East-West pipeline disrupted 39% of global trade and produced what the IEA calls the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market. The market has priced gradual normalization, and Tuesday's 3% crude decline reflects that. A re-closure would push oil back above $120, make the next Fed hike certain rather than possible, and compress equity multiples simultaneously with margin pressure. **The asymmetry is the point: further normalization is worth a few percent; re-escalation is a regime change.**

**3. AI concentration and the parabolic semiconductor move — HIGH**
AMD is up 29% in a month with 21 points of that in five sessions, the SOX has risen five consecutive days, and the proximate catalyst for the latest leg was a consumer app's App Store ranking. The AI monetization data underneath is genuine — Agentforce at $1.2bn ARR, ServiceNow past $1bn AI ACV, Snowflake's raise — but price action of this velocity typically resolves violently when the narrative is questioned. With four chipmakers now above $1trn, index-level concentration means a semiconductor de-rating is no longer a sector event.

**4. The SaaS repricing thesis reasserting itself — MODERATE-TO-HIGH**
Today's rally is a *rebuttal* of the February thesis, not a *refutation*. Seat-based pricing genuinely is declining from 21% to 15% of vendors, 40% of spend genuinely is projected to shift to usage/outcome models by 2030, and Zoom's −7% response to a 12% EPS beat shows the market will still punish seat-priced vendors instantly. **Paycom is directly exposed to this risk, and it is disclosed as the key risk in that name.** A single disappointing seat-based guide from a major HCM vendor could re-open the February trade across the sector.

**5. US–China negotiation breakdown ahead of the Trump–Xi meeting — MODERATE**
Preliminary talks were characterized as "very successful" by Treasury Secretary Bessent, with AI cooperation specifically cited, and this materially contributed to Monday's rally. That optimism is now embedded in prices. A breakdown at the capstone meeting would hit semiconductors hardest and would reintroduce export-control risk to the AI supply chain just as valuations have reached record levels.

## Bottom Line

The market is paying record prices for equities while the Fed hikes into 3.4% core inflation and a third of global shipping sits disrupted — a combination that makes a VIX near 15 look less like calm and more like complacency. We are not fighting the tape, but we are choosing where to stand on it: today's picks deliberately favor cash-generative enterprise software at 12–27x forward earnings over semiconductors that have risen 29% in a month, because the AI monetization evidence is now strong enough to support software multiples while the chip complex has already recognized most of its good news.

The most actionable insight from today's spotlight is that **pricing model, not earnings beats, now determines enterprise software returns** — Zoom beat EPS by 12% and fell 7%, while Snowflake's consumption-driven raise earned a 22% re-rating. Build the software sleeve around vendors migrating toward usage and outcome pricing (DOCU, ESTC) or structurally insulated from seat compression entirely (TYL), size the per-seat exposure (PAYC) deliberately rather than accidentally, and treat cheap-but-shrinking (OTEX) as a yield position rather than a growth one.
