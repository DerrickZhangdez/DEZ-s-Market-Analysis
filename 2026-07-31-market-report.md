# Daily Market Report — Friday, July 31, 2026

## Executive Summary

Markets are closing out July on a strong note as Big Tech earnings resolved the month's central anxiety — whether the AI capex cycle was rolling over — decisively in favor of "no." Amazon's blowout AWS quarter (+37% YoY cloud growth, the fastest since 2021) sent the stock up more than 11% and reignited the AI infrastructure trade, while Apple slid roughly 7% on soft services and supply-constrained guidance, a clean illustration that investors are no longer buying "Big Tech" as a bloc but sorting winners from laggards inside it. The tension beneath the rally is unchanged and worth naming plainly: equities are pricing an AI capex supercycle at the same time the rates market is pricing two Fed *hikes* in 2026, and those two things cannot both stay comfortable indefinitely.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

**The Fed held, but not quietly.** The FOMC voted **9–3 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50%–3.75%**, the fifth consecutive hold. The three dissents — Hammack (Cleveland), Kashkari (Minneapolis), and Logan (Dallas) — all pushed for tighter policy, reflecting genuine institutional frustration that inflation has now run above the 2% target for more than five years. A three-vote hawkish dissent is not noise; it is a signal that the committee's tolerance for "elevated but stable" inflation is fraying.

**Inflation:** June core PCE came in at **3.3% YoY** (+0.1% MoM), down from 3.4% in May. Headline PCE actually *fell* 0.1% for the month — the first monthly decline in six years — putting the annual headline rate at 3.7%. But the composition matters enormously here: the improvement was almost entirely energy, with energy goods and services down 5.9% and gasoline down 9.2% on the temporary Middle East de-escalation. Housing inflation moderated to +0.2%, goods fell 0.6%, and services rose just 0.1%. Core PCE has now printed at or above 3.3% for four straight months — the longest such stretch since fall 2023. Strip out the oil gift and the underlying trend is stubbornly sticky.

**Growth:** Q2 GDP slowed to a **1.5%** annualized rate. That is the uncomfortable part of the picture — decelerating growth alongside 3.3% core inflation is a mild stagflationary tilt, and it explains why the Fed is paralyzed rather than merely patient.

**Rates market:** Markets are now pricing **two 25bp hikes in 2026** with no further movement through 2027. Fed officials' own year-end projections sit between 3.6% and 4.1%. This is the single most underappreciated risk in the current tape: equity multiples are expanding on AI enthusiasm while the forward curve says the cost of capital is going *up*, not down.

**What to watch next week:** July employment report, ISM manufacturing and services, and the second wave of AI-infrastructure earnings from the supply chain (as opposed to the hyperscalers who buy from it).

## Geopolitical Factors

**Oil has decoupled from the risk narrative.** Brent has retreated to roughly **$72/bbl** and WTI slipped below **$69** — the lowest levels since late winter — driven by a rapid repricing of geopolitical risk premium following constructive US–Iran talks in Qatar and a subsequent MOU. The IEA is projecting a potential **3.7–4.0 million bbl/day oversupply**, which is a genuinely bearish structural overhang independent of politics.

**The Strait of Hormuz remains the tail risk that matters.** Roughly 20% of global oil flows and nearly 20% of global LNG trade transit the strait. A prolonged disruption is the cleanest path to Brent above $100, which would simultaneously delay monetary easing across major central banks and detonate the current equity multiple. Consensus forecasts have Brent averaging $87/bbl in 2026 and $82/bbl in 2027 — notably *above* spot, implying the sell-side still carries a meaningful re-escalation premium that the spot market has discarded.

**Trade-route stability and energy security** are now shaping the global outlook more than traditional growth data. For equity investors, the practical implication is that the disinflation currently flattering the PCE print is borrowed from diplomacy, not earned by policy — and it can be repossessed quickly.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: AI Infrastructure & Data Centers

**The setup.** Today's spotlight bucket is the physical layer beneath the AI trade — power, cooling, networking, interconnect, and memory. This is the sector where the July earnings season delivered its clearest verdict, and it is where I am concentrating today's picks.

**The capex number is the whole thesis.** Combined 2026 hyperscaler capex now stands at roughly **$725 billion**, up approximately 77% from 2025:

| Hyperscaler | 2026 Capex Guidance |
|---|---|
| Amazon | ~$200B |
| Microsoft | ~$190B |
| Alphabet | $175–185B (guided ~$200B for next 12 months) |
| Meta | $136.7B (raised >$10B) |

Nearly all of this is AI infrastructure: GPU clusters, custom silicon (TPU, Trainium, MTIA, MAIA), and physical data center construction. Alphabet alone reported **$44 billion of capex in a single quarter**, bringing trailing-twelve-month spend to $132 billion — roughly double the year-ago level.

**Power is the gating constraint, not chips.** This is the most important structural insight in the sector right now and it is still under-appreciated by generalist investors. You can order GPUs; you cannot order an interconnection queue slot. The binding constraint on 2026–2028 AI buildout has migrated from silicon supply to megawatts, substations, transformers, and the skilled electrical labor required to install them. That migration is precisely why the highest-conviction risk-adjusted opportunities have moved *down* the stack from the semiconductor layer into power generation and electrical construction.

**Cooling is undergoing a technology regime change.** GPU rack densities above 100kW have made air cooling physically inadequate. The forced migration to liquid cooling is not a preference cycle — it is thermodynamics — and it is re-rating the entire thermal management supply chain. Modine's Data Centers segment posted **$348.6 million in sales versus $183.7 million a year ago (+90%)**, and management is guiding to double data center revenue from ~$1 billion in FY26 to **$2 billion by FY28**. SPX Technologies raised its 2026 data center growth outlook from ~50% to **~70%**, lifting its data center revenue target from $200 million to $350 million, with that business projected to grow from ~13% of sales in 2026 to over 30% within four years.

**Optical interconnect has become the second bottleneck.** As GPU clusters scale to hundreds of thousands of accelerators, copper runs out of headroom and fiber takes over. Global shipments of 800G-and-above transceivers are projected to leap from **24 million units in 2025 to nearly 63 million in 2026**, with the 800G→1.6T transition ramping in volume through the year. The equity performance here has been genuinely parabolic: Applied Optoelectronics +441% YTD, Ciena +600% over one year, Lumentum +166% YTD, Coherent +97% YTD. Coherent's Datacenter & Communications segment now represents 75% of total revenue, growing over 40% YoY. Ciena raised FY26 guidance to $6.3 billion (~32% growth) and expects **over $5 billion in AI orders** with more than $3 billion of AI infrastructure revenue recognized this fiscal year.

**A necessary discipline note on the optical group.** Names that have appreciated 400–600% in twelve months are momentum vehicles, not valuation opportunities, regardless of how sound the underlying demand story is. Ciena at 48–65x forward and Seagate at 31–35x forward after a 685% one-year run are priced for uninterrupted execution. I am deliberately taking my interconnect exposure through the *manufacturing* layer rather than the branded-product layer, where the multiple is more defensible.

**Memory is the cheapest real asset in the AI complex.** Micron is in a confirmed supercycle — Q1 revenue of $13.64B (+57% YoY), Q2 guided to $18.7B at 68% gross margins, full-year consensus around $76B revenue and ~$34 EPS — with HBM4 feeding NVIDIA's most advanced platforms and capacity **fully booked under binding contracts**. It trades at a high-single-digit forward P/E. The SK Hynix limit-up move in Korea overnight is the market beginning to notice this dislocation.

**The valuation argument for the whole bucket.** AI infrastructure names generally trade at 18–35x forward earnings versus NVIDIA's ~47x, with equivalent exposure to the same capex wave. The sector remains under-owned relative to AI silicon — less crowded, less hyped, similar tailwind. That is the definition of a favorable risk-adjusted setup.

**The honest bear case.** Every thesis in this sector is a derivative of hyperscaler capex, which is discretionary, announced in advance, and revisable. Concentration risk is severe — Comfort Systems' technology customers went from 43% to 58.7% of revenue in a single year. If any two of the four major hyperscalers trim 2027 capex guidance, the entire complex de-rates simultaneously and correlation goes to one. Meta's stock already dropped this week on disappointing guidance and dwindling free cash flow, a reminder that the capex payers are themselves being scrutinized on returns. Position accordingly.

### Artificial Intelligence

The dominant AI story today is **differentiation within the AI trade rather than the trade itself**. Amazon's 37% AWS growth — beating the 31% consensus and marking the fastest expansion since 2021 — validated that AI demand is converting into realized cloud revenue, not just capex. Apple's 7% decline despite iPhone unit revenue surging 22% is the counterpoint: the market punished weak services and supply-constrained guidance (9–11% revenue growth versus 12% expected), and more fundamentally penalized Apple's comparatively conservative AI posture. The message from this earnings season is that investors are now willing to reward aggressive AI capital deployment and penalize perceived caution — a remarkable inversion of how markets treated capex just eighteen months ago.

Meta's guidance stumble deserves attention as the first real crack in that pattern: capex up over $10 billion to $136.7 billion, paired with a weak revenue forecast and shrinking free cash flow. That combination — spending more, earning less on it — is the specific failure mode that would end this cycle.

### Technology (Broad)

Cloud is where the AI narrative is being adjudicated, and it is passing. AWS at 37% growth, Alphabet's capex doubling year-over-year, and Microsoft's continued ramp all point to genuine capacity constraints rather than speculative building. The broad software complex has been a relative laggard as capital rotates toward companies with tangible AI revenue attribution. The Russell 2000's +1.37% versus Nasdaq's +2.80% on Thursday captures the persistent narrowness of this market — breadth remains the tape's structural weakness, and it is why index-level valuation statistics are increasingly uninformative about the median stock.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

Semiconductors led Thursday's rebound alongside Microsoft. The memory sub-sector is the standout: SK Hynix limit-up in Seoul, the KOSPI's 18% surge, and Micron's fully-booked HBM4 capacity all confirm an acute shortage in high-bandwidth memory that is now the binding input constraint on AI accelerator production. Storage has followed — Seagate's capacity is spoken for through 2027 with nearline HDD demand from hyperscalers, and the stock is up 245% YTD. On the equipment and packaging side, advanced packaging and optical assembly capacity remain tight, which is the structural reason Fabrinet's fiscal Q3 guidance implied roughly 35% YoY growth.

### Software & SaaS

Enterprise software remains the quieter corner of the tape, with capital continuing to favor infrastructure over applications. Friday's analyst activity was broad but unspectacular — rating changes across Apple, Roblox, Ford, Baxter, Boston Scientific, International Paper, GoDaddy, MarketAxess and others. One notable downgrade: Barclays' Nicholas Campanella cut **Edison International (EIX)** from Overweight to Equal-Weight, trimming the target from $78 to $75 — worth flagging because utility downgrades sit adjacent to the AI power thesis and are a reminder that not every electron-adjacent name benefits equally from data center load growth.

### 1. MOD — Modine Manufacturing Company

**Sub-sector:** Data center thermal management / cooling

**Thesis:** Modine is transforming from a legacy auto thermal supplier into a pure-play data center cooling company, and the market is still applying a partial industrial multiple to a business whose data center segment just grew 90% year-over-year. The Gentherm Reverse Morris Trust spin-off of Performance Technologies by year-end 2026 removes the cyclical automotive drag and forces a re-rating that has not yet fully occurred.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- P/E (trailing): ~138x — distorted by spin-off charges and legacy segment losses; not the operative number
- P/E (forward): **~24x** — the number that matters, and remarkably modest for the growth profile
- P/S: ~3.5x (est.)
- EV/EBITDA: ~18x (est.)
- PEG: ~0.7–0.9 (est., against data-center-led earnings growth)

**Growth Profile:** Data Centers segment revenue $348.6M versus $183.7M a year ago (**+90% YoY**). Management guiding data center revenue from ~$1B in FY26 to **~$2B by FY28** — a doubling in two years. Fair value estimates have been revised up from ~$266 to ~$330 as the multiple input moved from 20.7x to 24.8x.

**Competitive Moat:** Airedale by Modine carries more than two decades of concurrent free-cooling engineering expertise, positioning it for the industry's shift toward higher-temperature, higher-heat-density thermal architectures. The decisive moat evidence is the **Long Term Capacity Agreement signed May 26, 2026** to supply **over $4 billion** of Airedale data center cooling products during calendar 2027–2029, accompanied by a **$165 million upfront payment** to fund capacity investment. A customer prepaying $165 million to reserve capacity three years forward is the strongest possible signal of supply scarcity and switching costs.

**Key Risk:** Extreme customer concentration — the $4B LTCA is with a single strategic data center customer. Execution risk on the Gentherm spin-off is real, and any hyperscaler capex deceleration hits the order book directly. A capacity agreement is a reservation, not a guarantee of full drawdown.

**Rating: Strong Buy**

### 2. MU — Micron Technology, Inc.

**Sub-sector:** Memory & HBM semiconductors

**Thesis:** Micron is the single largest valuation dislocation in the entire AI complex — a company whose HBM4 capacity is fully booked under binding contracts, growing revenue 57% with 68% gross margins, trading at a high-single-digit forward P/E. Overnight's 18% KOSPI surge and SK Hynix limit-up move is the market starting to reprice global memory scarcity, and Micron is the US-listed vehicle for it.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- P/E (trailing): **16.7x** (as of July 29, 2026)
- P/E (forward): **~8.6x** — and below 6x on FY2027 (ending August 2027) estimates
- P/S: ~15x on trailing, but roughly 4x on forward FY26 revenue of ~$76B (est.)
- EV/EBITDA: ~7x forward (est.)
- PEG: **well below 0.5** — earnings expected to roughly double over the next year against a single-digit forward multiple

**Growth Profile:** Q1 FY26 record revenue of **$13.64B (+57% YoY)**. Q2 guided to **$18.7B with 68% gross margins**. Full-year FY26 consensus approximately **$76B revenue and ~$34 EPS**. Earnings expected to roughly double over the next twelve months. Net cash balance sheet.

**Competitive Moat:** HBM is a three-player oligopoly (Micron, SK Hynix, Samsung) with brutal capital intensity and multi-year technology qualification cycles that make new entry effectively impossible on any relevant timeframe. HBM4 chips are qualified into NVIDIA's most advanced AI platforms — a design win that is extremely sticky once locked. Capacity fully booked under binding contracts through year-end is the moat expressed as a cash flow certainty.

**Key Risk:** Memory is historically the most violently cyclical business in semiconductors, and the low multiple is the market pricing a coming downcycle rather than an oversight. The stock is up ~200% in 2026 at a ~$1.1 trillion market cap, so the easy money is gone. If HBM supply catches demand in 2027 — and capital is flooding in to make exactly that happen — pricing normalizes hard and the "cheap" multiple reveals itself as correctly discounting peak earnings. Size this position with that asymmetry in mind.

**Rating: Buy**

### 3. EME — EMCOR Group, Inc.

**Sub-sector:** Electrical & mechanical construction / data center buildout services

**Thesis:** If power and electrical installation is the true constraint on AI buildout rather than chips, EMCOR is the most reasonably-valued way to own that constraint — a record $15.6 billion backlog growing 33% year-over-year, trading at roughly 25x trailing earnings against a construction industry averaging near 40x and a peer group near 51x. This is the rare AI infrastructure name where the valuation gap runs in the investor's favor rather than against.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- P/E (trailing): **~24.9x** — versus Construction industry ~39.8x and peer group ~51.0x
- P/E (forward): **~29.0x**
- P/S: ~1.4x (est., on 2026 revenue guidance)
- EV/EBITDA: ~14x (est.)
- PEG: ~1.8–2.1 (est.)

**Growth Profile:** Q1 2026 revenue **+19.7% to $4.63 billion**. Record backlog of **$15.62 billion, up 32.9% YoY**. Raised 2026 guidance: revenue **$18.50–19.25 billion**, diluted EPS **$28.25–29.75**. Longer-term forecasts call for 13.9% earnings and 9.1% revenue growth per annum — deliberately conservative relative to current backlog conversion.

**Competitive Moat:** Skilled electrical and mechanical labor at national scale is the genuine scarce asset in data center construction, and it cannot be conjured by capital alone. EMCOR's US Electrical Construction segment is expanding through the Miller Electric acquisition and geographic penetration into Texas, Arizona and the Southeast — precisely where hyperscale buildout is concentrated. Multi-year backlog with pre-negotiated terms provides revenue visibility that the equipment vendors upstream lack. Screens as undervalued in 6 of 6 standard valuation tests, with DCF analysis suggesting shares trade at roughly a **43.5% discount to intrinsic value** — though other analysts view it as fairly valued, so treat that figure as one input rather than a conclusion.

**Key Risk:** Margin sustainability is the live analytical question. Backlog growth of 33% is meaningless if it was won on thin pricing, and specialty contractors have a long history of converting record backlogs into disappointing margins when labor costs inflate faster than contract escalators. Growing data center concentration also erodes the diversification that historically justified EMCOR's defensive characteristics.

**Rating: Strong Buy**

### 4. VST — Vistra Corp.

**Sub-sector:** Independent power generation / AI electricity supply

**Thesis:** Power is the binding constraint on the AI buildout, and Vistra is the value entry point into that constraint — roughly 9–13x EV/EBITDA for a business with 3,800MW of nuclear capacity already contracted to AWS and a seat at the table in Meta's multi-gigawatt clean energy package. The multiple compressed meaningfully into Q1 2026 even as the demand narrative strengthened.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- EV/EBITDA: **9.33x** as of Q1 2026, down from 12.81x in Q4 2025; other analyses cite ~13x — call it **9–13x** depending on EBITDA basis, and materially below the AI-adjacent peer set either way
- P/E (trailing): ~22x (est., normalizing the swing from prior-year loss)
- P/E (forward): ~20x (est.)
- P/S: ~2.5x (est.)
- PEG: ~1.4 (est., against ~14.6% forward EBITDA growth)

**Growth Profile:** Q1 2026 swung from a loss to a **$1,029 million profit on $5,640 million in revenue**, with record adjusted EBITDA. Full-year 2026 EBITDA guidance midpoint of **$7.2 billion**. Forward revenue growth ~13.6% and EBITDA growth ~14.6% — slower than Talen Energy's 35%/53.4% forward growth, but Vistra is larger, more diversified, and more profitable, which is the correct trade-off for a valuation-based mandate.

**Competitive Moat:** Existing dispatchable generation in the right grids is close to irreplicable — new nuclear takes a decade, and interconnection queues in ERCOT and PJM run years long. Vistra operates one of the largest competitive generation fleets in the US across nuclear, natural gas, and coal-to-gas assets in exactly the two grids where data center load growth is most acute. Industry-leading PPAs covering approximately **3,800MW of nuclear power with AWS** provide contracted, inflation-protected cash flows, and Vistra is one of four named suppliers in Meta's up-to-**6.6GW** long-term clean energy package.

**Key Risk:** Merchant power remains exposed to commodity and weather volatility, and the current oil/gas price collapse (Brent $72, IEA projecting 3.7–4.0 mmbbl/d oversupply) compresses spark spreads on the unhedged portion of the fleet. Regulatory risk is rising as state legislatures and public utility commissions confront the politics of data centers bidding up retail electricity prices — a backlash that is early but building. Data center load forecasts may also prove overstated if AI efficiency gains outrun compute growth.

**Rating: Buy**

### 5. FN — Fabrinet

**Sub-sector:** Optical manufacturing, packaging & photonics assembly

**Thesis:** Fabrinet is the disciplined way to own the optical interconnect bottleneck. Rather than paying 48–65x forward for branded optical names that have run 400–600% in a year, Fabrinet provides exposure to the same 800G/1.6T transceiver ramp through the contract manufacturing layer at a lower multiple, with revenue diversified across the entire customer set rather than concentrated in one product roadmap.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- P/E (trailing): **62.0x**
- P/E (forward): **44.1x**
- P/S: ~7x (est.)
- EV/EBITDA: ~35x (est.)
- PEG: **~1.3** — against 33.7% fiscal 2026 EPS growth, the most defensible relative valuation in the optical complex

**Growth Profile:** Fiscal Q3 2026 revenue guidance of **$1.15–1.20 billion**, implying roughly **35% YoY growth** at the midpoint. Fiscal Q4 (ending June 26, 2026) guided to **$1.25–1.29 billion revenue and $3.72–3.87 non-GAAP EPS**. FY2026 consensus EPS of **$13.60**, representing **33.7% growth** over fiscal 2025. Forward modeling calls for 20.3% annual earnings growth and 17.9% revenue growth. Consensus rating "Buy" with a 12-month target of ~$749.

**Competitive Moat:** Fabrinet assembles and tests a large share of the world's optical transceivers and components, with Lumentum and Coherent among its customers. Precision optical packaging is a genuinely difficult manufacturing discipline with long qualification cycles — customers do not casually re-qualify a new assembly partner mid-product-ramp. Critically, Fabrinet's diversified customer base means it captures the 800G→1.6T transition regardless of which branded vendor wins share, and industry consensus holds that demand outstrips supply through at least 2027.

**Key Risk:** Contract manufacturing is structurally a lower-margin, customer-dependent model with limited pricing power — Fabrinet captures volume but not the economics of the technology transition. Customer concentration is meaningful, and any insourcing decision by a major customer would be materially damaging. At 44x forward, the stock is not cheap in absolute terms; it is only cheap relative to a peer group that has gone parabolic, which is a relative argument that offers no protection if the whole optical complex de-rates together.

**Rating: Accumulate**

## Risk Factors

**1. The Fed is priced to hike, and equities are priced as if it won't. (Severity: High)**
Markets are discounting two 25bp increases in 2026 with year-end official projections of 3.6%–4.1%, while a 9–3 vote with three hawkish dissents signals a committee whose patience is genuinely exhausted after five-plus years above target. Long-duration growth equities carry the most exposure to this repricing, and AI infrastructure names are long-duration by construction — their value sits in out-year cash flows. This is the risk most likely to be mispriced today.

**2. Hyperscaler capex concentration is a single point of failure. (Severity: High)**
Every pick in today's spotlight sector, and much of the market's YTD gain, is a derivative of $725 billion in discretionary spending from four companies. Meta's stock already fell this week on weak guidance and dwindling free cash flow. If the payers get punished for spending, they spend less — and the supply chain de-rates as one correlated block. Comfort Systems' technology exposure rising from 43% to 58.7% of revenue in a year illustrates how quickly diversification has evaporated across this complex.

**3. Sticky core inflation with slowing growth. (Severity: Medium-High)**
Core PCE at 3.3% for four consecutive months against 1.5% Q2 GDP growth is a mild stagflationary configuration. June's headline improvement was borrowed from a 5.9% energy decline that itself depends on fragile Middle East diplomacy. Remove the energy gift and the Fed's problem gets visibly worse.

**4. Strait of Hormuz re-escalation. (Severity: Medium, but high-impact)**
Roughly 20% of global oil and LNG flows transit the strait. Spot has discarded the risk premium (Brent $72) while sell-side forecasts still carry it ($87 average for 2026) — that gap is the market's own admission of unresolved tail risk. A disruption pushing Brent above $100 would simultaneously reignite inflation, force central banks tighter, and compress equity multiples.

**5. Narrow market breadth. (Severity: Medium)**
Russell 2000 +1.37% versus Nasdaq +2.80% on the strongest day of the week is a persistent pattern, not a one-session anomaly. Rallies carried by a shrinking cohort of large caps are structurally fragile, and index-level valuation metrics increasingly obscure the condition of the median stock.

## Bottom Line

Big Tech earnings resolved July's central question — AI capex is accelerating, not decelerating, and AWS at 37% growth proves it is converting to revenue rather than merely to depreciation. But the market is now paying premium multiples for the AI narrative while the rates curve prices two hikes and core inflation sits at 3.3%, and the best risk-adjusted response to that combination is to own the constraint rather than the story: power, cooling, electrical installation, memory and optical assembly, where the same capex wave is available at 9–29x forward earnings instead of 45–65x. My conviction ranks MOD and EME first on the combination of visible multi-year backlog and unstretched multiples, with MU as the most extreme valuation dislocation in the complex and the most cyclical risk attached to it. Resist the parabolic optical names — a 500% twelve-month move is a momentum vehicle regardless of how correct the underlying demand story is, and this is a market where the discipline to buy the boring layer of a great theme is likely to be rewarded.
