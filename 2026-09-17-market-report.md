## Executive Summary

The day after the Federal Reserve's first rate hike since 2023, equities did the thing that only ever happens when the bond market blinks first: they went up. Wednesday's decision — a **unanimous 25bp increase to 3.75%–4.00%** — knocked 631 points off the Dow as Chair Kevin Warsh refused to hand markets a "one-and-done," but Thursday brought a broad relief rally, with S&P 500 futures up 1.3%, Nasdaq-100 futures up 1.7% and Dow futures up 619 points, supported by *falling* yields and *falling* oil. That combination is the market's tell: a hawkish Fed that the long end believes is not, on its face, an equity-bearish event, and the 2-year slipping a basis point to 4.72% while the 10-year and 30-year each shed roughly two is a credibility trade, not a growth scare.

The day's single largest stock-specific event sits squarely inside today's rotating spotlight. **Generac (GNRC) signed a global supply agreement with Amazon worth up to $8 billion in aggregate gross payments, with $2.4 billion of initial generator deliveries scheduled for 2027–2028, and issued Amazon a warrant on up to 1,693,745 shares struck at $200.9266** — sending the stock up 34%–45%. That is not a data center story about GPUs. It is a story about *diesel and gas reciprocating engines*, and it confirms the thesis this report has been building toward: the binding constraint on AI buildout has migrated from silicon to electrons, and the capital is now visibly following it into the least glamorous corners of the industrial economy.

Today's picks reflect that migration, and deliberately avoid the crowded, already-repriced end of it.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The hike happened, and the vote was the message

The FOMC raised the target range to **3.75%–4.00%** on September 16 — **the first increase since 2023** — and it did so **unanimously, 12–0**. The unanimity matters more than the 25bp. A Committee that voted 9–3 to *hold* at the July 28–29 meeting (with Hammack, Kashkari and Logan dissenting hawkishly) has, inside seven weeks, converged completely. There is no dovish bloc left to lean on.

Warsh's framing at the press conference was deliberately narrow: inflation has been **"too high … for too long,"** and **"we must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed. Today, the FOMC decided that this standard has not been satisfied."** He cited a strong economy and labor market as the permission structure, and — notably — **cited Middle East tension** as a contributing factor, which is an unusual admission that the Fed is now explicitly reacting to an energy-driven inflation impulse rather than looking through it.

### The dot plot is the part that stings

The updated SEP is where the hawkishness actually lives: **16 of 18 officials see at least one more 25bp hike this year, and four penciled in two more.** That is not a Committee delivering an insurance hike. That is a Committee opening a sequence. Terminal pricing has to migrate toward 4.00%–4.50%, and the equity market spent Wednesday afternoon digesting exactly that — stocks turned lower *during* the presser, not on the statement.

### Why Thursday rallied anyway

Three reasons, in order of importance:

1. **The long end fell.** If a hike compresses the term premium rather than expanding it, the discount rate applied to long-duration equity cash flows goes *down*. This is the mechanical opposite of what happened between Jackson Hole and September 16, when the 10-year gapped through 5% and multiples compressed irrespective of earnings.
2. **Oil eased.** Brent coming off the $100+ handle removes the single most visible forward inflation input, which in turn reduces the probability of the two-more-hikes tail in the dot plot actually being delivered.
3. **Positioning.** A 631-point Dow decline into a fully priced event is the definition of a market that sold the fact. The bounce is partly mechanical.

### The inflation picture Warsh is fighting

- **Headline CPI: 3.4% y/y (August)**, unchanged m/m — the *stall* is the problem, not the level.
- **Core: 2.4% y/y.**
- **Labor:** payrolls **+162,000**, unemployment **4.1%**, participation up to **61.6%** from a five-year low of 61.4%, U-6 down to 7.7%. A labor market with rising participation and 4.1% unemployment simply does not force a central bank to tolerate 3.4% headline.
- **Energy pass-through** is the swing factor. Diesel near $6/gal is a freight-cost input that shows up in goods inflation with a two-to-three-quarter lag.

### What to watch from here

1. **The next CPI print.** If headline stalls at 3.4% again, the second hike in the dot plot becomes live, and the 5% handle on the 10-year comes back.
2. **Oil.** Every $10 on Brent is roughly 0.3–0.4pp on headline CPI within two quarters. The Fed has now told you it is watching this.
3. **Fed–Treasury friction.** President Trump publicly characterized the hike as **"a raise against Trump"** — political framing of a monetary decision. Any escalation into institutional conflict is a risk-premium event independent of the rate path itself.
4. **FedEx, after the close today**, as the cleanest read on industrial freight volumes and the tariff/fuel cost pass-through.

### The mechanical consequence for equities

At a 4.72% 2-year and a ~5% 10-year, the equity risk premium on an S&P 500 near 7,600 is thin to negative on most conventional models. That has two implications for how this report constructs picks:

- **Multiple expansion is not available as a return driver.** Returns have to come from earnings growth or from a starting multiple low enough to re-rate on execution alone.
- **Backlog and contracted revenue are worth more than TAM.** In a rising-rate regime, cash flows you can *see* are worth disproportionately more than cash flows you can *imagine*. This single principle drives every name in the Stock Picks section below.

## Geopolitical Factors

**Iran and the Strait of Hormuz remain the dominant energy risk**, and 2026 has been an object lesson in how violently that risk reprices.

- **Early March:** US and Israeli military action against Iran triggered regional escalation and supply disruption; Brent exceeded **$110/bbl**, touching roughly **$117** in some sessions.
- **Mid-June:** collapse **below $70/bbl** as the Strait reopened and supply rebalanced.
- **Mid-September:** back above **$100**, with prediction-market odds of a $100 print going from 44% to effectively 100% inside 24 hours.
- **September 17:** easing, which is precisely why equities rallied.

The Strait handles roughly **20% of global oil shipments**. Academic work published this year on the 2026 conflict finds that geopolitical oil shocks transmit harder and faster than equivalent demand-driven moves, because they hit the supply curve and the risk premium simultaneously — the price effect front-runs the physical disruption. ING's read is that the buildup of US military assets in the region means **"the very real risk of significant escalation"** persists regardless of the current de-escalation in prices.

**The underappreciated second-order effect** — and it connects directly to today's spotlight: elevated and volatile natural gas and diesel pricing raises the operating cost of exactly the behind-the-meter generation that data centers are now contracting for. A $100 Brent world makes the *capex* on backup and bridge power more likely (you build for reliability regardless) but makes the *opex* meaningfully worse. Equipment suppliers get paid either way. That asymmetry is investable.

**Trade and tariffs** remain a background cost input to industrials and freight, with the effect showing up in margin commentary rather than headlines this week.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: AI Infrastructure & Data Centers — Power, Cooling, Networking, Interconnect, Memory

*(Rotation: day-of-year 260 mod 6 = 2)*

#### The thesis: the constraint moved, and the market has only partly repriced for it

For three years the AI trade was a *silicon* trade. The binding constraint was GPU supply, and the equity market allocated its multiple accordingly. That constraint has now visibly migrated. Jensen Huang's own framing — **"a supply-constrained outlook," with supply a bottleneck "at least through the end of fiscal year 28"** — is no longer only about wafer starts and advanced packaging. Increasingly it is about **whether a megawatt can be delivered to a rack.**

The numbers behind that migration:

- **Hyperscaler 2026 data center capex is running near $330 billion**, splitting roughly **60% to silicon and 40% to physical infrastructure** — real estate, power, cooling, networking. That 40% is a ~$130B annual pool, growing.
- **Data center power demand is forecast to rise as much as 170% by 2030** versus 2025.
- **American Electric Power alone plans $78 billion of investment between 2026 and 2030** to serve data-center-driven load, which Morningstar expects to translate into ~9% average annual earnings growth through 2030.
- **Liquid cooling penetration** goes from **<5% of new builds in 2023 to a projected 35–40% in 2026** as Blackwell-class racks make it non-optional.

#### The bottleneck is not the molecule. It is the machinery.

This is the most important and least understood fact in the sector right now, and it is where the mispricing lives.

**Gas turbines are sold out.** GE Vernova's combined gas turbine order-and-slot-reservation backlog has climbed to **116 GW**, heading for **125 GW by year-end**, with turbine supply effectively **sold out through 2030**. Wood Mackenzie expects turbine prices to reach **$600/kW by end-2027 — a 195% increase from 2019.** Critically, GE Vernova itself has said the backlog is *not the only bottleneck*: the constraint sits in **castings, welders and factory slots**, which is to say in things that cannot be conjured by capital alone within a 24-month window.

**The grid queue is worse.** Texas Governor Greg Abbott ordered an audit on August 3 of every data center in the ERCOT interconnection queue after requests swelled to roughly **474 GW — about 90% of it data centers, and more than five times the state's record peak demand.** That number is not a forecast of demand; it is a measure of speculative queue-stuffing, and the audit exists precisely because nobody can tell which requests are real.

**And even an approved interconnection does not get you power.** Substation transformers, generator step-up transformers and switchgear are now described as the tightest link in the chain. A developer with land, a GPU allocation and a signed interconnection agreement can still be eighteen months from energization because a transformer has not been cast.

#### Which is exactly why today's Generac–Amazon deal matters far beyond Generac

On September 16, **Generac signed a global supply agreement with a leading hyperscale operator — confirmed in the 8-K as Amazon — for backup power generators.** The structure is the interesting part:

- **Up to $8 billion in aggregate gross payments**, with **$2.4 billion of initial deliveries expected in 2027 and 2028.**
- Amazon received a **warrant on up to 1,693,745 shares at $200.9266**, of which **307,954 vested immediately** and the remainder vest in stages tied to payment milestones, exercisable through **September 16, 2033**, with anti-dilution and registration rights.
- GNRC rose as much as **45% after hours** and **~34% pre-market** — among the largest single-day moves in its history.

Read the structure, not the headline. A hyperscaler just **paid equity for supply certainty on diesel and gas reciprocating engines.** Amazon did not do this because backup generators are strategic technology. It did it because **the units are not otherwise obtainable at the volume and schedule required**, and because locking a supplier's capacity with aligned equity is cheaper than bidding against Microsoft and Google in a spot market for factory slots.

The read-through is the investable part: **if backup generation capacity is scarce enough for Amazon to issue warrants over it, then every adjacent, capacity-constrained piece of the electrical chain is underpriced relative to the certainty of its order book.** Reciprocating engines, medium-voltage switchgear, power conversion, transformers, EPC labor, and the optical transport that stitches multi-site campuses together — all of it is now demand-pulled by contracts rather than by forecasts.

#### Where the sector is *not* cheap — and why that matters for discipline

A valuation-led report has to be honest about how much of this is already in the price:

- **Powell Industries (POWL)** trades at a **P/E of 58.6x versus an estimated fair ratio of 37.7x** and an electrical industry average of 37.2x, and even on 2027 numbers is at **28x** — expensive for a project-driven industrial with lumpy revenue recognition.
- **AAON** is flagged by fair-value models at a premium, with insider selling, after a sharp run.
- **WESCO (WCC)**, the electrical distribution channel, is **+107% over 52 weeks** and now sits at **25.2x trailing / 21.2x forward with EV/EBITDA of 15.3x** — a fine business, no longer a cheap one.
- **Storage is the most extreme case.** Between June 30, 2025 and June 30, 2026, **Western Digital rose 898% and Seagate 569%.** The operating results justify a lot of it — Seagate's Q4 revenue was **$3.63B, +48.5% y/y, with gross margin expanding to 52.3% from 37.4%** and record **$3.1B** free cash flow; WDC posted **$3.75B, +43.8% y/y, 54.4% gross margin**. WDC's **~17x P/E** even screens as value. **This report is not picking either.** A 17x multiple on peak-cycle margins in a two-player commodity duopoly is a value trap in waiting, and HAMR/ePMR transition risk sits on top of it. When the cycle turns, the multiple and the earnings compress together. Noting a name and owning it are different acts.

That is the discipline for today: the theme is right, and large parts of its equity expression are already carrying 2028 in the price. The picks below are the parts that are not.

#### Networking and interconnect: the one place growth got cheaper

The exception to the "everything is priced" rule is optical. **Ciena** reported fiscal Q3 on September 3 with **EPS of $2.11 against a $1.67 consensus — a 26% beat** — and the stock **fell roughly 14% on the print and is down ~22% over 30 days.** Revenue in the prior quarter was a record **$1.57 billion, +40% y/y**; FY2026 guidance sits at **~$6.3 billion ±$100M** with **44.5%–45% adjusted gross margin and ~19% operating margin**; **backlog is $8.5 billion, expected to exceed $10 billion by year-end**; and management has guided **~30% revenue growth for 2027 as "a floor"** against a **30% revenue CAGR target through 2029.** 400G and 800G pluggable revenue is expected to **more than double from 2025**, and WaveLogic 6 holds a performance-per-watt lead on trans-oceanic and inter-data-center links.

A de-rating on a 26% beat, in the presence of an $8.5B backlog, is a *duration* event, not a *fundamentals* event — the same conflation this report flagged in enterprise software yesterday, appearing now in optical. That is a setup worth owning.

#### Memory

Memory supply is tight, HBM allocation is spoken for, and custom silicon backlogs stretch visibility into 2028. This is a genuine bottleneck and a genuine profit pool. It is also the part of the complex where the cycle risk is highest and where prior reports in this repository have already taken positions. Covered here, not picked here.

### Artificial Intelligence

**The financing structures are the story now, not the models.** Three data points define the current phase:

- **Nvidia is backing up to $105 billion in financing for an OpenAI data center in Ohio**, supporting an initial **4.25 GW** with an option for **3.75 GW** more, phasing online from 2028 — plus a **$1.5 billion investment in SB Energy**, the developer building and managing it. The project is projected to support 35,000 construction jobs through 2032 and 2,500 permanent roles.
- **Anthropic committed $50 billion** to US data center construction with UK-based neocloud **Fluidstack**, with Texas and New York facilities coming online through 2026.
- Reporting has noted the OpenAI–Nvidia arrangement came in **$145 billion below** earlier reported figures, which Fortune framed as a signal of concern about artificial demand for chips.

The pattern across all three: **the supplier is financing the customer.** Nvidia is extending credit to buy its own GPUs; Amazon is issuing warrants to secure generators. Vendor financing at this scale is how genuine capacity constraints get resolved — and also, historically, how late-cycle capex booms are extended past the point where end-demand supports them. Both things can be true at once, and the honest position is that this is the single most important thing to monitor for anyone underwriting AI infrastructure equities into 2027.

For today's purposes, note what it does to the *picks-and-shovels* names: vendor financing transfers credit risk to the supplier of capital while **leaving the equipment supplier's order book contracted and cash-collected.** The generator maker, the optical vendor and the EPC contractor get paid on delivery. That is the tier of the stack this report wants exposure to.

### 1. CMI — Cummins Inc. *(Power generation equipment — reciprocating engines & gensets)*

**Thesis:** Today's Generac–Amazon warrant deal repriced the *scarcity value of hyperscale backup generation* in a single session, and Cummins is the largest, best-capitalized, most diversified supplier of exactly that product — trading at a fraction of the multiple the market just awarded the pure-play. Cummins Power Systems is already delivering the growth: **Q2 segment sales +19% to $2.25 billion** on data center generation demand across the US, China and Asia-Pacific, with **Q1 Power Systems revenue at a record $1.96 billion, +19% y/y, and segment EBITDA of $577 million at a 29.5% margin.**

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **Trailing P/E:** ~20x
- **Forward P/E:** **~18.2x FY2026 EPS**, ~23x on some sell-side normalizations — call it **18x–23x**, versus an industrials group that has re-rated well past that for far less AI exposure
- **P/S:** ~1.5x
- **EV/EBITDA:** ~12x
- **PEG:** **~1.0–1.2** against 15%–20% earnings growth

**Growth Profile:** Full-year 2026 revenue guidance **raised to +10% to +13%** (from +8% to +11%) after Q2. **Data center revenue projected to grow from $5B in 2026 to $9B by 2030** — an ~16% CAGR in the segment that carries the highest incremental margin in the company. The truck cycle, currently a drag, is a *second*, uncorrelated recovery option that costs nothing at this entry price.

**Competitive Moat:** Installed base and service. Cummins sells the engine once and the parts, service contracts and overhauls for twenty-five years, through a global distribution and service network that no new entrant replicates. For a hyperscaler, the generator is a reliability asset whose value is entirely in uptime — which makes service density, not unit price, the purchase criterion. That is a moat that widens as the installed fleet grows, and it converts a cyclical equipment business into a recurring aftermarket annuity.

**Key Risk:** The truck cycle. Roughly half the business is tied to North American heavy-duty and medium-duty truck demand, which a hiking Fed and a freight recession can suppress for longer than data center growth can offset. FedEx's report after today's close is a live datapoint on exactly this. Secondarily: emissions regulation shifts and the cost of the Accelera zero-emissions segment, which remains a drag on consolidated margin.

**Rating: Buy**

### 2. CIEN — Ciena Corporation *(Optical networking & data center interconnect)*

**Thesis:** Ciena beat fiscal Q3 EPS by 26% ($2.11 vs. $1.67) on September 3 and the stock fell ~14% on the print and ~22% over thirty days — a de-rating of a business whose **backlog is $8.5 billion and expected to exceed $10 billion by year-end**, and whose management calls **~30% revenue growth in 2027 "a floor that will move higher."** When multi-gigawatt campuses get built across multiple sites, the fiber between them is not optional, and Ciena's WaveLogic 6 holds the performance-per-watt lead on exactly those trans-oceanic and inter-data-center links.

**Valuation Metrics:**

- **EV/EBITDA:** ~22x

A 32x forward multiple on a business whose growth depends on a handful of hyperscale customers means any single deferred build shows up violently in the stock — as the September 3 reaction demonstrated on *good* news. Supply constraints on components have also been flagged as capping near-term upside. This is the highest-multiple name in today's list and is sized accordingly.

**Rating: Buy**

### 3. PRIM — Primoris Services Corporation *(Infrastructure services — power & utility EPC)*

**Thesis:** The purest expression of the "labor and factory slots are the bottleneck" argument, available at a **trough-earnings multiple**. The stock is **down ~25% in 2026** on margin problems from legacy projects, while the order book went the other way: **total backlog of $13.9 billion as of June 30, 2026** ($7.7B Utilities, $6.2B Energy) after **record Q2 bookings of more than $3.9 billion.** You are being paid to wait for problem projects to roll off a book that has never been larger.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **Forward P/E (NTM):** ~23.4x — *depressed earnings denominator*
- **P/E on 2027 EPS:** **~14.2x**, a discount to peers and to its own history
- **P/S:** ~0.5x
- **EV/EBITDA:** ~9x
- **PEG:** **<1.0** on the 2026→2027 earnings recovery

**Growth Profile:** Q2 2026 posted a **net loss on lower revenue** — this is a genuine trough, not a cosmetic one. **Gross margins are projected to normalize to 10%–12% in 2027** as problem projects complete. The company sits at the intersection of three simultaneous spending cycles: **renewable energy, data center buildout, and natural gas generation.** Backlog conversion, not demand generation, is the entire earnings story from here.

**Competitive Moat:** Skilled craft labor at scale. The binding constraint GE Vernova named — **welders** — is Primoris's asset. Self-perform capability in high-voltage transmission, substation and generation construction, with a bonded, qualified workforce and utility master service agreements, cannot be stood up quickly by a competitor with capital alone. When 474 GW is sitting in the ERCOT queue and transformers and switchgear are the tightest link, the firms that can *physically install* them price with leverage.

**Key Risk:** Execution, and it has already bitten. Fixed-price EPC is a business where one badly bid project erases a year of margin, and Primoris has just demonstrated that. If the 2027 margin normalization to 10%–12% slips another year, the 14x on 2027 becomes 14x on a number that does not arrive. This is the highest-variance name in today's list and the one where position sizing matters most.

**Rating: Accumulate**

### 4. AEIS — Advanced Energy Industries, Inc. *(Power electronics — data center power conversion)*

**Thesis:** The rack-level answer to the same question Generac answers at the building level — how do you get clean, dense, efficient power into a Blackwell-class rack — and it is the one name in the power complex that still screens with a **PEG near 1.0** and a **~30% discount to the Zacks tech sector on forward P/E.** Data center revenue **more than doubled in 2025** and the company guides to **over 30% growth in that segment in 2026**, with its move into **800V DC architecture** positioning it directly on the roadmap NVIDIA has specified for next-generation racks.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **Forward P/E:** ~28x — **roughly a 30% discount to the tech sector**
- **P/S:** ~5x
- **EV/EBITDA:** ~20x
- **PEG:** **~0.99** versus the tech sector's 1.4

**Growth Profile:** **Q1 2026 EPS of $2.09, beating consensus by 6.1%, on revenue of $511 million** (+1.2% vs. estimates). **Non-GAAP operating margin expanded 560bp y/y to 19.1%** — the margin story is as good as the revenue story, which is what distinguishes a genuine mix shift from a volume bump. Data center computing revenue more than doubled. The semiconductor capital equipment segment, currently the slower half, is a *second* cyclical recovery option embedded at no cost.

**Competitive Moat:** Qualification lock-in. Power conversion units are designed into a rack architecture years ahead of volume and are re-qualified only under duress — efficiency, thermal envelope and failure rate are all safety-and-uptime critical. Advanced Energy's position across both semiconductor process power and data center power gives it an engineering base and a cost structure that a single-market competitor cannot match.

**Key Risk:** The rally has already happened — **+40% over three months and a tripling over twelve** — and at least one analyst has downgraded specifically on valuation after the move. If data center segment growth decelerates below the 30% guide while the semicap recovery stays deferred, there is no second engine, and a 28x multiple on a company with cyclical DNA compresses quickly.

**Rating: Accumulate**

## Risk Factors

**1. The dot plot is not priced. (Severity: High)**
Sixteen of eighteen FOMC members see at least one more hike this year; four see two. Markets rallied Thursday on a two-basis-point decline at the long end, which is a thin foundation. If the next CPI prints another 3.4% stall, terminal pricing moves toward 4.50% and the 10-year revisits and exceeds 5.04%. Every multiple in this report compresses in that scenario, and the highest ones — CIEN at 32x, AEIS at 28x — compress most.

**2. Energy re-escalation. (Severity: High)**
Brent went from sub-$70 in June to above $100 in September. The Strait of Hormuz carries ~20% of seaborne oil, US assets continue to build in the region, and Warsh has now explicitly named Middle East tension as an input to a *monetary policy decision*. A renewed supply shock is simultaneously an inflation shock, a Fed-path shock and a margin shock — three transmission channels from one event.

**3. AI capex financing quality. (Severity: High and rising)**
Nvidia financing up to **$105 billion** for an OpenAI facility and Amazon paying in **warrants** for generator supply are both rational responses to genuine scarcity. They are also vendor financing, which historically extends capex cycles past the point of end-demand support. The reported **$145 billion shortfall** versus earlier figures on the OpenAI–Nvidia arrangement is the first visible crack in the headline-number narrative. Monitor the *cash* conversion of infrastructure orders, not the announced totals.

**4. Rate transmission into the real economy. (Severity: Medium-High)**
Lennar missed on EPS **and** revenue **and** margin **and** cut its delivery target, on the same day the Fed hiked. Housing is the cleanest transmission channel from policy to activity, and it is already breaking. FedEx after today's close is the second datapoint. If both confirm, the "strong economy" leg of Warsh's justification weakens — which is good for rates and bad for cyclical earnings, a combination that hurts industrials with truck exposure (a direct CMI risk).

**5. Concentration and crowding in the AI infrastructure trade. (Severity: Medium)**
The entire complex now trades as a single factor. Broadcom's early-September results dragged Intel, AMD and Micron on no company-specific news. Diversification *within* AI infrastructure is largely illusory on a one-week horizon; it only pays over a full cycle. Position accordingly.

---

## Bottom Line

The Fed hiked, told you it may hike again, and the long end rallied anyway — which is the bond market saying it believes Warsh will actually get inflation down, and is the single most constructive development for equities in this report. Take it at face value but not on faith: the dot plot's second hike is not in the price, and the next CPI print decides whether Thursday's rally was a bottom or a bounce.

The actionable signal today was not the Fed. It was **Amazon paying equity for diesel generators** — a hyperscaler telling you, in the most credible way available, that electrons and the machinery that delivers them are now scarcer than the silicon they feed. Own that constraint where it has not already been repriced: **Cummins, Ciena, Primoris, Advanced Energy and Jabil** — five sub-sectors, a blended PEG near 1.0, and not one of them requiring multiple expansion to work.
