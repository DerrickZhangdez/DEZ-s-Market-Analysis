# Daily Market Report — Friday, September 4, 2026

## Executive Summary

Friday was a jobs-day market, and the jobs number was a genuine shock: U.S. payrolls added **162,000** in August against a consensus near **55,000**, with unemployment holding at **4.1%**. In an ordinary cycle that would be unambiguously good news; in this one — with the Fed on hold at 3.50%–3.75%, three dissenting votes for a *hike*, and Chair Kevin Warsh warning that underlying inflation is not slowing — a hot labor print is a hawkish event. What kept the tape orderly was timing: Governor Christopher Waller had already signaled on Thursday that he would support holding rates steady in September, knocking September hike odds from roughly 70% earlier in the week to about even money, and that cushion absorbed the payrolls surprise rather than amplifying it. Equities entered Friday off a strong Thursday session (S&P 500 +1.06% to 7,747.71) and futures edged higher after the release, with yields retreating rather than spiking.

Beneath the index level, the more interesting story is a violent dispersion inside enterprise software — today's rotating spotlight. This week alone Snowflake surged ~22% on a third consecutive quarter of *accelerating* product revenue growth (+37%), Zscaler rose on a 25% ARR quarter, Ciena guided fiscal 2026 revenue up 35%, and Guidewire — which beat on every current metric — was taken down nearly 15% after hours because it guided fiscal 2027 ARR growth to 18% instead of 19%. That is the signature of a market that has stopped paying for software as an asset class and started underwriting each name on its own cash flows and its own AI attach rate.

The macro regime is the key to reading the picks below. With the policy rate biased upward and the 10-year at 4.78%, long-duration software multiples are the wrong place to be, and the opportunity has moved to profitable, cash-generative, moderately-growing software trading at single- and low-double-digit forward earnings multiples. Today's slate leans hard into that: six names across six software sub-sectors, none of them mega-cap, none of them Magnificent Seven, none of them repeated from the last seven reports.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The hike debate is real, and it is close to a coin flip

The Federal Reserve has held the federal funds rate at **3.50%–3.75% for five consecutive meetings** through July 2026. What makes this hold different from an ordinary pause is the composition of the vote: **three FOMC members dissented in favor of raising the policy rate by 25 basis points**. Dissents in the hawkish direction, at a meeting where the committee did nothing, are the clearest possible signal that the next move is not down.

Chair **Kevin Warsh** has reinforced that reading, flagging that underlying inflation is not slowing, and his Jackson Hole address in late August pushed market-implied September hike odds above **63%**. That is the setup Waller then partially dismantled.

### The Waller pivot and the payrolls shock

The week's two decisive events landed 24 hours apart:

1. **Thursday, September 3 — Waller signals a hold.** Governor Christopher Waller indicated he will support holding rates steady at the September meeting. Market-implied odds for a hike at the **September 15–16 FOMC** fell sharply — to roughly **54.6%**, down about 12 percentage points, per CME FedWatch, and down from about 70% earlier in the week. Treasury yields retreated globally, and equities responded with a broad 1%+ advance.

2. **Friday, September 4 — payrolls run hot.** August payrolls came in at **+162,000** versus a consensus around **55,000–56,000**, a decisive beat, with the unemployment rate unchanged at **4.1%**. For context on how noisy this series has been, July had printed a *loss* of roughly 23,000 jobs, which at the time had collapsed September hike odds. A swing from −23k to +162k in one month is a large revision to the labor-market narrative.

Post-payrolls, swaps settled at roughly **even odds** of a quarter-point September hike. The remarkable part is that yields *fell* on a hot number — evidence that Waller's guidance was treated as more informative about the September decision than the data itself, with the hawkish implication pushed out to later meetings.

### What the forward curve says

- Fed funds futures price roughly **4% by year-end 2026**, implying about one hike gets done before the calendar turns.
- **J.P. Morgan Global Research now expects the Fed to hike in December**, arguing the central bank has yet to lay out a credible roadmap for tackling inflation.
- The distribution of outcomes has narrowed even as the *direction* flipped: this is no longer a market debating the depth of a cutting cycle, it is one debating the timing of one or two hikes.

### The regime implication for portfolio construction

This is the analytical core of today's report. A world where the policy rate is biased **up** from 3.50%–3.75%, the 10-year sits at 4.78%, and nominal growth is strong enough to generate 162k jobs and a 19% small-cap advance is a world that systematically punishes duration and rewards near-term free cash flow. In equities that means:

- **Penalized:** high-multiple, low-profitability growth; anything whose value sits in terminal-year cash flows; SaaS priced above roughly 10x revenue.
- **Rewarded:** businesses with 40%+ EBITDA margins, net cash balance sheets, buyback capacity, and forward P/Es in the teens or below — *provided* growth has not gone to zero.

Today's spotlight sector happens to contain an unusual number of the second category, because the 2025–2026 software derating was indiscriminate.

## Geopolitical Factors

### U.S.–Iran: active exchange of fire around the Strait of Hormuz

This is the most acute risk on the board, and it escalated within the last week.

- **August 30–31:** U.S. forces struck **Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island**, near the Strait of Hormuz, preventing an attempted **mining of the waterway**. Iran retaliated with missiles fired toward U.S. military targets in **Jordan and the United Arab Emirates**. This was the first direct exchange in roughly a month, following President Trump's warning that "we're going to hit them hard" after Iranian targeting of U.S. forces in Jordan.
- **Oil reaction:** Brent rose **2.7% to settle at $90.49**, moving above $90/bbl; crude jumped ~3% overnight.
- **Equity reaction (Aug. 31):** S&P 500 −0.3%, Dow −0.7%, Nasdaq −0.1% — a notably contained response. Energy outperformed sharply, with **Exxon Mobil +2.7%** and **Chevron +2.1%**.
- **Consumer transmission:** U.S. gasoline **topped $4/gallon on a daily basis in August for the first time**, and the new strikes pushed prices higher again. This is the channel by which Hormuz risk becomes a Fed problem — energy passthrough into headline inflation, in a month where the committee is already one CPI print away from a hike.

An attempted mining of Hormuz is a materially different class of event from a proxy exchange. Roughly a fifth of seaborne crude transits that chokepoint, and the World Bank has documented that Hormuz disruption sends prices surging. Markets are pricing a contained conflict; the tail is not contained.

### The broader risk-premium stack

Beyond Iran, three additional situations are contributing to the crude risk premium:

- **Venezuela:** the overthrow of the country's leadership, with attendant uncertainty over production and sanctions policy.
- **Iran sanctions:** a new round of U.S. sanctions layered on top of the military pressure.
- **Ukraine:** slow progress in peace negotiations, keeping European energy and grain flows unresolved.

### The countervailing force: supply

The reason $90 Brent has not become $120 Brent is that the underlying balance is loose:

- **J.P. Morgan** forecasts Brent averaging **$86 in Q3 2026**, **$80 in Q4**, and **$78 at year-end** — i.e., they expect the geopolitical premium to bleed out.
- **Goldman Sachs** projects lower oil prices through 2026 as supply swells.
- **OPEC+** maintained its decision to pause production increases for the first quarter, holding back barrels that could otherwise cap rallies.
- **ABN AMRO** frames the current market precisely: "geopolitics drive volatility amid supply glut." **ING** argues the lingering uncertainty requires a "crude rethink."
- Offsetting the glut view, near-term balances are tight: a **3.7 mb/d deficit** was projected for Q2 2026 on reduced Middle East production, and shipping-asset dislocation is keeping physical markets strained.

**Net read:** two-sided risk with a fat right tail on price. Structurally bearish supply, acutely bullish geopolitics. For an equity portfolio, the practical consequence is that a software-heavy slate like today's carries an unhedged energy-inflation exposure — an oil spike is simultaneously a Fed-hike catalyst and a software-multiple compressor. That is a real, acknowledged weakness of today's construction, addressed in the Portfolio Construction Notes.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Enterprise Software & SaaS

*(Rotation math: day-of-year 247 mod 6 = 1 → Enterprise software & SaaS — data, security, devtools, vertical SaaS.)*

This is the right week for this spotlight. Enterprise software just delivered the most informative earnings cluster of the quarter, and the market's reaction function has changed in a way that creates exploitable mispricing.

#### The single most important chart in software right now: dispersion

Four data points from the last four trading days tell the whole story.

**1. Snowflake (SNOW) — acceleration gets paid, enormously.**
Q2 fiscal 2027 revenue of **$1,546.79M, +35.1% YoY**, beating the $1,481.23M consensus by 4.4%. Product revenue — the metric management leans on — hit **$1.49B, +37% YoY**, the **third consecutive quarter of accelerating growth**. Management attributed roughly **half of the acceleration to AI products**, with core platform migrations and consumption growth supplying the rest. The **CoCo AI coding agent** now sits in **9,100 accounts**, up more than 2,000 in the quarter. FY27 product revenue guidance was raised to **$6.07B from $5.84B**. Shares jumped **~22–23%**.

**2. Zscaler (ZS) — security is the highest-quality demand signal in software.**
Q4 fiscal 2026 revenue **$898M, +25% YoY** (vs. $877M expected), with **ARR also +25%**, and adjusted EPS of **$1.19 vs. $1.09** expected. FY27 guidance: revenue **$3.908–3.938B (+16.6%–17.5%)**, ARR **$4.396–4.426B**, non-GAAP operating income **$924–932M**, and free cash flow margin **23.0%–23.5%**. CEO Jay Chaudhry said he is "very bullish" on the newly launched Zero Trust iteration **for AI agents**, expected to accelerate into FY28–FY29. Shares rose. The causal driver is worth naming precisely: rising *AI risk* is spurring urgent demand for cyber tools. Agentic AI deployment creates non-human identities that must be governed, and that is a budget line that did not exist two years ago.

**3. Guidewire (GWRE) — a beat is no longer enough.**
This is the cautionary tale, and it happened Thursday. Guidewire delivered Q4 FY26 revenue of **$411M (+15%)**, adjusted EPS of **$0.99** (topping consensus), **ARR of $1.242B, +19% YoY** in constant currency and **above the high end of guidance**, subscription and support revenue **+33%**, and genuinely exceptional retention: **gross ARR attrition below 1.5% overall and below 1% for core systems customers**, with strong adoption of new products ProNavigator and PricingCenter. The stock closed the regular session **+5.24% at $202.86** — then fell **14.82% after hours to $172.80**. The reason: FY27 guidance of **18% ARR growth**, down from 19%, partly because management assumes record-low attrition normalizes. A one-point deceleration, transparently and conservatively explained, cost roughly a fifth of the company's market value against its intraday high.

**4. Ciena (CIEN) — the AI-adjacent networking bid is ferocious.**
Fiscal Q3 revenue **$1.67B, +37% YoY**, adjusted EPS **$2.11, +215% YoY**, with FY26 revenue guidance raised to **$6.42B ±$50M, +35% at the midpoint**. Ciena is not a SaaS company, but it is the clearest evidence that the AI buildout is now converting into hard revenue for the layer that connects data centers.

#### What that dispersion means

Put those four side by side and a rule emerges. **The market is paying for the second derivative of growth and for demonstrable AI monetization, and it is charging punitive rent on any deceleration in a premium multiple.** Snowflake accelerated and got 22%. Guidewire decelerated by one point off a 19% base and lost 15%. Both were, on the fundamentals, good quarters.

This is the correct behavior for a market where the risk-free rate is 4.78% and the policy rate may go higher. When money costs something, the terminal value in a DCF shrinks and the *shape* of the growth curve matters more than its level. Investors are no longer buying software beta; they are underwriting individual cash flow streams.

#### The valuation backdrop: a genuinely bifurcated asset class

The multiple structure of public software as of August–September 2026:

| Metric | Level |
|---|---|
| Median public SaaS EV/Revenue | **~6–7x** |
| Top-quartile EV/Revenue | 13–14x |
| **Bottom-quartile EV/Revenue** | **1–2x** |
| SaaS index aggregate EV/EBITDA | ~26.6x |
| 309 listed system/application software cos. (Jan. 2026) | 11.4x revenue, 24.5x EBITDA for profitable names |

That bottom quartile at **1–2x revenue** is the entire opportunity set. A 6–7x median with a 1–2x floor and a 13–14x ceiling is not a valuation range, it is two different asset classes wearing the same sector label. Software multiples in August 2026 show clear segmentation across **infrastructure, vertical, and horizontal** categories, and public investors are now valuing these businesses on **AI application risk, technical complexity, market position, and specialization depth — rather than TAM size alone**. That last shift is the death of the "$100B TAM" pitch deck as a valuation input, and it is unambiguously healthy.

Notably, **EV/EBITDA has displaced EV/Revenue as the operative metric** for SaaS in 2026, and the index at ~26.6x EBITDA is described as fairly reasonable by historical standards. When an asset class stops being valued on revenue, the companies that were only ever going to be valuable on revenue get repriced — permanently.

#### Sub-sector by sub-sector

**Data infrastructure — the premium tier.** Data infrastructure commands the **highest multiples across all software categories**, driven by the AI data boom, with platforms like Snowflake and Databricks now genuinely indispensable to enterprise AI initiatives. Databricks' ~$1B Series K underscores the private-market premium on this layer. The investment problem is that this is precisely where multiples are least forgiving — Snowflake earned its 22% but is not cheap. The opportunity is one tier down, in the profitable, unglamorous data platforms trading at bottom-half multiples.

**Security — the most defensible budget in enterprise IT.** Zscaler's quarter is the tell, but the structural point is broader: security buyers are now evaluating vendors on their capacity to **govern the data pipelines that feed generative models**. That reframes data security from a compliance cost into AI enablement infrastructure. Varonis is the instructive counterexample of execution risk in this sub-sector — its total SaaS ARR reached **$726.0M in Q2 2026, +52% YoY**, with ARR ex-conversions **+25%** and new-logo SaaS ARR up more than 20% on AI-security products (Atlas, Interceptor, Database Activity Monitoring), against a **$48B** addressable market. Yet the equity has been destroyed: a **October 28, 2025** disclosure of weaker-than-expected on-premises-to-SaaS renewals and conversions sent shares down **over 48% in a single day**, erasing ~$3.8B of market value; FY26 adjusted EPS guidance of **$0.08 at the midpoint missed consensus by ~76.6%**; gross margin narrowed 4.2 points to **75.3%**; and billings *declined* 1.5% YoY. The stock bottomed near **$23.69** in February, a 62.9% fall from its $63.90 high, and market capitalization sits near **$2.93B**. At roughly 4x SaaS ARR for a 25% ex-conversion grower, VRNS is statistically arresting — and it is not in today's slate, deliberately. Growing ARR while billings shrink and margins compress is the signature of a business buying growth, and the SaaS-transition mechanics that broke in October 2025 have not visibly been repaired. This is a watch-list name pending two clean quarters of billings growth, not a position.

**Vertical SaaS — where the durable moats live.** The highest-valued vertical software comps include S&P Global, Cadence, Moody's, and Constellation Software — businesses whose pricing power comes from embedding in a specific industry's workflow rather than from horizontal scale. The strategic argument gaining traction is that **vertical AI is a potentially 10x larger opportunity than vertical SaaS, because it competes for the ~13% of U.S. GDP spent on business labor rather than the ~1% spent on IT**. Whether or not that multiple is right, the direction is: software that automates work priced against a labor budget has a far larger ceiling than software priced against an IT budget. Guidewire's sub-1% core attrition is what that moat looks like in a metric — and Thursday's selloff is what happens when you pay 15x revenue for it.

**Devtools — where private capital is distorting public comps.** Developer infrastructure is seeing extraordinary private momentum: Anysphere (Cursor) reached **$500M ARR** after doubling roughly every two months through early 2025, and Supabase has attracted **1.7M+ developers** to its open-source backend. For public-market investors this is mostly a warning. The fastest-compounding assets in this sub-sector are private, and the AI coding agents driving that growth are simultaneously a competitive threat to incumbent devtools and, per Snowflake's CoCo disclosure, a revenue driver for adjacent platforms. Public devtools names carry genuine disruption risk that their multiples do not always reflect.

**Horizontal/legacy platforms — the value bin.** ServiceNow grew revenue **21% to $13.3B in 2025** with **$1.75B of net income** and still fell through 2025 and early 2026. **SAP dropped 13% in 30 days.** When high-quality horizontal platforms derate on good numbers, the sub-sector is being repriced for rates, not for fundamentals — and that is a buyer's condition for the profitable subset.

#### Two more names from this week's tape

The smaller reporters round out the picture: **Upland Software** and **Consensus Cloud Solutions** both filed Q2 FY26 results, and **MediaAlpha** filed a September 2 8-K. The small-cap software cohort remains where the widest valuation gaps sit, and also where the execution risk is most concentrated.

#### The spotlight thesis, stated plainly

Enterprise software in September 2026 offers something it has not offered since 2022: **a large set of profitable, moderately-growing, net-cash software businesses trading at forward earnings multiples in the 10–15x range**, in an asset class whose bottom quartile trades at 1–2x revenue. The market's new discipline — pricing AI attach rates and cash flow rather than TAM — is exactly what created these gaps. Today's picks are drawn from that intersection, weighted toward names where the AI story is a real revenue line rather than a slide.

### Artificial Intelligence

The AI capital cycle has moved into a phase where the financing structures are more remarkable than the technology announcements.

**Nvidia's vendor-financing flywheel.** Nvidia is providing **up to $105 billion in financing** for a new OpenAI data center in **Pike County, Ohio** (the PORTS-Pike Technology Campus), supporting an initial **4.25 gigawatts** of compute with an option for an additional **3.75 GW**. **SB Energy** will build and manage the facility under a **20-year lease to OpenAI**, with Nvidia providing the compute and capacity phasing in from **2028**. This builds on Nvidia's earlier commitment of **up to $100 billion** to OpenAI as part of a partnership to deploy at least **10 GW** of Nvidia systems, and reporting that the two have discussed a backstop of **up to $250 billion**.

**Anthropic's build-out.** Anthropic has committed **$50 billion** to a data center partnership with U.K.-based neocloud provider **Fluidstack**, with facilities in **Texas and New York** coming online through 2026.

**The analytical point investors should sit with.** When the dominant supplier of AI accelerators is financing its largest customer's purchases of those accelerators, at the scale of $100B+, the revenue quality question becomes unavoidable. This is not necessarily improper — vendor financing is a legitimate and common structure for capital equipment — but it does mean a meaningful share of reported AI infrastructure demand is being underwritten by the supplier rather than by the customer's independent cash flows or external capital. That circularity is the single largest systemic risk in the AI trade, and it is not reflected in a VIX of 14.

Two mitigating observations. First, the 2028 phasing on the Ohio capacity means these commitments are long-dated and revocable in practice, which cuts both ways: less near-term revenue certainty, but also less near-term balance-sheet risk. Second, the demand appearing in *third-party* results — Ciena's +37% revenue quarter, Broadcom's +221% AI semiconductor growth, Snowflake's AI-driven acceleration — is not vendor-financed by Nvidia and constitutes genuine independent corroboration that the workloads are real.

**Where AI is showing up as software revenue.** This is the more investable observation. AI is now a line item, not a narrative: roughly **half** of Snowflake's acceleration came from AI products; Zscaler's Zero Trust for AI agents is an emerging ARR driver; Guidewire's ProNavigator and PricingCenter are AI-era products contributing to sub-1% attrition; Pegasystems has built its entire 2026 product cycle around agentic AI with a novel pricing architecture (below). Enterprise AI monetization has crossed from pilots into consumption.

### 1. PEGA — Pegasystems, Inc. *(Enterprise workflow, decisioning & agentic AI platform)*

**Growth profile.** The headline optics are ugly and the substance is not. Q1 2026 revenue was **$429.97M, −10% YoY**, with net income **$32.76M, −62% YoY** — both artifacts of revenue recognition shifting away from upfront term licenses. Underneath: **Pega Cloud ACV +29% YoY (+27% cc)** in Q1 and **+22% YoY** in Q2, with Pega Cloud revenue rising to **$205M from $151M**, and Pega Cloud ACV crossing **just over $900M** — approaching the $1B mark. Total ACV grew **12% (+11% cc)**. Cash generation is the real story: **$207M of FCF in Q1**, **$288M in the first half**, and **$575M guided for the full year**, alongside repurchases of **9 million shares for over $360M**.

**Competitive moat.** Pega sits in mission-critical decisioning and case-management workflows at large financial institutions, insurers, and government agencies — systems that are re-platformed on decade timescales, not procurement cycles. The 2026 product cycle deepens this: **Pega Infinity 26** pairs agentic AI with built-in governance and a pricing model that **eliminates consumption-based AI charges**, and the **Predictable AI** architecture shifts heavy reasoning to design time so runtime agents are fast, reliable, and dramatically cheaper to run. Pega has explicitly positioned this as eliminating the "AI token tax." For a CIO who has been burned by unbounded inference bills, predictable AI cost is a procurement argument, not a technical one — and it is the kind of thing an incumbent with the workflow already in place can sell far more easily than a startup can.

**Key risk.** The transition math has to keep working. Reported revenue declining 10% and net income falling 62% gives the bears a clean narrative, and if cloud ACV growth decelerates from the high 20s toward total-ACV growth of ~12% before the license drag rolls off, the stock is cheap for a reason rather than cheap by accident. **Rating: Buy.** The largest position in today's slate. A 10.6x forward multiple on a business generating $575M of FCF with a ~$900M cloud ACV base growing in the 20s is the clearest risk-reward on this page, and the agentic-AI pricing architecture is real optionality that costs nothing at this multiple.

**Rating: Buy.** RPO accelerating to +28% while the multiple sits below the sector median is a straightforward mispricing, and the AI-retrieval position is the highest-quality optionality in today's slate.

### 4. DT — Dynatrace, Inc. *(Observability & AI operations)*

**Thesis.** Dynatrace crossed **$2B of ARR growing 16%** with a **29% non-GAAP operating margin**, **$529M of free cash flow**, and roughly **$4.6B of net cash** — and Q1 FY27 delivered **41% organic net-new ARR growth**, an inflection that the 26x forward multiple does not yet reflect. As enterprises put agentic AI into production, observability shifts from an infrastructure cost to the control plane for systems no human is directly supervising.

| EV/Sales | **~5.1x** *(derived: $10.73B / $2.10B)* | — |
| Market cap / EV | **$15.34B / $10.73B** (~$4.6B net cash) | Sept. 2026 |
| TTM revenue / profit | **$2.10B / $151.37M** | Sept. 2026 |

*Note on the EV/EBITDA discrepancy:* the 49.8x GAAP EV/EBITDA and 25.4x EV/FCF cannot both describe the same economics — the gap is stock-based compensation, which depresses GAAP EBITDA far below cash generation. The reliable anchors are **EV/FCF of 25.4x**, the **29% non-GAAP operating margin**, and **$529M of actual free cash flow**. The forward P/E of ~26x versus a GAAP trailing P/E of 107x reflects the same distortion. Investors should size this position knowing that a meaningful share of Dynatrace's compensation is dilutive rather than cash — this is the most SBC-dependent name in today's slate.

**Growth profile.** FY26 closed with **ARR above $2 billion, +16%** — the **fourth consecutive quarter at 16%**, which is consistency rather than deceleration. Full-year non-GAAP operating income of **$592M (29% margin)** and **free cash flow of $529M** (a ~25% FCF margin on $2.1B of revenue). The forward-looking datapoint that matters most: **Q1 FY27 delivered 41% organic net-new ARR growth**. Net-new ARR is the leading indicator for the ARR base, and a 41% increase in the rate of new business capture is not consistent with a 16%-grower's multiple. FY27 guidance is **ARR +15.5%–16.5%** and **revenue +14%–15%** — guidance that appears conservative against the Q1 net-new figure. Gross margins run in the mid-80s.

**Competitive moat.** Dynatrace's differentiation is its automatic, dependency-aware topology mapping — the OneAgent deployment model discovers and maps the application estate without manual instrumentation, which is the hard part of observability at enterprise scale. Once that map exists and alerting, SLOs, and root-cause automation are built on top of it, replacement means re-instrumenting the entire estate. The evolution **from observability into a comprehensive AI operations platform** extends this: agentic AI systems make autonomous decisions at machine speed, and enterprises deploying them need automated root-cause analysis rather than dashboards a human reads after the fact. The company that already holds the dependency graph is best positioned to sell that.

**Key risk.** Competitive intensity from Datadog, Splunk/Cisco, Grafana, and the open-source OpenTelemetry standard, which commoditizes the instrumentation layer where Dynatrace's agent advantage lives. If OpenTelemetry becomes fully sufficient, the moat narrows to the analytics layer, which is more contestable. Secondary risk: the multiple already embeds an inflection — at 26x forward earnings and a 1.70 PEG, Dynatrace is priced for the 41% net-new ARR to persist, and a reversion toward the guided 15.5%–16.5% range without upside would leave the stock fairly valued rather than cheap.

**Rating: Accumulate.** The quality is unambiguous and the 41% net-new ARR inflection is a genuine catalyst, but a 1.70 PEG and heavy SBC dilution justify building this position over time rather than paying up at once. The highest-quality business in the slate; not the best price.

But 27x forward with direct exposure to the report's largest named geopolitical risk argues for building into weakness rather than a full position today. The smallest allocation in the slate.

## Risk Factors

Ranked by severity — probability weighted against market impact.

### 1. A September Fed hike, or a hawkish hold that repriced the curve *(Severity: High | Probability: ~50%)*
This is the dominant risk because it is both imminent and close to a coin flip. Swaps price roughly **even odds** of a 25bp hike at the **September 15–16 FOMC**, having swung from 63%+ after Warsh's Jackson Hole address, to ~54.6% after Waller's Thursday comments, to about 50/50 after Friday's 162k payrolls. **Three FOMC members already dissented in favor of a hike.** J.P. Morgan expects a December hike, and futures price ~4% fed funds by year-end. The transmission to this report's picks is direct and mechanical: a hike lifts the 10-year from 4.78%, raises the discount rate on software cash flows, and compresses the 26–27x forward multiples on DT, MANH, and DSGX hardest. Watch the pre-FOMC August CPI — Waller's hold is explicitly data-contingent, and a hot core print is the trigger.

### 2. Strait of Hormuz escalation *(Severity: Very High | Probability: Low-to-moderate)*
The severity ranking here is about magnitude, not likelihood. The U.S. and Iran exchanged direct fire on **August 30–31** — U.S. strikes on Iranian rocket launchers on **Larak Island** that **prevented an attempted mining of the strait**, answered by Iranian missiles toward U.S. targets in **Jordan and the UAE**. Brent settled at **$90.49 (+2.7%)** and gasoline had already topped **$4/gallon** daily in August for the first time. A successful closure or mining of Hormuz would be a multi-standard-deviation event for oil, and — critically — it would arrive as a *stagflationary* shock: higher headline inflation forcing a hawkish Fed into a weakening economy. The market's response so far (S&P −0.3% on August 31, VIX near 14) prices this as noise. It is not noise; it is a low-probability, very-high-impact tail that options markets are currently selling cheaply.

### 3. Premium-multiple guidance sensitivity across software *(Severity: High for this slate | Probability: High)*
Guidewire is the proof of concept: a beat on revenue, EPS, and ARR, with sub-1% core attrition, met a **−14.82%** after-hours move because FY27 ARR guidance came in at 18% instead of 19%. That is not a Guidewire idiosyncrasy, it is the market's current reaction function for any software name carrying a premium multiple into a print. **MANH and DSGX in today's slate carry comparable multiples**, and their next reports are live single-name risks. This is the specific reason both are rated Accumulate rather than Buy.

### 4. Circularity in AI infrastructure financing *(Severity: High | Probability: Low near-term)*
Nvidia is financing **up to $105B** for OpenAI's Ohio data center, has committed **up to $100B** to OpenAI for 10GW of systems, and has reportedly discussed a backstop of **up to $250B** — while OpenAI uses that capital to buy Nvidia compute. Anthropic has separately committed **$50B** with Fluidstack. When the dominant supplier underwrites its largest customer's purchases at this scale, reported demand is partly supplier-underwritten rather than independently funded. The mitigants are real — the Ohio capacity phases in from **2028**, and third-party corroboration is strong (Broadcom's AI revenue **+221%**, Ciena **+37%**, Snowflake's AI-driven acceleration, none of it Nvidia-financed) — which is why this ranks fourth rather than first. But a credit event or a capex pause at any major AI lab would cascade through semis, networking, power, and data-infrastructure software simultaneously, and it is the risk least reflected in a VIX of 14.

### 5. Volatility complacency and the small-cap unwind *(Severity: Moderate | Probability: Moderate)*
A **VIX near 14** coexisting with a coin-flip rate hike, an active U.S.–Iran military exchange, and $90 oil means protection is historically cheap relative to the risk set — and that hedges are not in place across the market. Separately, the **Russell 2000's +19% YTD versus the S&P's +12%** is a magnificent run that depends on nominal growth staying strong *and* financing conditions not tightening further. Small caps carry more floating-rate debt than large caps; a September hike plus a 5%+ 10-year would hit that 700bp of outperformance disproportionately, and a small-cap unwind would drag the mid-cap software names in today's slate with it regardless of their individual fundamentals.

## Bottom Line

The August jobs report was strong enough to keep a September hike on the table and Waller's Thursday guidance was dovish enough to keep it from being a certainty — so the market gets three more weeks of coin-flip Fed risk with a VIX near 14, which is the cheapest hedging opportunity on the board. Inside equities, the actionable dislocation is in enterprise software, where a market that has stopped paying for revenue multiples and started underwriting cash flows has left a bottom quartile trading at 1–2x sales while it pays 22% single-day premiums for acceleration: Guidewire lost nearly 15% for guiding ARR growth to 18% instead of 19%, on a quarter it beat.

The response is to own profitable software cheaply rather than to fight the regime — **PEGA at ~10.6x forward with $575M of free cash flow and QLYS at 8.7x EV/FCF with 46% EBITDA margins** are the kind of positions that survive a hike, with **ESTC's +28% RPO** as the growth expression at a below-median multiple and DT, MANH and DSGX accumulated rather than chased. Two things this slate does not do: it does not diversify out of software, and it does not hedge the Hormuz tail — an oil spike would force the Fed's hand and compress every multiple in it at once, and any investor implementing it should hold that exposure knowingly.
