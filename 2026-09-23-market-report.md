## Macro & Monetary Environment

**The Fed has pivoted, and the market has not fully repriced.** The September 16 FOMC decision lifted the target range to 3.75%–4.00% — a quarter point, unanimous, and the first increase since 2023. This is not a hawkish hold or a skipped cut; it is a directional reversal, and it deserves more weight than a market making record highs is currently giving it.

The justification is straightforward and sits in the inflation data:

- **Core PCE near 3.4%**, well above the 2% target and no longer describable as transitory or as a services-lag artifact.
- **Headline PCE revised up to 3.7%**, with the Committee's own median 2026 projection now at 3.7% headline / 3.4% core.
- **Energy pass-through** from the U.S.–Iran conflict is explicitly cited as elevating near-term price pressures. The Dallas Fed has published working-paper research (wp2609) on precisely this channel.
- The projected **return to 2% has been pushed further out the horizon** — the Committee is no longer forecasting a near-term convergence.

Critically, the Fed is not hiking into weakness. The statement language describes economic activity expanding at a solid pace, resilient domestic spending, strong productivity growth, robust capital investment, and job gains keeping pace with the workforce. This is a central bank tightening because the economy can take it and inflation demands it — which is a materially different regime from 2022's hike-into-slowdown.

**What the dot plot implies:** the median SEP path points to roughly one more quarter-point increase before year-end, landing near **4.1% for end-2026**. That is the single most important number for equity multiples over the next quarter.

**Dollar and rates:** the dollar steadied near its strongest level in two months on Wednesday on rate-hike prospects. A firming dollar is a headwind for large-cap multinationals' translated earnings and for emerging markets, and a relative tailwind for domestically-focused small and mid-caps — consistent with the Russell's YTD outperformance noted above.

**What to watch this week:** the next PCE print is the event that matters. A core reading that fails to decelerate validates the second hike and pressures long-duration equities; a cooler print lets the market argue the September move was a one-and-done insurance hike. Also watch the front end of the curve for confirmation that the market is pricing the December meeting correctly — the gap between SEP guidance and market-implied pricing has been a reliable source of volatility all year.

**Investment implication:** in a rising-rate, above-target-inflation regime, the market pays for *near-term realized cash flow* and *contracted revenue visibility*, and discounts *terminal-value promises*. Every pick in today's report is chosen against that filter — companies with signed backlog converting to revenue inside 24 months, not companies whose value sits in a 2032 TAM slide.

## Geopolitical Factors

**The U.S.–Iran conflict remains the dominant geopolitical risk**, and it has been an active fuel crisis, not a background tension, for most of 2026.

The mechanics of the risk:

- **The Strait of Hormuz** carries over 20% of global oil trade. Closure — which has occurred during this conflict — plus attacks on energy infrastructure inside Iran and in several GCC countries produced a large, genuine disruption to global supply. This is a physical supply shock, not a risk-premium story.
- **Price path in 2026 has been violent:** Brent peaked near **$118 in late March**, collapsed to roughly **$70 by July 1**, rebounded above **$100 by late July**, oscillated between **$87 and $97 through August**, and surged as high as **$109 in early September** on renewed attacks on shipping and energy infrastructure. That is a ~70% peak-to-trough-to-peak round trip inside six months.
- **Today's direction is favorable:** oil is easing on hopes for a diplomatic breakthrough to end the Middle East war, and that easing is part of why equity futures are steady and why yields paused their climb.

**The economic cost is quantified.** J.P. Morgan Global Research estimates that if Brent remains elevated through mid-year, global GDP growth for the first half of 2026 could be depressed by an annualized 0.6%. Allianz has published formal scenario analysis on the conflict's market implications. There are also **second-order shortages in fertilizer and food security** tied to energy input costs — a channel that runs straight through the materials complex.

**The second front is trade.** Investors are watching a **looming Trump–Xi meeting**, which sits alongside the Iran diplomacy as the two binary events that could reprice risk assets in either direction on short notice. A constructive outcome on both would be a powerful risk-on catalyst; failure on both simultaneously is the bear case.

**Domestically, a new political risk has surfaced for the AI trade specifically:** the House has passed a bill to shield consumers from data center costs. The politics of who pays for the grid buildout — ratepayers or hyperscalers — is becoming legislative rather than theoretical. This is directionally favorable for merchant generators and for behind-the-meter/co-located power structures, and unfavorable for regulated utilities hoping to socialize AI-driven capex across the ratebase. It is a live variable in today's spotlight.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: AI Infrastructure & Data Centers — Power, Cooling, Networking, Interconnect & Memory

*(Rotation: day-of-year 266 → 266 mod 6 = 2 → AI Infrastructure & Data Centers)*

This is the physical layer beneath the AI trade: the switchgear, the chillers, the fiber, the drives and the megawatts. It is where the capex actually lands, and — critically for a valuation-driven report — it is where you can still find contracted, visible revenue at multiples that do not require heroic assumptions.

#### The capex arithmetic

Hyperscaler 2026 data center capex runs approximately **$330 billion**, and the split is the most useful number in this entire report: roughly **60% flows to silicon and 40% to physical infrastructure** — real estate, power, cooling, networking. That 40% is roughly **$130 billion of annual spend** on things that are poured, bolted, wired and cooled.

Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta are collectively expected to spend hundreds of billions in 2026, with a large share directed at data centers, servers, networking, power systems and cooling. The demand is not speculative. It shows up as signed backlog.

This is what a genuine multi-year industrial supercycle looks like in the order book, and it is the strongest available evidence that the electricity investment cycle has moved from *forecast demand* into *contracted, prepaid, multi-year commitments*.

#### Thermal: the liquid-cooling inflection

The most important structural statistic in the cooling sub-sector: **fewer than 10% of data centers are liquid-cooled today**, and every GPU generation pushes that number higher. The transition from "optional upgrade" to "mandatory standard" is the inflection point, and it benefits Vertiv, Modine, nVent, AAON and SPX Technologies.

The economics are already visible. SPX Technologies grew data center revenue from **$200 million in 2025 to a guided $350 million in 2026** (+~50%), and has stated an eventual annual data center revenue *capacity* of approximately **$1.1 billion at full production** — roughly triple the current run rate. AAON's BASX-branded data center cooling orders rose 160% year over year.

But thermal is also where execution risk is concentrating. AAON **raised** its 2026 revenue growth outlook to 40%–45% and the stock **fell 10.2%**, because gross margin slipped to 24.3% and full-year margin guidance was cut to 25%–26%. SPXC similarly fell 6.6% after raising 2026 revenue guidance, on higher leverage. The market has stopped paying for growth in this sub-sector and started demanding that the growth arrive at acceptable margins and without balance-sheet strain. That is a healthy development for valuation-driven buyers and it is creating the entry points.

**The equipment suppliers have massively outperformed while the independent power producers have been taken out and shot.** Both groups are levered to the same demand driver. The market has decided that selling the turbines and switchgear is a better business than owning the generation, and it has expressed that view with a 60–100 percentage point spread inside nine months.

There is a reasonable argument embedded in that spread — equipment makers have visible, contracted, prepaid backlog while merchant generators carry commodity and regulatory exposure. But the magnitude looks like an overshoot, particularly for the generators that have already converted their exposure from merchant to contracted. Vistra has **3,809 MW of nuclear under 20-year contract** to Amazon (1,200 MW at Comanche Peak) and Meta (2,609 MW in PJM). That is not merchant risk. That is a utility-grade annuity signed by two of the best credits on earth, and the stock is down 13.6% YTD.

The scarcity argument favors existing nuclear and gas generation sited in load-growth regions, precisely because hyperscalers need large, reliable, carbon-free blocks of power and cannot wait for new construction. The recent House bill on consumer cost protection strengthens rather than weakens this: if ratepayer socialization of data center costs becomes politically untenable, hyperscalers must contract directly with generators — which is the exact structure Vistra has already built.

#### Memory & storage: the tightest link in the chain

This is the sub-sector where demand most clearly exceeds supply, with quantified numbers:

- **Western Digital's entire 2026 HDD production is sold out.** Firm purchase orders from its top seven customers are secured through 2026, with multi-year deals extending into 2027–2028.
- **Morgan Stanley projects HDD supply falls 10%–15% short of demand in 2026**, with AI-driven demand growth of 40%–50% outpacing supply growth of 30%–35%, and **the shortage persisting until at least 2028**.
- Hard drive prices have surged as much as 50% as AI data centers buy out supply.
- Seagate's nearline exabyte capacity is largely allocated and locked through 2027, with planning into 2028.

Nearline storage is the least glamorous and most genuinely supply-constrained part of the AI buildout. Every inference workload generates data that must be retained cheaply, and there is no substitute at the capacity tier.

#### Interconnect & optics: the sub-sector to admire, not to buy

The demand case is impeccable. Copper interconnect is hitting hard power, distance and bandwidth limits as clusters scale, forcing more optical links per unit of compute. Data center interconnect spending should climb from about **$15 billion in 2025 to $26 billion by 2030**. NVIDIA validated the thesis with capital on March 2, 2026, investing **$2 billion in Lumentum** alongside multi-year, multi-billion-dollar purchase commitments, plus an additional **$2 billion committed to Coherent**.

And the valuations have completely run away from it. **Lumentum is up 166% YTD and 1,269% over one year, at a 159x P/E.** Coherent is +89.9% YTD, Credo +77%, Astera Labs +59%.

A 159x multiple on a cyclical component supplier, after a 13-bagger, is not a valuation-based investment — it is a momentum position wearing a thesis. This report will not recommend it. **Correct thesis, wrong price** is the most expensive mistake available in this market, and optics is where it is on offer today.

#### Data center REITs

Equinix raised 2026 AFFO guidance to **$4.20–$4.28 billion**, implying 9%–11% YoY growth; Digital Realty guides to roughly 8% core FFO growth. DLR's Q1 2026 produced $707 million in annualized GAAP base rent bookings at 100% share, anchored by a **200 MW AI inference lease — the largest hyperscale deal in company history**. Lease structures have shifted decisively in the landlords' favor: 15-year terms are now the norm, and tenants pre-lease 24–36 months before delivery.

Valuation signals here are genuinely contradictory — one source puts EQIX at 38–40x P/AFFO against a 22–26x peer median, another has it at 18.4x forward AFFO and 86% of NAV, and a blended intrinsic-value model implies ~8% downside from $1,111.65. When credible sources disagree by a factor of two on the multiple, that is a signal to demand a wider margin of safety, not to pick the flattering number. No REIT pick today.

#### Spotlight conclusion

The physical layer of the AI buildout has better revenue visibility than the silicon layer and, in selected names, materially better valuation support. The discipline required is to avoid the two crowded extremes: the optics complex at triple-digit multiples on one end, and the assumption that every contractor with "data center" in its deck deserves 40x on the other. The opportunity sits in the middle — contracted backlog at high-teens-to-high-20s forward multiples — and in the dislocated generator complex.

### Artificial Intelligence

The model layer has turned into an outright price war, and that is the most consequential AI development of the month for equity investors.

**The releases, compressed into three weeks:**

- **OpenAI launched GPT-6 Sol and Luna on Tuesday**, priced at **half the GPT-5.6 tier** — $2/$10 per million tokens for Sol versus $4/$20 previously — positioned as "cut from the same cloth" as flagship Astra.
- **Anthropic launched Claude Opus 5.5 on September 22** at $4/M input and $20/M output, roughly **40% cheaper** than the prior Opus.
- **Meta released Muse Spark 1.3**, its most powerful model to date, with paid developer access.

**Meta's agent push is working.** Muse, a personal AI agent running on a secure approval-based virtual machine, has expanded to macOS with direct access to user files and applications, and has surpassed **500,000 total users, 250,000 daily actives, and 2 million prompts in its first week**. Tuesday's strong reception for Muse was the specific catalyst that lifted the AI infrastructure complex.

**Competitive dynamics are genuinely fluid.** Reports suggest OpenAI's Astra has captured significant enterprise AI spending share, and Anthropic is weighing a counter-launch — notable given its CEO's public calls to slow AI development. That tension between commercial pressure and stated safety posture is now a market-relevant variable, not just a philosophical one.

**Funding:** Mirendil, founded by former Anthropic researchers to build self-improving AI, is in talks to raise roughly **$1 billion led by Kleiner Perkins at a $5 billion valuation** — evidence that private capital for frontier labs remains abundant despite public-market skepticism about model-layer economics.

**The investment read-through is the point.** Aggressive price cuts at the model layer — 40% to 50% in a single cycle — compress margins for anyone selling tokens and simultaneously *increase* aggregate inference volume. Cheaper tokens mean more tokens consumed, which means more GPUs, more power, more cooling and more storage. **Model-layer price deflation is infrastructure-layer volume inflation.** This is the core argument for owning the picks-and-shovels rather than the models, and it is why today's spotlight and today's picks sit where they do. Microsoft slashing Copilot prices up to 50% for enterprise clients while preparing a new AI "super app" is the same dynamic expressed at the application layer.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

**Semis are the funding source for the current rotation.** Broadcom reported fiscal Q3 after the close on September 2 with a weaker-than-expected AI chip outlook and — critically — **reiterated rather than raised** its 2026 guidance. Shares fell as much as 15% and the selloff rippled across the sector, hitting Intel, AMD and Micron, as investors took profits after a blistering rally.

The lesson is about expectations, not fundamentals. In a sector priced for acceleration, *maintaining* guidance is received as a downgrade. That asymmetry — where only an upward revision avoids punishment — is characteristic of late-stage sector positioning and argues for caution on semiconductor multiples even where the underlying business is fine.

**The structural picture remains intact:** hyperscaler capex is expanding, memory supply is tight, and custom silicon backlogs stretch visibility into 2028. NVIDIA posted a blowout July quarter and remains the merchant GPU platform; Broadcom is the custom silicon and networking arm of the same buildout; Micron sits on the memory bottleneck that gates both. **Micron reports September 30** and is the next major catalyst for the group — it will be read as the definitive statement on whether memory pricing power is holding.

The memory and storage bottleneck is the most quantifiable constraint in the chain, and it is where this report is taking its hardware exposure — through the storage tier, where supply is contractually sold out through 2026 and the shortage is forecast to persist to 2028, rather than through the logic tier, where the expectations bar has become punishing.

The market is paying for growth and refusing to pay for cost control. Beating on the bottom line while growing below 5% is not a viable equity story in this tape.

### 1. DY — Dycom Industries, Inc. *(Telecom & data center infrastructure services — fiber, power solutions, building systems)*

**Thesis:** Dycom's Power Solutions acquisition pivoted a steady fiber-to-the-home contractor into a direct AI data center play, with over 90% of that segment tied to hyperscaler demand management describes as "absolutely insatiable" — and the market is still valuing the whole company at a discount to its slower-growing peers. A record **$12.24 billion backlog** at a **2.2x book-to-bill** ratio against FY27 revenue guidance of $7.38–$7.65 billion means roughly 1.6 years of contracted work already signed.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **Forward P/E: 16.7x** — versus MasTec at 21.68x and an industry average of 19.08x
- Trailing P/E: elevated on the acquisition-year comparison; forward is the relevant figure given the Power Solutions step-change
- P/S: ~0.5x on FY27 guidance midpoint of ~$7.5B — this is a services business and screens accordingly
- EV/EBITDA: low-to-mid teens, modestly below the specialty contractor group
- **PEG: ~0.40** (16.7x forward against 42.1% projected EPS growth) — the most attractive growth-adjusted valuation in this report by a wide margin

**Growth Profile:** Q1 FY27 contract revenue **+56.1% YoY to $1.96 billion**, with **organic growth of 24.7%** — the growth is not merely acquired. Full-year organic contract revenue rose 16.7%. Backlog **+46.5%** to a record. Projected **EPS growth of 42.1% in 2026 and 37.2% in 2027**. Guidance was raised alongside the record backlog.

**Competitive Moat:** Scale in skilled-trade labor is the binding constraint in this industry, not capital or technology. Dycom fields one of the largest trained workforces in North American telecom and utility construction, holds multi-year master service agreements with carriers and hyperscalers that renew on incumbency, and has geographic density that makes it the low-cost bidder in the markets it already serves. A new entrant cannot hire its way into this business in under a decade.

**Key Risk:** Customer concentration and project timing. Revenue is concentrated among a handful of large carriers and hyperscalers, and a single customer deferring a program can move a quarter materially. The book-to-bill of 2.2x provides cushion, but backlog is a contractual right to perform work, not a guarantee of when it converts. Labor cost inflation in skilled trades is the secondary risk — it compresses margins on fixed-price work.

**Rating: Strong Buy.** The cheapest forward multiple among the major infrastructure contractors attached to the fastest organic growth and the highest book-to-bill. The valuation gap to MasTec is not justified by any difference in backlog quality.

### 2. WDC — Western Digital Corporation *(Data storage — nearline HDD for hyperscale AI)*

**Thesis:** Western Digital has **sold out its entire 2026 HDD production capacity**, with firm purchase orders from its top seven customers and multi-year agreements extending into 2027–2028, against a market Morgan Stanley expects to remain 10%–15% undersupplied through at least 2028. Post-SanDisk separation, this is a pure-play on the single most supply-constrained physical input to AI infrastructure.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **Trailing P/E: ~17x** — *with an important caveat below*
- Forward P/E: high-teens to low-20s depending on how aggressively the analyst community models 2027 pricing
- P/S: ~3x on FY26 revenue of $12.92B (share count ~350M at $461)
- EV/EBITDA: low-to-mid teens, reflecting a cleaner post-separation balance sheet
- PEG: below 1.0 on consensus forward growth
- **Caveat, stated plainly:** FY26 reported earnings of $9.29 billion on $12.92 billion of revenue — a 403.58% increase — is not a sustainable operating margin. That figure includes one-time gains associated with the SanDisk separation. **Underwrite this on operating earnings, not on the headline trailing P/E of 17x**, which is flattered by the separation accounting. The investment case survives that adjustment, but the multiple is not as cheap as the screen suggests, and anyone who buys it on the screen alone has not done the work.

**Growth Profile:** FY26 revenue **$12.92 billion, +35.7%** from $9.52 billion. **89% of revenue now comes from hyperscaler AI cloud** versus just 5% from consumer — a complete transformation of the business mix and its cyclicality profile. HDD exabyte shipments surpassed 450EB by late 2025 and remained elevated into 2026, with AI-driven demand growing 40%–50% against supply growth of 30%–35%. Drive prices have risen as much as 50%.

**Competitive Moat:** The nearline HDD market is a **two-player oligopoly** (Western Digital and Seagate) with capacity that takes years and enormous capital to add — and neither participant has any incentive to add it aggressively after a decade of destructive price competition. There is no substitute technology at the capacity tier: NAND is an order of magnitude too expensive per terabyte for cold and warm storage at exabyte scale. Multi-year contracted volumes with the top seven customers convert a historically brutal commodity cycle into something closer to a contracted industrial business. WD is extending ePMR before its own HAMR product ships in 2027.

**Key Risk:** **This is still a cyclical, and the stock has already run hard** — it traded between $437.68 and $474.54 on September 23 at $461.01. Every prior HDD upcycle has ended with the industry adding capacity into peak demand and destroying pricing. The specific risk is a hyperscaler capex digestion phase in 2027–2028 arriving at the same moment HAMR capacity comes online. Sizing should reflect that this is a supply-cycle trade with a long runway, not a permanent compounder. Sentiment is also stretched: 26 analysts average a "Buy" with a 12-month target of $664.92, implying 50.65% upside — when the sell side is that uniformly positive after a move this large, the marginal buyer is already in.

**Rating: Buy.** Contracted, sold-out capacity into a structurally undersupplied market is the highest-visibility revenue in the AI hardware chain. Size it as a cyclical, buy it on weakness rather than chasing the highs, and underwrite the operating earnings rather than the headline multiple.

### 3. SPXC — SPX Technologies, Inc. *(Thermal management & data center cooling, plus detection & measurement)*

**Thesis:** SPX is converting from an industrial HVAC conglomerate into a data center cooling platform, guiding data center revenue from $200 million in 2025 to **$350 million in 2026 (+50%)** against a stated eventual capacity of approximately **$1.1 billion at full production** — and the stock **fell 6.6% on the guidance raise** over leverage concerns, creating the entry point. The non-data-center businesses provide a stable earnings base that pure-play cooling names lack.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **Forward P/E: ~27x NTM** — below Trane Technologies at 30x, despite materially faster data center growth
- 2026 adjusted EPS guidance: **$8.20–$8.60** (raised)
- 2026 revenue guidance: **$2.575–$2.645 billion**
- **2026 Adjusted EBITDA guidance: $630–$660 million**, approximately **+27% YoY at the midpoint** (raised from an initial $600–$625M)
- EV/EBITDA: high-teens on 2026 guidance — a premium to the industrial group, a discount to data-center pure-plays
- PEG: roughly 1.0 against ~27% EBITDA growth

**Growth Profile:** Q1 2026 non-GAAP adjusted EPS of **$1.69 versus $1.56 consensus (+8% beat)**. HVAC segment revenue **$394.0 million, +22%**; Detection & Measurement **$172.8 million, +8.3%**. Guidance has been raised twice during 2026 — in April and again in July. Data center sales expected up approximately 50% in 2026, with the $1.1 billion capacity figure implying roughly a tripling from the 2026 run rate over time.

**Competitive Moat:** Evaporative and hybrid cooling at hyperscale is an engineering and qualification business, not a commodity one. Once a cooling architecture is designed into a data center template and qualified by the operator, it is replicated across the fleet — switching costs are structural and the incumbent captures the entire build program. SPX's Marley and recently-acquired cooling brands hold long-established positions in critical-application thermal, and the Detection & Measurement segment provides genuinely uncorrelated cash flow that funds the cooling capacity expansion without equity issuance.

**Key Risk:** **Leverage and margin execution.** The 6.6% drop on a guidance raise was specifically about higher leverage from acquisitions funding the capacity buildout. The sector-wide warning is AAON: it raised revenue guidance 40%–45% and fell 10.2% because gross margin slipped to 24.3% and full-year margin guidance was cut. Rapid capacity expansion in thermal has repeatedly produced start-up cost drag — SPX's own Q1 HVAC margin fell roughly 40bps on start-up costs. If data center revenue triples toward $1.1 billion while incremental margins disappoint, the multiple compresses regardless of the revenue growth. One source flags that a pullback into the low-to-mid $150s would put the stock in genuine margin-of-safety territory, which is a fair statement about where the risk/reward improves further.

**Rating: Buy.** Cheaper than Trane on forward earnings with a faster data center ramp, a real second business, and a recent guidance-raise selloff that has already reset expectations on leverage.

### 4. POWL — Powell Industries, Inc. *(Electrical equipment — custom switchgear, power control rooms, circuit breakers)*

**Thesis:** Powell's order book has reached a point of near-total visibility: a **$2.4 billion backlog** with roughly one-third tied to data centers, **2027 essentially fully booked**, and the company now **taking orders into 2028**. Approximately 55% of backlog converts to revenue within 12 months, which makes the next four quarters close to arithmetic rather than forecast.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **Forward P/E: 26.3x** (as of September 5), roughly **28x 2027 earnings**
- Trailing P/E: 34.7x
- Industrial Products industry median forward P/E: 16.96x — Powell trades **55.2% above** the group
- TTM revenue **$1.16 billion**, TTM net income **$190.86 million** — a ~16.5% net margin, exceptional for custom electrical equipment
- P/S: ~2.3x; EV/EBITDA mid-to-high teens, supported by a **net cash balance sheet**
- Next-year EPS estimate **$15.08** versus $14.98 — roughly flat, which is the crux of the bear case

**Growth Profile:** Revenue and margin have inflected hard on the data center and power-infrastructure cycle; the stock is **+79.2% YTD** through late August and was up 87% YTD as of late July at $219.72. Management's Q3 FY2026 commentary confirms the North American power infrastructure cycle is being driven simultaneously by data center generation *and* distribution — two independent demand vectors, not one.

**Competitive Moat:** Custom-engineered switchgear and power control rooms are safety-critical, long-lead-time, heavily specified equipment. Utilities, refiners and data center operators qualify vendors over years and rarely switch, because a switchgear failure takes the entire facility down. Powell's installed base generates a recurring high-margin service and retrofit annuity, and the lead times themselves are the moat — with 2027 booked and 2028 opening, a competitor cannot take share from Powell in 2027 at any price, because Powell's capacity is already sold and the customer's alternative is to wait.

**Key Risk:** **Valuation is the thesis risk, and it is a serious one.** At 26–28x forward against a 17x industry median, with next-year EPS guided roughly flat at $15.08 versus $14.98, the multiple is discounting a growth reacceleration that consensus estimates do not yet reflect. GuruFocus puts GF Value at $86.03 and rates the stock significantly overvalued. The bull case rests on backlog conversion at maintained margins; if the flat EPS estimate proves correct, a 28x multiple on a no-growth year is indefensible and the stock derates hard. This is also a capital-goods cyclical: the order book that extends to 2028 today can be deferred if hyperscaler capex plans change. The average analyst target of $280 implies ~55% upside, but that target embeds the reacceleration.

**Rating: Accumulate.** The business quality and the visibility merit a Buy; the multiple against a flat near-term EPS estimate does not. Scale in on weakness, size it below the other positions, and require the next two quarters to show backlog converting at maintained margins before adding. The net cash balance sheet limits the downside scenario but does not justify paying up.

### 5. VST — Vistra Corp. *(Independent power producer — contracted nuclear & gas generation)*

**Thesis:** This is the deliberate contrarian position in today's report. Vistra has **3,809 MW of nuclear under 20-year contract** to Amazon (1,200 MW at Comanche Peak) and Meta (2,609 MW in PJM) — converting merchant generation into a utility-grade annuity signed by two of the strongest credits on earth — and the stock is **down 13.6% year to date** while the equipment suppliers selling into the same demand are up 40%–80%. The market has priced the shovel sellers for a supercycle and the power sellers for a recession.

**Valuation Metrics:**
- **EV/EBITDA: 11.40x** (at $137.37, August 31) — a reasonable absolute multiple for contracted generation
- Trailing P/E: 26.8x, a 34% premium to sector median — though this understates the case, since the contracted PPA cash flows have not yet entered results
- EV/FCF: 33.81x — the least attractive metric in the set, and the honest weak point
- Net margins: 11.5%
- **2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance: $6.8–$7.6 billion**, which **excludes** benefits from the pending Cogentrix acquisition *and* the signed nuclear agreements; consensus sits at $7.34 billion
- 2027 guided opportunity: **$7.4–$7.8 billion** ongoing-operations adjusted EBITDA midpoint, still excluding Cogentrix and Meta — which the CFO indicates **could add $700 million more**

**Growth Profile:** Q2 2026 adjusted EBITDA **+30% to $1.77 billion**. Management guides to **20.4% FCF growth in FY26**, excluding potential upside from new PPAs. The critical timing point: **only part of the PPA contribution enters results during 2027** — Meta PPA cash flows begin in 2027, Cogentrix contributes in 2027, and the full run-rate arrives later. The guidance that investors are valuing the stock against systematically excludes the contracts that constitute the investment thesis.

**Competitive Moat:** Existing nuclear generation sited in load-growth regions is the scarcest asset in American power. It cannot be replicated — new nuclear takes a decade-plus and costs multiples of the replacement value of the existing fleet — and it is the only source of large, reliable, carbon-free blocks that satisfy hyperscaler procurement requirements. The regulatory and political environment is moving in Vistra's favor: the House bill shielding consumers from data center costs makes ratepayer socialization harder, which pushes hyperscalers toward exactly the direct bilateral contracting structure Vistra has already executed.

**Key Risk:** **The 2027 timing gap is the whole problem.** Meta PPA cash flows, Cogentrix contribution and the associated EBITDA step-up arrive in 2027 and beyond, while the stock must be held through a period where reported results do not yet reflect them — and a −13.6% YTD return demonstrates the market's willingness to discount future contracted cash flow when it is not yet in the P&L. Residual merchant and retail exposure remains outside the contracted nuclear block, leaving commodity and weather sensitivity. EV/FCF of 33.81x is genuinely expensive and reflects heavy capital commitments. Rising rates are a direct headwind to a capital-intensive, leveraged generator. And the sector-wide drawdown — Talen −17.9%, Constellation −20.8%, NRG −28.0% — indicates the de-rating is thematic, so it can persist well past the point where it is fundamentally justified.

**Rating: Buy.** The contracted nuclear block is worth more than the market is paying for the entire equity, the guidance excludes the thesis, and the 60–100 point performance gap versus the equipment suppliers is an overshoot. Underwrite it as a 2027 story bought at 2026 prices, and size it accepting that the re-rating may take several quarters to arrive.

### Portfolio Construction Notes

**Diversity mandate compliance.** Five picks across **five distinct sub-sectors**: infrastructure services (DY), data storage hardware (WDC), thermal management (SPXC), electrical equipment (POWL), merchant power generation (VST). This exceeds the three-sub-sector minimum. **Zero companies above $1 trillion market cap** — the largest is WDC at roughly $160 billion, and POWL is under $3 billion. **All five picks are outside the Magnificent Seven**, exceeding the two-name minimum.

**Deliberate omissions and why.** This report declines the two most crowded corners of its own spotlight sector. **Optics and interconnect** (Lumentum at 159x P/E after a 1,269% one-year move, Coherent +90% YTD, Credo +77%, Astera Labs +59%) has an impeccable demand thesis at a price that removes all margin of safety — NVIDIA's $2B investments validate the sector, not the multiple. **Comfort Systems (FIX)** has the single best backlog in the group at $14.06 billion (+73%) and 53.1% first-half revenue growth, but trades at **43.8x forward earnings, more than double the sector median** — a superb business at a price that requires everything to go right. **Argan (AGX)** at 38.04x forward and 24.91x EV/EBITDA fails the same test despite doubling its backlog. **EnerSys (ENS)** at 28.9x screens roughly 16% above fair-value estimates. **AAON** was excluded specifically because its raised guidance came with a gross margin decline to 24.3% and a full-year margin cut — SPXC offers similar exposure with a second business funding the ramp. **Data center REITs** were excluded because credible valuation sources disagree by roughly a factor of two on EQIX's AFFO multiple; that dispersion demands a wider margin of safety than current prices offer.

**Risk balance across the sleeve.** Three picks (DY, SPXC, POWL) are order-book businesses whose primary risk is margin execution on rapid capacity expansion — a correlated risk, and deliberately the largest weight given the visibility. WDC is a supply-cycle position with different drivers and different failure modes. VST is the explicit contrarian leg, negatively correlated to the sentiment that has driven the other four. If the AI capex narrative breaks, DY/SPXC/POWL fall together and WDC follows; VST is the only position that could plausibly benefit from a rotation toward contracted, defensive cash flow. That is intentional, but investors should size the three order-book names as one thematic exposure rather than three independent ideas.

**Valuation discipline, stated honestly.** Two of these picks are not cheap. POWL at 26–28x forward against a 17x industry median and a flat next-year EPS estimate is rated **Accumulate rather than Buy** for exactly that reason. WDC's headline 17x trailing P/E is **flattered by one-time SanDisk separation gains** and must be underwritten on operating earnings. Only DY, at 16.7x forward against 42% projected EPS growth, is cheap on a standalone basis. In a market where the Fed is hiking into 3.4% core inflation, paying above-median multiples requires contracted revenue as the justification — which is the common thread across all five.

**Ticker-collision checks.** **DY** (Dycom Industries, NYSE) is a two-letter symbol and the most dangerous in today's list: it must not be confused with **DYN**, with **DAY** (Dayforce), or with **DXYN**; note also that Dycom's fiscal year is offset — its "fiscal 2027" is the year ending January 2027, so **FY27 guidance describes calendar 2026 work**, and comparing it against peers' calendar-2027 estimates is a real analytical error. **WDC** (Western Digital) is now a **pure-play HDD company following the SanDisk separation** — older data feeds, sell-side models and SEC filing headers still carry the combined entity's financials, and **SNDK** (Sandisk) is a separate listed company whose results must not be blended in; WDC is also distinct from **WD** and **WDFC** (WD-40 Company), a completely unrelated specialty chemicals business that is routinely confused with it in screens and order-entry systems. **SPXC** is **SPX Technologies** (NYSE) — distinct from **SPX** (the S&P 500 index symbol in most quote systems, not a tradable equity), from **SPXL/SPXS** (leveraged index ETFs), and from **FLOW**, the symbol of the former SPX FLOW after its 2015 spin-off and subsequent take-private; searching "SPX" will return index data, not the company. **POWL** (Powell Industries, Nasdaq) is distinct from **PWR** (Quanta Services) and **POWI** (Power Integrations) — the POWL/POWI pair is a live transposition risk since both are Nasdaq-listed power-related names, and **PWR** appears in today's spotlight data as a separate company (+43.1% YTD). **VST** (Vistra, NYSE) is distinct from **VSTO**, **VST.TO**, and critically from **VRT** (Vertiv, **picked September 11 and inside the one-month look-back**) — both are AI-power-complex names and confusing them would break the rotation. Verify CUSIPs on DY and WDC in particular before entry.

## Risk Factors

**1. The Fed is hiking into an equity market at record highs, and the market is not positioned for it. (Severity: High)**
A unanimous 12–0 vote, core PCE at 3.4%, headline at 3.7%, and a median path pointing to one more hike to ~4.1% by year-end. The VIX at 14.21 says the market is not hedging this. The mechanism of damage is multiple compression, and it falls hardest on the longest-duration equities — which, after a year of AI-driven re-rating, is a large share of index weight. A core PCE print that fails to decelerate is the trigger.

**2. Strait of Hormuz and the oil round trip. (Severity: High)**
Over 20% of global oil trade transits the Strait, closure has already occurred during this conflict, and Brent has traveled from $118 to $70 to $109 within six months. J.P. Morgan estimates sustained elevated prices could cut first-half global GDP growth by an annualized 0.6%. This risk is uniquely dangerous *because* it is bidirectional and currently easing — the market is pricing the diplomatic breakthrough, which means the downside surprise is larger than the upside. An energy re-spike also directly feeds the inflation data driving Risk 1, creating a compounding scenario.

**3. AI capex digestion — the risk that governs today's entire pick list. (Severity: High)**
Every pick in this report depends on hyperscaler capex near $330 billion continuing. Backlogs stretching to 2028 and 2031 are contracts, not certainties; capital-goods order books get deferred. The early warning signal has already appeared: **Broadcom reiterated rather than raised 2026 guidance and lost 15%**, dragging the sector. Aggressive model-layer price cuts — OpenAI halving GPT-6 pricing, Anthropic cutting Opus 5.5 by ~40% — increase inference volume but compress the economics of the companies funding the buildout. If hyperscaler ROI on AI spend disappoints, the capex plans that constitute these backlogs are the first line item cut. **Micron's September 30 report is the near-term referendum.**

**4. Narrow breadth and record-high concentration. (Severity: Medium-High)**
Tuesday's session — Nasdaq record close, Dow down 185, S&P flat — is the structural signature of this market. When a handful of AI-levered names carry the index, a single disappointment transmits to the whole market with no offsetting leadership. The Russell 2000's +20.8% YTD is a genuine mitigant and suggests participation is broader than the mega-cap narrative implies, but the index-level risk remains concentrated.

**5. Binary event risk: the Trump–Xi meeting. (Severity: Medium)**
A scheduled, dated catalyst with materially asymmetric outcomes for supply chains, semiconductor export controls and tariff policy. Markets are trading steady into it, which means positioning is not defensive. Constructive resolution alongside Iran de-escalation is the melt-up case; failure on both simultaneously is the correction case.

**6. Thermal and contractor margin execution — the specific risk to this sleeve. (Severity: Medium)**
AAON raised revenue guidance 40%–45% and fell 10.2% on a gross margin slip to 24.3%. SPXC fell 6.6% on a guidance raise over leverage. FIX at 43.8x and AGX at 38x have no room for error. The pattern is consistent: the market now demands that data center growth arrive at acceptable margins without balance-sheet strain. Three of today's five picks (DY, SPXC, POWL) carry this correlated exposure.

## Bottom Line

The Fed has started hiking into 3.4% core inflation while equities sit at record highs on a VIX of 14 — a combination that says the market has not yet repriced the cost of capital, and that protection is cheap for anyone who wants it. In that regime, contracted revenue converting inside twenty-four months is worth more than any terminal-value story, which is why today's capital goes to the physical layer of the AI buildout rather than the model layer that is busy cutting its own prices by 40%–50%.

The specific opportunity is the dispersion *within* that layer: Dycom at 16.7x forward against 42% EPS growth and a 2.2x book-to-bill is genuinely mispriced relative to peers at 19–22x, while Comfort Systems at 43.8x and Lumentum at 159x are the same thesis at prices that have already paid it out. And the 60-to-100-point performance gap between the equipment suppliers (+40% to +79%) and the independent power producers (−13% to −28%) is the clearest dislocation on the board — Vistra's 3,809 MW contracted to Amazon and Meta is not merchant risk, and the guidance investors are valuing it against explicitly excludes it.

Own the backlog, insist on the margin, and buy the contracted cash flow the market is refusing to pay for.
