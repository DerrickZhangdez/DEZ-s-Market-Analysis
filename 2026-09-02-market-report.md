## Executive Summary

The market enters Wednesday's session on the back foot after a broad Tuesday selloff driven by three reinforcing pressures: renewed direct US–Iran hostilities, Brent crude pushing to roughly $95 a barrel, and a global bond selloff that lifted the 10-year Treasury yield to 4.79% and the 30-year to 5.27%. Our picks lean into that asymmetry through physical energy logistics, tubular goods, midstream cash flow, naval defense and agricultural inputs — while explicitly declining to chase the aggregates names that have already re-rated.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

The July FOMC held the target range at **3.50%–3.75%** on a **9–3 vote**. Three dissents is an unusually fractured committee, and the dissents pointed toward tightening, not easing. The statement language acknowledged that inflation "remains elevated relative to the Committee's 2% goal, in part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy" — a formulation that matters enormously right now, because it signals the Fed is *not* inclined to look through energy-driven inflation as transitory noise.

Core inflation is holding at **2.5%**. That is close enough to target that, in an ordinary cycle, the Fed would be cutting. It is not cutting, because the labor market has stayed resilient and because the energy shock is pushing in the wrong direction. Following the Chair's press conference, the CME FedWatch tool showed a **60.1% implied probability of a hike** at the September meeting. Forbes' Bill Conerly has argued explicitly that the Fed will hike a quarter point in September *despite* a cooler CPI print, on the grounds of persistent underlying pressure.

Investors should sit with how unusual this configuration is. Equities are near highs, the Russell 2000 is having its best year in 23 years, and the central bank is more likely than not to raise rates in two weeks. Leadership changes at the Fed have added a further layer of uncertainty to the reaction function — the committee's behavior is harder to model from historical precedent than at any point in recent memory.

**The long end is where the damage is.** The 10-year at 4.79% and the 30-year at 5.27% represent a genuine global bond selloff, not a repricing of Fed policy alone. A 5.27% 30-year yield is a direct, mechanical headwind to every long-duration equity valuation, which is precisely why the Nasdaq (−1.03%) underperformed the Dow (−0.79%) on Tuesday and why Nasdaq futures are the weakest of the three this morning.

**What to watch this week:** the September 16 FOMC decision is the dominant event, but the intervening CPI print will be read almost entirely through its energy component. Watch also for any pass-through from energy into food and utilities — the second-round effects are what would convert a supply shock into an inflation problem the Fed must answer with rates.

## Geopolitical Factors

This is the day's central story, and it is worth being precise about the mechanics rather than gesturing at "tensions."

The US and Iran exchanged direct attacks on Sunday, August 30 — the first direct exchange in roughly a month — and President Trump threatened further strikes on Monday. The conflict itself dates to late February 2026, so markets have had six months to adjust; what is new is the re-escalation after a period of relative calm, which resets the risk premium that had been decaying.

**Oil.** Brent rose 56 cents (0.6%) to $91.05 on September 1, with WTI up 83 cents (1.0%) to $86.59. By Wednesday Brent was trading around **$95**, its highest in nearly six weeks. BloombergNEF's framework had suggested $91 as a late-2026 disruption scenario; we are already through it.

**The Strait of Hormuz is the transmission mechanism.** Kpler data showed visible commodity vessel transits collapsing to **five per day** over the weekend. The waterway carried roughly a fifth of global oil supply before the conflict. Gulf crude exports have fallen from about 17 million barrels per day in 2025 to roughly **9 million bpd as of August 2026** — a 47% decline. Iran closed the Strait outright on March 2, driving Middle East–to–Asia VLCC rates to their highest levels since at least November 2005. The LR2 benchmark from the Middle East Gulf to Japan hit a record **$107.72 per ton on August 27**. Al Jazeera has documented a 95% drop in Hormuz traffic reshaping global shipping routes.

**The macro cost.** J.P. Morgan Research estimates that Brent remaining elevated could depress first-half 2026 global GDP growth by an annualized **0.6%**. That is a meaningful growth hit layered on top of an inflation impulse — the textbook definition of a supply shock, and the reason the Fed's job is so unpleasant right now.

**The investment implication, stated plainly.** When 20% of seaborne oil supply must reroute around a chokepoint, the economic rent accrues to whoever owns the physical assets that do the rerouting — tankers, tubular goods, pipelines, storage — not to the producers whose barrels are stranded. This is the analytical core of today's spotlight, and it is why our pick list looks the way it does.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Industrials, Energy, Materials & "Picks-and-Shovels" Plays

*Spotlight rotation: day-of-year 245, 245 mod 6 = 5 → bucket 5. The two prior visits to this bucket were July 28 (CF, TRGP, FLS, FLR, STLD) and August 3 (APD, LYB, MT, OKE, MLI). All ten of those names are deliberately excluded today, so this is the third consecutive fresh slate in this bucket.*

The organizing insight for this spotlight is that **the Hormuz disruption is a transfer of economic surplus from oil producers to oil logistics.** A barrel that cannot move through the Strait is worth less to its owner and more to whoever can carry it the long way around. Tanker ton-miles have exploded even as volumes have fallen, because the same cargo now travels much farther. This is not a speculative thesis; it is arithmetic showing up in freight rates that hit multi-decade highs.

**Marine energy transport — where the rent is actually landing.** International Seaways' June-quarter results are the cleanest evidence available. Blended spot TCE rates reached **$79,000 per day**, up from $27,500 in the year-ago quarter — a 187% increase. Profit-sharing arrangements lifted blended VLCC earnings across spot and time-charter vessels above **$150,000 per day**. The company posted record EBITDA of $345 million and record quarterly free cash flow of $261 million, nearly $100 million above its prior high. Critically, this is being converted to shareholders rather than reinvested into a newbuild cycle: the declared dividend was **$5.05 per share**, the largest in company history, representing at least 85% of adjusted net income. Balance sheet: net loan-to-value of **6%** — $250 million net debt against roughly $4 billion of asset values, with $935 million total liquidity. Teekay Tankers is the mid-size peer reference (Suezmax, Aframax, LR2) for investors who want the product-route exposure rather than the crude-route exposure.

**Tubular goods and pressure control.** Tenaris is the most defensible balance sheet in the energy equipment complex. Half-year 2026 revenue was **$6.07 billion** with profit of $1.06 billion; Q1 net sales rose 4% sequentially and 6% year-on-year to $3.1 billion with EBITDA of $735 million, holding margins at about **24%**. After a $606 million dividend in May and $90 million of buybacks in the first half, the company still held **$3.6 billion of net cash** at the end of June. EPS reached $1.01 in the half, flattered by the cancellation of 62.4 million treasury shares. The honest complication — and Tenaris disclosed it themselves — is that management warned of a near-term hit from Middle East disruption, since the company has real operational exposure to the region it also serves. Cactus (pressure control and spoolable technologies, $1.08 trailing EPS, $0.15 quarterly dividend with an August 31 ex-date) and Liberty Energy (North American pressure pumping, with an added enhanced-geothermal option) are the domestic-focused alternatives; both are more levered to US shale activity than to the Gulf.

**Midstream.** The sub-sector splits cleanly into premium growth and value cash flow. **DT Midstream** trades at 14.21x EV/EBITDA — a genuine premium — justified by a $3.4 billion brownfield backlog at 5x–8x build multiples and reaffirmed 2026 EBITDA guidance of $1,155–$1,225 million against an early 2027 outlook of $1,225–$1,295 million. Its pivot from gathering toward pipelines serving LNG, industrial, utility and data-center demand is strategically sound but fully priced. **Western Midstream** is the value side of the same trade: record Q2 EBITDA of $736.5 million, distributable cash flow of $537.2 million, and a full-year guidance raise to $2.75–$2.95 billion — a $250 million increase over the original range, with DCF guidance up $200 million, driven by the Brazos Delaware acquisition. It yields roughly 8% with high-single-digit EBITDA growth expected in 2027.

**Construction materials — and a pick we are declining to make.** JPMorgan upgraded **Martin Marietta** to Overweight from Neutral this morning with a $680 target, citing $85 million of cost synergies achievable by year two with upside, plus $100–$175 million of potential commercial synergies from the Lhoist North America acquisition closing in Q3. Full-year revenue guidance was raised to $7.2–$7.4 billion. The business quality is not in question — aggregates are a structurally advantaged, locally monopolistic asset class. **The valuation is.** MLM trades at roughly **34.5x trailing and 29.3x forward earnings**, against a Simply Wall St fair ratio of 23.5x, a US basic materials industry multiple of 22.2x and peers at 22.8x. DCF-based work suggesting the stock is 19.3% below fair value at $662.65 requires believing a long runway of synergy capture and infrastructure volume. Blended analyst targets sit near $700. We are covering MLM, not recommending it: a 29x forward multiple on a cyclical materials business, two weeks before a probable rate hike, is not a valuation-based pick — and this report's mandate is explicitly valuation-based. That discipline is the point.

**Agricultural inputs.** Potash is the bright spot and nitrogen is the drag. **Nutrien** posted Q2 net earnings of $1.22 billion ($2.53 diluted EPS), adjusted EBITDA of $2.43 billion and adjusted EPS of $2.61, and raised 2026 potash sales volume guidance to **14.2–14.8 million tonnes** on stronger first-half sales and better second-half order-book visibility, while maintaining its 74–77 million tonne global shipment forecast. Management describes fertilizer markets as structurally tight and has stepped up buybacks. Both EBITDA and net earnings still declined year-on-year, as higher benchmarks were offset by lower volumes and rising sulfur costs. **Mosaic** is the cautionary contrast and we are avoiding it: a Q1 net loss of $258 million including $442 million of charges from idling Araxá and Patrocínio in Brazil, EPS down more than 55% to roughly $0.20–$0.22, and a genuine margin squeeze from higher sulfur and ammonia costs against falling product prices. At $21.40 and a $6.8 billion market cap it is statistically cheap with a $27.19 consensus target, but it is cheap for a reason.

**Naval defense.** Huntington Ingalls is the industrial name where the geopolitical shock and the fundamentals point the same direction. Backlog reached **$57.3 billion**, up 7.9%. Submarine contract modifications for Virginia-class Block VI and Columbia-class totaled $76.6 billion in aggregate program value, of which approximately **$25 billion** flows to Newport News and $5.5 billion to Columbia. Newport News revenue rose 15.3% to $1.8 billion; Ingalls rose 16.7% to $845 million on amphibious assault ship volumes. Management raised 2026 shipbuilding revenue guidance by $500 million and lifted the low end of margin guidance to **6.0%–6.5%**, citing improved throughput and labor agreements aiding retention. Throughput improved 12% year-to-date against a 15% full-year target. HII trades near **15.6x earnings** — remarkable in a defense sector that Finviz has flatly characterized as "ultra expensive in 2026." Peer context: L3Harris at 31.5x trailing and 22.8x forward with a 2.20% yield; Leidos at 1.17x sales, down 20% from its YTD high on fears that AI commoditizes cybersecurity services, with a $208.27 consensus target.

### Artificial Intelligence

The AI capex story remains structurally intact but is increasingly a **credit** story rather than an equity story, and that distinction deserves more attention than it gets. The Nvidia–OpenAI strategic partnership, originally announced in September 2025, covers at least 10 gigawatts of AI data centers — millions of GPUs — with Nvidia intending to invest up to $100 billion progressively as each gigawatt is deployed. The first gigawatt lands in the second half of 2026 on the Vera Rubin platform, making this quarter the first real test of deployment against announcement.

Two subsequent developments matter more for risk assessment. Nvidia is backing **$105 billion** in financing for an OpenAI data center in Ohio, supporting an initial 4.25 GW with an option for a further 3.75 GW. And CNBC reported in late July that the two companies were in talks over a backstop arrangement of up to **$250 billion**.

The circularity here should be named: a chip vendor financing its own customer's purchases of its chips is a structure that works beautifully while end demand compounds and becomes reflexively dangerous if it stalls. With the 30-year Treasury at 5.27%, the cost of capital underpinning these commitments has risen materially since they were struck. This is not a reason to be short AI infrastructure; it is a reason to prefer exposure through assets with contracted cash flows over exposure through vendor-financed demand.

### 1. INSW — International Seaways, Inc. *(Crude & product tanker shipping)*

- **Thesis:** The Hormuz disruption has converted tanker ton-miles into the scarcest asset in energy logistics, and INSW is monetizing it immediately in cash rather than in newbuild orders — record Q2 EBITDA of $345 million, record FCF of $261 million, and the largest dividend in company history at $5.05 per share. With Gulf exports down 47% and cargoes rerouting the long way around, the rate environment is a function of geography, not of oil price direction.
- **Valuation Metrics:** P/E ~**6x** 2026 earnings (trailing `not verified` separately). P/S `not verified`. EV/EBITDA `not verified`, but net debt of $250 million against ~$4 billion of asset values and $345 million of *quarterly* EBITDA implies a low-single-digit multiple. PEG **n/m** — forward EPS is a spot-rate function, not a growth series.
- **Growth Profile:** Blended spot TCE $79,000/day versus $27,500/day a year prior (+187%). Blended VLCC earnings above $150,000/day including profit-sharing. Q2 FCF ~$100 million above the previous record.
- **Competitive Moat:** Genuinely thin in the conventional sense — shipping is a commodity business and INSW does not control freight rates. ### Portfolio Construction Notes

**Diversity mandate compliance.** Five picks across **five distinct sub-sectors**: marine energy transport (INSW), energy equipment and tubulars (TS), energy midstream (WES), naval defense (HII), agricultural inputs (NTR) — comfortably above the three-sub-sector minimum. **Zero companies above $1 trillion market cap** (against a limit of one); the largest here is NTR in the ~$30 billion range. **All five sit outside the Magnificent Seven** (against a minimum of two).

**The internal hedge is deliberate.** INSW profits from Gulf disruption; Tenaris explicitly warned it is hurt by the same disruption. These positions genuinely pull against each other, and that is the intent — the pick list is not a single leveraged bet on the conflict continuing. A de-escalation that collapses tanker rates simultaneously removes Tenaris's disclosed headwind and relieves NTR's sulfur and gas cost pressure. WES (fee-based contracts) and HII (sovereign-funded backlog) are largely indifferent to which way the conflict resolves. Anyone who wants a pure long-conflict expression should buy INSW alone and skip the rest — but that is a trade, not a portfolio.

## Risk Factors

**1. A September Fed hike into an already-tightening bond market (highest severity).** Sixty percent implied probability, a 9–3 vote with hawkish dissents, and explicit statement language declining to look through energy-driven inflation. The equity market is priced for a benign path that the Fed is signalling it may not deliver. If the 30-year moves through 5.50%, every long-duration multiple in the market compresses regardless of earnings, and there is no rate-cut backstop available.

**2. Further Gulf escalation and a genuine supply break.** Hormuz transits are already down to five vessels per day and Gulf exports have halved. The gap between a 47% export decline and a *complete* closure is the difference between $95 Brent and a genuine crisis. J.P. Morgan's 0.6% annualized hit to first-half global GDP assumes elevated-but-functioning flows. Direct US–Iran exchanges resumed Sunday after a month's pause; the risk is bidirectional, and de-escalation would reverse several of this week's moves violently.

**3. Energy pass-through into core inflation.** The mechanism to watch is second-round effects — energy into food, freight, utilities and eventually wages. Core is at 2.5% and would be near-target in ordinary circumstances. If $95 Brent converts into a broadening core impulse, the Fed's September decision stops being a close call and becomes the start of a tightening sequence the equity market has not discounted at all.

**4. AI capex financing circularity.** Vendor financing at the $100–$250 billion scale — Nvidia investing in OpenAI, backstopping $105 billion for the Ohio facility, and reportedly discussing up to $250 billion — is a structure whose robustness has never been tested at this magnitude, and it was underwritten at materially lower long-term rates than today's. The first gigawatt deploys in the second half of 2026, making this quarter the initial reality check on announcement-versus-deployment.

**5. Small-cap and high-beta positioning after a 23-year-best run.** The Russell 2000 is up ~20% YTD, its strongest since 2003, and fell 1.23% Tuesday — underperforming both the S&P and the Dow. Small caps carry more floating-rate debt than large caps, making them the most rate-sensitive cohort into a possible hike. Crowded positioning after a great year is what turns an orderly repricing into a disorderly one.

## Bottom Line

The market is absorbing a genuine supply shock at a moment when the central bank cannot ease into it — an unfamiliar configuration for investors conditioned by fifteen years of reflexive monetary support, and the reason today's weakness deserves respect rather than dip-buying reflex. The disciplined response is not to reduce equity exposure but to relocate it: away from long-duration multiples that need the 30-year to fall, and toward physical assets, contracted cash flows and sovereign-funded backlogs that get paid regardless of where the discount rate settles. Today's picks — a tanker operator at 6x earnings returning cash rather than ordering ships, a tubular manufacturer with $3.6 billion of net cash, an 8% fee-based midstream yield, the only builder of American nuclear carriers at 15.6x, and a potash franchise stabilizing on raised volume guidance — are all expressions of that single idea, and we have deliberately declined the higher-profile upgrade of the day (MLM at 29x forward) because a valuation mandate that bends for a good story is not a valuation mandate.
