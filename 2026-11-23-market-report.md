## Macro & Monetary Environment

**The Fed is hiking, and that is the single most underappreciated fact in this tape.** The federal funds rate currently sits at 3.75%–4.00% following a September increase — the first in three years — and an October hold. CME FedWatch now puts the probability of a quarter-point hike at the December 8–9 meeting at approximately **97%**, up sharply from 76.3% a month earlier. A majority of economists in a Reuters poll expect a move to 4.00%–4.25%, which would mark the second and final hike of this cycle.

**Inflation is the reason.** September CPI rose to **3.7% year over year**, up from 3.4% in August. Governor Waller has noted core inflation running at roughly a **3.5% annualized rate over the trailing three months**. The October meeting minutes revealed that a "vast majority" of the 19 policymakers were worried inflation could persist *even as energy prices fall* — language that signals concern about breadth, not just level.

**The half-point tail risk is live but contested.** Waller has explicitly opened the door to a 50bp increase depending on incoming data; New York Fed President Williams has pushed back on that possibility. This split matters enormously for duration-sensitive assets, and it is the reason Wednesday's data release carries outsized weight.

**2027 paths diverge sharply.** JPMorgan, Bank of America and Deutsche Bank model increases at three meetings; Goldman Sachs and UBS see a more limited path; ING and OCBC anticipate an extended hold after December. That dispersion among top-tier houses is itself a volatility input.

**This week's calendar — compressed and consequential:**
- **Wednesday:** PCE inflation report — the week's defining event, landing directly ahead of the December FOMC and into thin pre-holiday liquidity.
- **Tuesday:** Best Buy reports fiscal Q3 before the open, the last major U.S. retail print before Black Friday and the first under incoming CEO Jason Bonfig, who succeeded Corie Barry effective November 1.
- **Thursday:** Markets closed for Thanksgiving.
- **Friday:** Early close at 1:00 p.m. ET.

The structural hazard this week is liquidity, not just data. A hot PCE print into a half-staffed holiday order book is a recipe for gap moves that exceed what the information content alone would justify.

**Retail backdrop:** The National Retail Federation forecasts November–December retail sales growth of 4.2% over 2025, to $1.013 trillion. Early reporting from the November 17–19 retail cohort pointed to a clear value-over-premium bifurcation — Walmart, TJX and Ross strong; Target weak — consistent with a consumer that is still spending but trading down. Against 3.7% CPI, a 4.2% nominal sales forecast implies roughly flat real volumes.

## Geopolitical Factors

**Middle East risk remains the dominant commodity driver, and it cuts both ways.** Oil has been trading on diplomacy and tanker incidents rather than on physical balances:

- Early November saw crude fall approximately **6%** on renewed prospects for U.S.–Iran negotiations, before recovering part of those losses. Subsequent reporting describes those talks as **deadlocked**, leaving open the risk of military escalation and a sharp upward reversal.
- An **unprecedented level of tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz** has offset the physical impact of rising Gulf exports, leaving prices directionless at elevated levels.
- ABN AMRO estimates the current geopolitical premium at **$4–$7 per barrel**, and models that a Hormuz closure could push prices as much as **50%** higher.
- Russian crude exports hit a four-month high, adding supply-side offset.

**Price levels are genuinely unclear across sources** — ranging from Brent "stuck near $93" to "back above $96 as Iran tensions escalate," with one industry scorecard of uncertain date citing $115. Readers should treat any single quoted level skeptically. Saxo's year-to-date figures are striking: Brent up 107%, WTI up 92%, with gasoil and NY ULSD up 194% and 165% respectively — a reminder that the energy complex has been the year's defining inflation impulse and is a direct input into the Fed's reluctance to ease.

**The counter-argument deserves weight.** AEGIS argues physical balances remain loose, global supply continues to exceed demand, and long-dated prices show limited response to persistent geopolitical noise. That is the "no war, no peace" equilibrium: elevated spot volatility sitting atop a fundamentally heavy market. Rystad flags Middle East escalation and **OPEC+ alliance breakdown** among its top flashpoints for the next six to twelve months.

Notably, **oil was sliding on Monday** even as equity futures surged — a divergence worth watching, since it relieves one inflation input precisely when the Fed needs relief.

**Domestic political risk has become a market factor.** Reporting ties part of the November selloff to investor fears that an incoming Democratic Congress, and individual states, could move to constrain the AI industry and data-center buildout. Separately, Treasury Secretary Bessent's comments on an intensifying probe into Nvidia's China dealings have injected policy risk directly into the single largest index constituent.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Fintech, Payments & Financial Data

*(Rotating spotlight — day-of-year 327, 327 mod 6 = 3)*

Fintech enters this week as one of the more analytically interesting corners of the market, because the sector splits cleanly into three groups whose fortunes are diverging rather than moving together. Understanding that split is the whole game right now.

**Group one: volatility and rate beneficiaries.** Market-structure businesses are having an extraordinary year, and the mechanism is simple — their revenue is a toll on uncertainty. CME Group posted Q3 2026 adjusted EPS of $3.02, up 12.7% year over year, on third-quarter record average daily volume of 29.4 million contracts (+16%), with **double-digit volume growth across all six asset classes**. October was stronger still: a record 30 million contracts per day, up 15%, with **Treasury futures ADV up 48%**. That Treasury figure is the tell. A Fed that is hiking into reaccelerating inflation, with a live 50bp tail and three-way disagreement among major banks about 2027, generates exactly the rate-hedging demand that drives CME's highest-margin complex. Cboe was scheduled to report October 30 with consensus calling for $3.40, up 27.3% year over year, having beaten in each of the prior four quarters. With the VIX at 36 and the 10-year oscillating around 5%, these franchises are structurally long the current regime.

**Group two: the de-rated incumbents.** The legacy processing complex has been brutalized, and in at least one case the destruction looks disproportionate to the deterioration. Fiserv has fallen roughly **59% over the past year** and lost about three-quarters of its market value since early 2025. The company cut 2026 guidance in August — organic revenue growth to down-1%-to-flat from 1%–3%, adjusted EPS to $7.20–$7.40 from $8.00–$8.30 (a 14%–17% decline), and adjusted operating margin to 31%–31.5% from roughly 34% — and reaffirmed that lowered outlook at Q3 on November 3, alongside a **$6.45 billion non-cash goodwill impairment**. Freedom Broker correctly notes this is the second reduction in ten months, pushing turnaround proof points into 2027. Management has cut roughly 3,000 employees and contractors since June and raised the Project Elevate cost-savings target to $1 billion under pressure from Jana Partners; the company has also cycled through three CEOs since May 2025. Michael Burry has disclosed a position. At roughly 10x trailing earnings this is a real option on self-help — but three CEOs in eighteen months and a multi-billion impairment are not noise, and the honest read is that the turnaround has not yet earned the benefit of the doubt.

**Group three: the growth cohort, now priced like software rather than fintech.** The February "SaaSpocalypse" compressed average forward software multiples from roughly 39x to about 21x, and digital-banking and payments software was caught in the downdraft regardless of individual execution. Q2 Holdings is the cleanest example of fundamentals improving while the multiple contracts: gross margin expanded from 53.6% to 59.2% year over year on a completed cloud migration, subscription revenue grew 17%, and management raised full-year 2026 guidance — yet JPMorgan cut its target to $60 from $80 while *maintaining* Overweight, an explicitly valuation-driven mark-down. Alkami ended its strategic review without a sale, sending shares nearly 20% lower on September 23, and now carries 54 banks under contract with 42 live; Craig-Hallum downgraded to Hold on "lack of conviction." nCino sits at the uncomfortable intersection of a 73.5x P/E against roughly 8% expected sales growth and 18.24% short interest — the one name in the group where the de-rating looks incomplete.

**Cross-border is the sector's genuine problem child, and the cause is policy, not execution.** Euronet's Q3 revealed the mechanism plainly: money transfer revenue fell 4% while segment operating income fell **35%** and adjusted EBITDA fell 32%, with management attributing the pressure directly to **U.S. immigration policy changes** alongside macro uncertainty. This is a volume-mix problem in remittance corridors that no amount of operating discipline fixes quickly. Euronet cut its 2026 outlook to roughly $4.375 billion revenue and ~$725 million adjusted EBITDA, with adjusted EPS growth of about 10% — the low end of the prior 10%–15% range. Yet the same quarter showed ePay revenue and operating income each up 5% and **digital accelerator revenue up 30%** (33% year to date), and the company has repurchased $200 million of stock across the first three quarters under a $425 million authorization. dLocal faces an analogous but distinct issue: management flagged previously unanticipated FX headwinds in Brazil, Mexico and Argentina worth roughly **six percentage points** of negative impact to Q4 year-over-year gross profit growth, plus softer take rates in smaller African and Asian markets — while simultaneously *raising* total payment volume growth guidance. Volume is compounding; the dollar is taxing the translation.

**The consumer-fintech read-through is more encouraging than the share prices suggest.** SoFi's October 27 Q3 report delivered a record 17.1 million members, **44% revenue growth**, raised 2026 guidance to roughly $5.04 billion, and introduced a 2027 outlook of $6.4 billion revenue and $0.90 EPS. Bears focus on credit normalization, a declining capital ratio and insider tax-related selling — legitimate concerns in a tape where the Fed is still tightening into a 3.7% CPI. But a forward multiple in the mid-to-high teens against that growth trajectory is not a hype valuation.

**The synthesis:** fintech's bifurcation maps almost perfectly onto the macro. Businesses that monetize volatility and rate uncertainty are compounding; businesses that monetize cross-border migration flows are being policy-taxed; and businesses that monetize bank IT budgets are fundamentally fine but have been re-rated alongside software. That dispersion is where the opportunity sits.

### Artificial Intelligence

**Anthropic's IPO is now the sector's reference price, and it is behaving badly.** The company priced at **$83 per share** — above its $70–$78 range — selling 956 million shares for proceeds variously reported at $81 billion (AP) to more than $100 billion (NPR), valuing it at roughly **$1.5 trillion**. Shares opened at $111.50, a 34% premium, closed the debut session at $97.17, then **fell roughly 10% on Friday** as investors booked profits. Reported market capitalization at the open spanned $1.28 trillion to roughly $2 trillion depending on source and share-count treatment. The listing was explicitly framed as a bellwether for the AI bubble debate, and the verdict so far is unflattering: the largest IPO in history could not arrest a broader slide, and the aftermarket is already negative.

**Nvidia is simultaneously the index's largest problem and its largest potential source of relief.** The sequence matters. On November 12 the company announced Huang was stepping down as CEO and from the board; former CFO Colette Kress was appointed interim CEO, Heidrick & Struggles retained for a permanent search, and the board launched an investigation into the circumstances of the departure — with board chair Harvey Jones stating on the earnings call that the board does not intend to reinstate him. Sources conflict on the stated cause, variously citing reported extramarital relationships and a client-related scandal with alleged national-security dimensions. The Wall Street Journal reported an SEC investigation mid-week; Reuters then reported, citing two people briefed on the matter, that enforcement staff are examining the company's **accounting**, while explicitly noting it could not determine when the probe opened or its precise focus. The SEC declined to comment. Additional federal inquiries reportedly touch possible chip export-rule violations, and shareholders have filed suit alleging the company and Huang misled investors about national-security concerns.

The market impact: shares fell about 14% Friday to a 52-week low, roughly 23% on the week — **its worst week in 18 years** — taking year-to-date losses to about 33% and erasing on the order of $1 trillion in market capitalization. On Monday the stock was rebounding, with Bessent's commentary on the China probe cutting against the bounce.

**The investment question is whether this is idiosyncratic or systemic.** The bull case for separation is straightforward: an accounting inquiry resting on anonymous sourcing, with no confirmed scope, and a CEO transition — however disorderly — do not alter the demand for accelerated compute. The bear case is that Nvidia's multiple was carrying the entire complex's cost of capital, and governance plus regulatory overhang raises that cost for everyone financing AI buildouts. Friday's 10.7% decline in the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index argues the market is currently pricing the second interpretation.

**Capex commitments continue regardless.** Anthropic has announced a $50 billion AI infrastructure buildout with data centers in Texas and New York, with IPO proceeds earmarked for large-scale model development and compute. The tension the market is now pricing: Anthropic, OpenAI and SpaceX's AI division have collectively lost more money than they have made, and the political environment for data centers has turned hostile.

### Technology (Broad)

The November drawdown has been notably indiscriminate, which is itself diagnostic. Tech stocks suffered what Fortune characterized as their biggest selloff since Covid, driven by investors pricing a "premature end to the AI datacenter boom" — with the post-midterm political environment cited as a catalyst for regulatory constraints on AI and data-center development.

The year's pattern has been a steady repricing of *revenue durability*, not just multiple compression. The February leg down was driven by the recognition that AI has the capacity to compress revenue across software categories rather than simply expand TAM. The October leg was driven by AI **financing** concerns — how the infrastructure gets funded — with the iShares Expanded Tech-Software ETF (IGV) falling 4% against QQQ's 1% decline on October 16, a clean illustration of software absorbing the sharpest pain. The November leg has added governance and regulatory risk at the single largest constituent.

Monday's bounce was led by exactly what fell hardest: chip and AI names, with Nebius and AMD among the stocks singled out as rallying. Asian authorities moved to support markets, and South Korea's finance minister reiterated plans to stabilize if necessary after the Kospi fell 3.3%. One analyst's framing deserves repeating: *"As long as volatility remains elevated, even a sharp bounce is hard to read as the start of a sustainable recovery."*

### Hardware & Semiconductors

Semis bore the brunt of Friday's selling and led Monday's recovery, and the memory complex is where the genuine fundamental debate lives.

**Memory is now a two-sided argument.** Micron fell roughly 12% Friday and SanDisk about 15% after TrendForce projected **flash memory prices falling in 2027**. That forecast collides directly with Micron management's position that memory supply remains tight through 2028. The company has backed its own view with capital: an October expansion of its buyback authorization to **$35.2 billion**, with repurchases eligible to begin December 9, and 26 long-term customer agreements covering more than 35% of projected revenue through 2030. Those contracts are the most concrete evidence available that the cycle has structurally changed — but NAND oversupply risk in 2027 is a legitimate concern, and the market is currently siding with TrendForce.

**Foundry fundamentals remain exceptional.** TSMC's October revenue hit a monthly record, up roughly **60% year over year**. That figure is difficult to reconcile with a thesis that AI infrastructure demand is collapsing. On relative valuation, TSMC trades near 18x expected 2027 earnings against roughly 30x for AMD — a spread that looks increasingly hard to justify given TSMC's structural position and lower customer-concentration risk.

**AMD's data center segment grew 97% year over year in Q3**, with a Strong Buy consensus (28 Buys, 3 Holds) and an average target of about $652 implying roughly 54% upside from Friday's close. Broadcom was cited near $332 against a median target of $525. These targets reflect pre-drawdown sentiment and should be read as such — analyst targets lag violent repricings.

Price data across semis is currently unreliable, with Micron quotes ranging from roughly $594 to $725 depending on source and timestamp. Verify before acting.

### Software & SaaS

The software de-rating has been the year's most severe and most fundamental. Forward earnings multiples compressed from roughly 39x to about 21x within months of February's initial shock. The driver was never merely rate sensitivity — it was the recognition that AI agents can substitute for seat-based software revenue, a genuine threat to the unit of value rather than to the discount rate applied to it.

Recent datapoints sharpen the picture. Wells Fargo moved **Snowflake** to Equal Weight from Overweight with a target cut to $130 from $200 — a reduction of that magnitude signals a thesis change, not a tweak. Barclays separately cut Snowflake citing competition from Amazon and Oracle's bundled AI offerings, going to the core question of whether data-platform economics survive hyperscaler bundling. Snowflake fell 6.4% and Salesforce 3.6% during the early-November rout, with Palantir and ServiceNow each dropping 5% and Salesforce 4% in the mid-October financing-driven session. Morgan Stanley has reportedly flagged a deteriorating debt outlook for the software industry — a claim sourced to a low-reliability outlet and worth independent verification, but directionally consistent with the October financing narrative.

The constructive observation is that indiscriminate de-rating creates dispersion opportunity. Vertical software serving regulated, non-discretionary budgets — bank core systems, compliance, investment accounting — faces materially lower AI-substitution risk than horizontal seat-based SaaS, yet has been marked down on the same multiple compression. That is the inefficiency today's spotlight picks are built to exploit.

A lender growing 40%+ into a Fed hiking cycle with 3.7% CPI and a consumer visibly trading down faces real normalization risk, and bears correctly flag a declining capital ratio. Insider tax-related selling has added pressure. If unemployment turns, the growth multiple and the credit book deteriorate simultaneously — this pick carries the highest beta to a genuine recession of the six.

**Rating: Buy**

### Portfolio Construction Notes

The six names are deliberately arranged along a volatility axis rather than a single factor. CME and MKTX are **long uncertainty** — their revenue rises with the exact conditions that are damaging the rest of the market, making them the slate's internal hedge. QTWO is the quality-growth sleeve with the lowest AI-substitution risk, funded by non-discretionary regulated IT budgets. EEFT and DLO are the value sleeve, both cheap for identifiable and *specific* exogenous reasons — U.S. immigration policy and Latin American FX respectively — rather than for secular decline, which is the distinction that separates value from trap. SOFI carries the slate's growth beta and its credit risk.

What the slate deliberately avoids: semiconductor and AI-infrastructure exposure, where the governance and regulatory overhang at the largest constituent makes the cost of capital unknowable this week; unprofitable fintech; and anything requiring the December FOMC to deliver a dovish surprise. A position sized into a VIX-36 tape ahead of a Wednesday PCE print into holiday liquidity should be scaled in, not initiated at full weight.

## Risk Factors

**1. A hawkish PCE surprise into holiday liquidity — severity: high, probability: moderate.** Wednesday's PCE report lands two weeks before an FOMC meeting carrying 97% odds of a hike, with Waller having openly floated 50bp. Core inflation running at a 3.5% three-month annualized rate leaves little room for an upside print. The compounding factor is timing: a hot number into a pre-Thanksgiving order book with half the desks away produces gap moves disproportionate to the information. This is the week's dominant risk because it is both dated and unhedgeable through the holiday.

**2. The AI de-rating resuming after a positioning bounce — severity: high, probability: moderate-to-high.** Monday's 2.8%–3.5% futures rally came after a week in which AI-linked shares suffered their worst performance since March 2020. Sharp rallies within downtrends are characteristic of forced-covering, not repair. The analyst framing is correct: with volatility elevated, a bounce cannot be distinguished from a bear-market rally in real time. The S&P 500 and Dow remain more than 10% below records and the Nasdaq is negative year to date — the technical damage is not undone by one session.

**3. Nvidia's regulatory and governance overhang broadening — severity: high, probability: low-to-moderate.** An SEC accounting inquiry at the index's largest constituent, resting on anonymous sourcing with undetermined scope, combined with an interim CEO, a board investigation into its own former chief executive's departure, separate federal inquiries into possible export-rule violations, and shareholder litigation, constitutes an unusually broad risk surface. Bessent's commentary on the China probe suggests escalation rather than resolution. Any confirmation of accounting issues — as distinct from the current report of an examination — would reprice the entire AI complex's cost of capital. The probability is genuinely low; the severity, were it to crystallize, is extreme.

**4. Middle East escalation reversing the oil relief — severity: moderate-to-high, probability: moderate.** With U.S.–Iran talks deadlocked, an unprecedented rate of Hormuz tanker attacks, and a $4–$7 geopolitical premium already embedded, the risk is asymmetric to the upside in crude. ABN AMRO models that a Hormuz closure could lift prices as much as 50%. Oil fell on Monday, relieving one inflation input precisely when the Fed needs it — a reversal would simultaneously worsen the inflation path and the growth path, the one combination that leaves the Fed without a good option. OPEC+ cohesion is a secondary flashpoint.

**5. Political and regulatory constraints on AI infrastructure — severity: moderate, probability: moderate.** Investor fears of an incoming Democratic Congress and state-level crackdowns on AI and data-center development were cited as a direct contributor to the November selloff. This risk is slow-moving and difficult to price, but it bears on the terminal value of every capex commitment currently underwriting semiconductor and power-infrastructure earnings — including Anthropic's announced $50 billion buildout. Unlike the other risks listed, it has no catalyst date and therefore tends to be underweighted.

## Bottom Line

Today's rebound is a function of positioning, not of resolution: nothing about the Fed's December trajectory, Nvidia's regulatory surface, or the AI capex financing question improved over the weekend, and a VIX near 36 argues the market has not yet decided what these assets are worth. The more durable observation is that indiscriminate selling has created genuine dispersion — businesses that monetize volatility and non-discretionary regulated spending have been marked down alongside the speculative complex despite improving fundamentals, which is where today's spotlight slate is positioned. Investors should resist the urge to call a bottom ahead of Wednesday's PCE print into thin holiday liquidity; scale into quality at a discount rather than chasing the bounce.
