## Macro & Monetary Environment

**The Fed is arguing with itself, and the bond market is siding with the hawks.** At the July 29 meeting the FOMC held the federal funds target at **3.50%–3.75%**, where it has stood since December. What made the meeting remarkable was the dissent: **three** regional presidents — Beth Hammack (Cleveland), Neel Kashkari (Minneapolis) and Lorie Logan (Dallas) — voted for a quarter-point **increase**. Nine, including Chair Warsh, voted to hold. Dissents in favor of tightening, in this number, are a genuine rarity.

Warsh's own rhetoric has been notably harder than the vote he cast. Participants "generally noted that inflation had increased further and remained well above the Committee's 2 percent longer-run objective," and Warsh has publicly foreclosed any flexibility on the target: *"There is no soft implicit target, not on this committee's watch."* Markets initially read his first press conferences as dovish; the more careful read of his actual words points toward a hike. The bond market has made its choice:

- **30-year Treasury yield: 5.21%** — the highest since 2007.
- **10-year Treasury: ~4.63%** (as of August 4).
- **September FOMC hike probability: ~80%** per futures.

The next FOMC decision lands **September 16**, and it carries a Summary of Economic Projections — the first full dot plot of the Warsh era. That is the event risk that matters for the rest of the quarter.

**Today's data cut the other way.** The July ADP National Employment Report, released at 8:15 a.m. ET, showed private payrolls up just **44,000** — the weakest since the start of the year — following a revised +95,000 in June. Wage data was more stubborn: annual pay growth for job-stayers held at **4.4%**, while job-changers posted **7.0%** median pay growth, the fastest since August 2025.

That combination is the crux of the current setup. Hiring momentum is visibly cooling into the summer, but the pay data still runs well above anything consistent with 2% inflation, and headline inflation has been *rising*. Warsh is, in effect, betting that inflation cools before his own committee fractures. If the next CPI print runs hot, he has already built the rhetorical scaffolding for a September hike — and equities at record highs are not priced for one.

**What to watch this week:** the remaining July labor data for confirmation or contradiction of the ADP slowdown; any Warsh or dissenter commentary that firms up September; the long end of the curve, where 5.21% on the 30-year is the real constraint on equity multiples; and the tail of a very heavy earnings week (488 companies reported today alone).

## Geopolitical Factors

**The Strait of Hormuz is the whole story, and it is inflecting.** The conflict that began when the United States and Israel started striking Iran on **February 28, 2026** led Iran to retaliate against shipping with drones, ballistic missiles and small attack boats. The strait has been effectively closed since, with major carriers including Maersk formally suspending transit. Roughly 20–30% of global seaborne crude and a large share of LNG normally moves through that chokepoint, and its closure drove crude above $80 in one of the sharpest rallies in years.

Today the market is pricing the *reopening*:

- **Brent: below $79/bbl. WTI: toward $75/bbl** — after losing more than 10% across the prior two sessions.
- Qatar confirmed Tuesday that an **interim proposal has been drafted**, with both Washington and Tehran signaling progress.
- Trump called off a planned strike, stating that the US had "just been asked by Iran, and other Middle Eastern Countries, to hold off any attack in that the perimeters of a deal has been agreed to," and has extended his deadline for a deal.

Falling crude is the proximate cause of the five-day equity rally: it relieves the inflation problem that is currently splitting the FOMC, and it disproportionately helps the domestically-oriented small and mid caps that have been leading. **But the asymmetry here is severe and under-appreciated.** Equities have now banked the reopening as a near-certainty. The deal is interim, undrafted in final form, and rests on the good faith of parties that were exchanging missile fire five months ago. A collapse in talks would re-close the strait, send crude back through $80, and hand the September hawks their case on a platter — hitting stocks through both the earnings and the discount-rate channel simultaneously. The crisis has, as one analysis put it, confirmed "the return of energy security to the centre of geopolitics and statecraft."

Secondary geopolitical channels to monitor: tanker rates remain elevated with alternative routing still in place; refiners have enjoyed improved near-term margins that would compress on normalization; and prolonged disruption still feeds gasoline, diesel and petrochemical prices with a lag, meaning some inflation pass-through is already in the pipeline regardless of what happens next week.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Enterprise Software & SaaS

This is the most interesting sector in the market right now, because it is the only major group where a genuine narrative dislocation has produced genuinely cheap assets with genuinely intact cash flows.

**What happened.** Public B2B software equities suffered a **~25% valuation compression** in the first quarter of 2026 — the sharpest correction for the industry since the 2022 rate-hike cycle. The iShares Expanded Tech-Software ETF (IGV) fell more than 21% from January 1, and enterprise software EV/Sales multiples **cratered from a 5.6x average at the end of 2025 to just 4.2x by mid-March**. The move was christened the "SaaSpocalypse."

**Why.** This was not a demand event. It was a crisis of confidence in the *business model*. The proliferation of autonomous, agentic AI raised the question of whether per-seat licensing — the bedrock of enterprise software economics for two decades — survives when the "seat" is a piece of software rather than a human. If an AI agent does the work of five analysts, does the vendor sell five seats or one? By some accounts the repricing erased on the order of $2 trillion of nominal SaaS value. Crucially, the selloff was driven "largely by sentiment rather than fundamentals," even as leading names continued compounding profit and cash.

**The recovery, and where we are now.** The turn began **April 13, 2026**, when institutional buying lifted IGV more than 6% in 48 hours, with Oracle and ServiceNow spearheading the rebound. Options positioning in IGV has since been read as a bet that the AI fear is already fully priced. But the recovery has been highly uneven — it has rewarded the perceived AI *winners* and left an entire tier of profitable, cash-generative, unglamorous enterprise software trading at multiples that look nothing like software.

**Today's evidence that the panic overshot.** **Shopify** reported Q2 revenue of **$3.58 billion, up 34%** year over year, against a $3.45B consensus, with GMV up 32% to **$115.57 billion**. Management guided Q3 revenue growth to a low-thirties percentage rate versus consensus of 26.3%, and raised full-year revenue *and margin* guidance. The stated driver is the exact opposite of the bear thesis: advanced AI tooling **expanding margins and improving merchant retention**. Shares rose as much as 33.8% premarket and closed up roughly **19%**. When a software company demonstrates that AI is an operating-leverage story rather than a cannibalization story, the multiple responds violently — which tells you how little of that outcome is priced into the group.

**The framework that matters.** The SaaSpocalypse thesis has real teeth for one specific archetype: horizontal, seat-priced, workflow-thin software where the product is essentially a UI over a database and the human user is the unit of value. It has far weaker teeth where:

1. **The software is the regulated system of record.** Compliance obligations, audit trails and regulator sign-off make replacement a multi-year board-level project, not a procurement decision.
2. **Pricing is decoupled from headcount** — transaction, consumption, claim, or asset-based.
3. **A two-sided network exists.** An agent can replicate features; it cannot replicate the other side of a marketplace.
4. **The buyer is a bank, insurer, or government** — institutions with the longest replacement cycles and the lowest tolerance for vendor risk in the economy.

Every pick in this report was selected against those four filters. That is the analytical core of today's work: buy the enterprise software the agentic-AI narrative cannot actually eat, while it is still priced as though it can.

**Names screened and rejected on valuation.** Discipline requires naming what failed. **JFrog (FROG)** is an excellent business — Q1 revenue +26%, net retention 120%, a raised 118% NDR floor, cloud revenue accelerating to 45% growth, and TD Cowen's top small/mid-cap idea — but at **14.2x forward revenue** (versus a 4.1x peer median), 11.3x EV/FY26 revenue and 47.7x EV/FY26 FCF against 18.5% guided FY26 growth, it is priced for a great deal more than it has guided to, with heavy stock-based comp diluting cash-flow quality. **Vertex (VERX)** is optically the sector's most bombed-out name — down 63%, a 52-week range of $36.75 to $10.21, recently ~$11.04 against a $36.85 GF Value estimate — but ROE of −1.0% and a −1.3% net margin against sector averages of 22% and 18% put it squarely in value-trap territory; the screen that flags it 66% undervalued also flags it "Possible Value Trap," and we agree with the second label. **Domo (DOMO)** rallied 30%+ in a single session on Q4 FY2026 results, but the fundamental base is too thin to underwrite. **Figma (FIG)** may well grow revenue 50% over two years and still be expensive today. **Descartes Systems (DSGX)** deserves special mention as the closest miss: 12–15% revenue growth, a 46% EBITDA margin, $368.85M net cash ($4.29/share), and a logistics SaaS moat that has shown *no material impact* from AI-disruption fears — but at 38.8x trailing and 27.1x forward earnings it is quality at a full price, not value.

### Artificial Intelligence

The AI trade is now a capital-spending story with a widening gap between the spending and the revenue it is meant to produce.

The five largest US cloud and AI infrastructure providers — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle — have collectively committed to **$660–690 billion of capex in 2026**, close to double 2025 levels. **Anthropic** announced plans to invest **$50 billion** in US AI infrastructure, starting with its own data centers in Texas and New York City in partnership with Fluidstack; **AMD** agreed in late July to invest up to **$5 billion** in Anthropic as part of a computing-power arrangement. **Nvidia** and **OpenAI** are in talks over a backstop of up to **$250 billion** to fund infrastructure plans — this after Nvidia's announced intent to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI did not materialize as structured, with $30 billion ultimately contributed to the round OpenAI closed in March.

Two things deserve flagging. First, the **circularity** is now explicit: chip vendors funding model labs that buy chips, and infrastructure providers backstopping the customers who fill their data centers. Revenue recognized on one leg of these arrangements is capital supplied on the other. That is not fraud, but it does mean reported demand is partly manufactured by the vendors, and it degrades the signal quality of the demand data everyone is underwriting.

Second, **Amazon is scrapping its Nova family of models** and restarting its foundation-model effort under new leadership, leaning more heavily on its multi-billion-dollar Anthropic stake for frontier capability while it rebuilds. For a company of Amazon's resources to reset its frontier program is a real datapoint on how brutal the capability frontier has become for anyone outside the top handful of labs.

The uncomfortable arithmetic: capex is expanding roughly **46% faster than sales** across the complex — wider than the **32%** divergence observed during the 2001 telecom excess cycle. And the investment boom is itself generating a meaningful share of the profits used to justify current prices. Morgan Stanley's headline 2026 risk is precisely that the "AI capital boom fails to boost productivity."

### Technology (Broad)

Today's tape was a clean illustration of rotation *within* technology rather than out of it. The Nasdaq's +0.35% badly lagged the Dow and Russell, and the dispersion beneath the surface was enormous — **Shopify +19%** against **AMD −7%** on the same day, both after beating estimates.

Yesterday's session set the frame: **Palantir soared 29%** on blowout Q2 results, its best day in more than two years. The market is not de-rating technology as an asset class; it is imposing an extremely high bar and punishing anything that clears it by less than expected. Roughly **90% of companies reporting this season have beaten estimates**, which means beats have no scarcity value — the only thing that moves a stock is the *guide*, and specifically whether the guide accelerates.

Elsewhere in platform-land, **Cloudflare** continued pushing into agent infrastructure, announcing expansions to its Agent Cloud plus, on August 4, an Agent Development Lifecycle and Cloudflare Wallets to give AI agents native payments and verifiable identity on the web. Cloudflare counted 42% of the Fortune 500 as paying customers as of March 2026 and 4,400+ large customers in Q1. Agent-native infrastructure — identity, payments, permissioning for non-human actors — is a genuinely new software category being staked out right now.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

**Semis have decoupled from their own fundamentals, and today's AMD reaction is the cleanest evidence yet.**

AMD delivered what would, in most years, be a triumphant quarter: revenue up **50% to a record $11.54 billion** against $11.31B expected; non-GAAP EPS of **$1.66** versus $1.62 consensus; **data center revenue up 107% to $6.7 billion**; data center segment operating income of **$2.1 billion** against a $155 million *loss* a year earlier, taking the segment to 58% of total company revenue; and Q3 guidance of roughly **$13 billion**. The stock fell **7–8%**.

The explanation is entirely about the starting point. AMD entered the print up **132% year to date**, having tripled over twelve months, on a P/E near **170**. As Morningstar's Michael Field put it, "It's simply a case of market expectations being too high." Sell-side reaction has been to defend the name — analysts broadly expect AMD to outperform despite the slide.

The pattern generalizes, and the statistic is striking. Of the **45 tech hardware and semiconductor companies** that reported since July 1, **38 beat on the top line and 36 beat on the bottom line** — and the group's average one-month price performance is **−13.4%**, with 36 of 45 trading lower. An 84% top-line beat rate producing a double-digit *decline* is not a fundamentals problem. It is a positioning and expectations problem, and it is the most important tell in the market: the AI hardware trade has moved into the phase where good news is fully owned and any deceleration is repriced brutally. Related storage names moved on the same dynamic, with SanDisk (SNDK) rebounding after an AMD-driven dip, and Western Digital and SanDisk both reporting into questions on NAND pricing and enterprise SSD margins. HBM4 ramps carry real lead-customer concentration risk, and capex at $7.83B in a single quarter for some names means leverage and free-cash-flow trends will be scrutinized.

### Software & SaaS

Covered at length in today's spotlight above, so briefly on the read-across: the sector's EV/Sales reset from 5.6x to 4.2x has not fully reversed, and the recovery since April 13 has been concentrated in names the market has decided are AI winners. That leaves a large middle tier — profitable, cash-generative, embedded in regulated workflows — trading on earnings multiples that would be unremarkable for an industrial.

The contrast with semiconductors is the trade of the moment. In semis, 84% of companies beat and the group fell 13.4%. In value-tier enterprise software, expectations are so thoroughly reset that a single quarter demonstrating AI-driven margin expansion is worth 19% in a day, as Shopify just showed. One group has all the good news priced in; the other has a structural bear thesis priced in that does not apply uniformly to its members. Today's picks are positioned on the correct side of that asymmetry.

Also of note for software-adjacent names: **UBS downgraded FIS to Neutral** from Buy, cutting its target to $49 from $63 on reduced 2026 revenue and profitability guidance — a reminder that within payments and financial technology, execution is dispersing sharply, and that "cheap" requires a stabilizing or improving numerator.

## Stock Picks — Valuation-Based

Five picks, all drawn from today's enterprise software spotlight, all in the $3.5B–$6B market-capitalization band, all selected specifically for resistance to seat-based agentic-AI cannibalization. Each is a business where the software is the regulated system of record, pricing is decoupled from headcount, a network effect exists, or the customer is a bank or insurer — usually more than one of those.

### 1. NICE — NICE Ltd.
*Sub-sector: Customer-experience / contact-center AI and financial-crime compliance*

**Thesis.** NICE is the single most direct victim of the SaaSpocalypse narrative — contact-center software priced per agent seat is the textbook case for agentic-AI disruption — and it has been repriced as though the outcome is already decided, to roughly 9x forward earnings with an 8.7% free-cash-flow yield and no debt. But the company just delivered revenue **above the high end** of guidance with cloud revenue growing 12.6%, meaning the disruption is showing up in the narrative and not yet in the numbers, while management buys back stock into the fear.

**Valuation metrics.** Trailing P/E **~9.7x**. Forward P/E **~7.5x–9.2x** depending on source, with the company's own FY26 EPS guidance of $11.06–$11.26 against a ~$101.51 share price implying roughly **9.1x forward**. That forward multiple sits about **51% below the software industry median of 18.7x**. Free cash flow of **$586.1 million** TTM (operating cash flow $610.7M less $24.6M capex) for a **free-cash-flow yield of 8.72%**. One fair-value model puts intrinsic value at $268.07 against ~$101.51 — a 62% discount — which is almost certainly too aggressive, but the direction is not in dispute. EV/EBITDA is materially below the P/E-implied level given a net cash balance sheet. PEG on ~9x forward earnings and high-single-digit revenue growth with mid-teens cloud growth screens near or below 1.0.

**Growth profile.** Total revenue growth **~9%** with FY2026 guidance of **$3.17–$3.19 billion**. Cloud revenue **+12.6%** in Q2 2026 (and +14.6% in Q1), with full-year cloud guidance of **13%–15%**. International revenue **+22%** year over year — the geographic mix shift is where the growth actually is. FY26 EPS guidance of **$11.06–$11.26**. CEO Scott Russell noted delivery above the high end of the revenue range and at the high end of the non-GAAP EPS range.

**Competitive moat.** Two distinct moats. CXone is the embedded cloud platform inside very large enterprise contact centers, where migrations are multi-year, integration-heavy programs touching telephony, CRM, workforce management and recording. Second and more durable: **Actimize** financial-crime and compliance software sits inside regulated financial institutions where the software *is* the audit trail presented to regulators. That is not a product a bank swaps for an agent framework. Balance sheet is a weapon here — **no debt**, $304.1M cash and short-term investments at Q1-end, $253.3M of buybacks executed in Q1 alone and a **new $600 million repurchase authorization**.

**Key risk.** The bear case is real, not imaginary: if agentic AI genuinely collapses contact-center seat counts, NICE's per-agent pricing model faces structural revenue compression, and a 9x multiple can become a 9x multiple on a shrinking earnings base. The mitigant is that NICE sells the AI agents too, and that compliance revenue is headcount-independent — but this is the pick where the thesis and the sector's central fear collide most directly. Size accordingly.

**Rating: Strong Buy**

### 2. OTEX — Open Text Corporation
*Sub-sector: Enterprise information management, content services and information governance*

**Thesis.** OpenText trades at roughly **6.9x EV/EBITDA** — a decade low — with a free-cash-flow yield above **18%** and a 4.66% dividend, while guiding to **22%–25% free-cash-flow growth** in fiscal 2026 and raising cloud, bookings and FCF targets. This is the least exciting business in this report and the most mispriced: the market is paying a distressed multiple for a company whose cash generation is accelerating.

**Valuation metrics.** **EV/EBITDA 6.94x**. **EV/FCF 13.45x**. Free-cash-flow yield **>18%**, equivalent to more than **$4.00 per share** in free cash flow. Dividend of **$1.10** annually for a **4.66% yield** (implying a share price around $23.60). The bull case for re-rating toward a historical 10x EBITDA implies **above $55/share**, or more than 100% upside. Trailing P/E is distorted by amortization from acquisitions, which is precisely why EV/EBITDA and EV/FCF are the right lenses here — on a cash basis the company is trading at roughly five and a half years of free cash flow.

**Growth profile.** This is a cash-flow compounder, not a revenue compounder, and the thesis must be stated honestly: total revenue is roughly flat to modestly declining as management divests non-core assets, while **cloud revenue and adjusted EPS both hit records in Q3** with strong growth in content and cloud bookings. The growth line that matters is free cash flow, guided to **+22% to +25%** for FY2026. Management has raised FY26 guidance for cloud revenue, bookings *and* free cash flow, and continues disciplined capital allocation alongside portfolio reshaping.

**Competitive moat.** OpenText owns the enterprise content and records infrastructure — the systems where large organizations keep documents they are legally required to retain, produce on demand, and prove the chain of custody for. Renewal rates are extraordinarily high because the alternative is migrating decades of legally-material records, and the buyers are typically large regulated enterprises and governments. Information governance is a compliance function; AI agents create *more* unstructured content to govern, not less. The installed base is the asset, and it is close to irreplaceable in practice.

**Key risk.** Low-to-negative organic growth is the genuine issue. A roll-up funded by acquisition carries leverage, and if free cash flow disappoints, the deleveraging path and the dividend both come under pressure simultaneously. The multiple is cheap because the market does not believe the top line stabilizes — the thesis requires only that it does not deteriorate faster than cash generation improves.

**Rating: Buy** *(deep value / income)*

### 3. ACIW — ACI Worldwide, Inc.
*Sub-sector: Payments software infrastructure*

**Thesis.** ACI provides the real-time payments and billing software that banks and billers cannot switch off, growing 7–9% with a raised guide, at **14.8x forward earnings** and **14.3x EV/EBITDA** — a discount to most industry peers on both EV/EBITDA and price-to-free-cash-flow — while returning 50–60% of operating cash flow via buyback on a $4.08 billion market cap.

**Valuation metrics.** Trailing P/E **18.71x**, forward P/E **14.79x** (some sources put forward P/E at 18.58x on more conservative estimates; either way the multiple is reasonable against expected earnings growth). **EV/EBITDA 14.29x**, **EV/FCF 17.57x**. The stock trades at a discount to most peers on both EV/EBITDA and P/FCF. PEG screens as not overvalued once growth is factored in; on the ~14.8x forward multiple against high-single-digit revenue growth plus mid-teens EBITDA growth and a shrinking share count, PEG lands comfortably near 1.0–1.3. Market cap **$4.08 billion**.

**Growth profile.** FY2026 revenue guidance **raised to $1.89–$1.92 billion** from an initial $1.88–$1.91B, representing **7%–9% constant-currency growth**, with adjusted EBITDA guided to **$540–$555 million** — an EBITDA margin near 29%. Segment detail from 2025 shows **Payment Software +9%** and **Biller +13%**, so the faster-growing segment is the higher-quality recurring one. The Q1 2026 beat came with the guidance raise.

**Competitive moat.** ACI's software is, in the company's own accurate framing, mission-critical payment and billing infrastructure "deeply embedded in customers' complex and highly regulated workflows." Real-time payment rails are the definition of a system where failure is unacceptable and certification is onerous. Replacement projects at a bank take years, require regulatory comfort, and carry career risk for whoever proposes them. Critically for the AI thesis: **pricing is transaction-linked, not seat-linked.** More automation means more transactions, which means more ACI revenue. Agentic AI is a tailwind to transaction volume, not a threat to a seat count.

**Key risk.** Revenue recognition on the license side is lumpy and timing-dependent, which produces noisy quarters and occasional guide-downs unrelated to underlying demand. Bank IT budget cycles are the second constraint — a genuine credit or funding shock at financial institutions would push discretionary modernization spend to the right, and the FIS downgrade today is a reminder that the sector's execution is dispersing.

**Rating: Buy**

### 4. CCCS — CCC Intelligent Solutions Holdings Inc.
*Sub-sector: Property & casualty insurance claims network SaaS*

**Thesis.** CCC operates the two-sided network connecting auto insurers to collision repair shops, growing 10% at a **~40% adjusted EBITDA margin** with free cash flow that **tripled year over year** in the latest quarter, at roughly $7.95 per share against fair-value estimates of $10.02–$11.79. The market is applying a SaaSpocalypse discount to a business whose moat is a network, not a feature set.

**Valuation metrics.** Market capitalization **$3.55 billion** (as of July 30, 2026) at approximately **$7.95** per share. Independent fair-value estimates cluster at **$10.02** and **$11.79**, implying roughly **21%** undervaluation on the more conservative figure. Adjusted EBITDA margin of approximately **40%**. Q2 2026 free cash flow of **$82.4 million** annualizes toward a high-single-digit free-cash-flow yield on the current market cap. This is the pick where I have the least clean read on trailing and forward P/E — GAAP earnings are suppressed by amortization and stock-based compensation, so EV/EBITDA and free-cash-flow yield are the honest lenses, and both screen inexpensive for a 40%-margin network business.

**Growth profile.** Q2 2026 revenue **+10%**, with full-year guidance **raised to approximately 10%** annual growth, supported by a **multi-year enterprise agreement with a major insurer**. Revenue has compounded at roughly 10% over the last three years — consistent rather than spectacular. The standout line is cash conversion: operating cash flow of **$101.6 million** in Q2 2026 versus **$43.1 million** a year earlier, and free cash flow of **$82.4 million** versus **$27.4 million**. That is a genuine inflection, not a rounding artifact.

**Competitive moat.** This is the strongest structural moat in today's list. CCC sits between two constituencies — insurance carriers on one side, tens of thousands of collision repair facilities on the other — and each side is present because the other is. A competitor, human or agentic, can build better estimating software; it cannot conjure the counterparties. Layered on top is two decades of proprietary claims and repair-cost data, which is the training substrate for exactly the AI features customers now want. Notably, **two top-five insurers expanded adoption of CCC's AI-based claims routing** during Q2 2026 — the AI cycle is pulling revenue *toward* the network owner.

**Key risk.** **Declining net dollar retention** is the concern I would underwrite most carefully, alongside slowing organic growth and lower insurance claims volume — safer vehicles and fewer accidents are a secular headwind to a business paid per claim. If NDR keeps sliding, 10% growth becomes 6% growth and the multiple does not save you. This is the reason for a more measured rating despite the network moat.

**Rating: Accumulate**

### 5. QTWO — Q2 Holdings, Inc.
*Sub-sector: Digital banking platform SaaS*

**Thesis.** Q2 provides the digital banking platform for banks and credit unions, and just **raised 2026 guidance on AI-driven demand** — sending shares up 11.5% — with subscription ARR growing 15% and record adjusted EBITDA. When the market's central fear is that AI destroys software demand, a software company raising guidance *because of* AI demand is the most direct available refutation, and it is not priced as one at ~$60.42 against a $71.69 fair-value estimate.

**Valuation metrics.** Share price approximately **$60.42** against a **$71.69** GF Value estimate — roughly **15.7%** undervalued — with an average analyst price target of **$73.45**. FY2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance of **$244–$248 million** on revenue of $881–$886 million works to a **~28% adjusted EBITDA margin**. On EV/EBITDA the name screens in the mid-to-high teens against 11% revenue growth and 15% ARR growth — reasonable rather than cheap. I want to be straightforward: this is the **growth-at-a-fair-price** slot in today's list, not a deep-value name. It is here because the AI-demand datapoint is a direct rebuttal of the sector's bear thesis, and because switching costs in core digital banking are close to absolute.

## Risk Factors

**1. A September Fed hike that equities are not priced for. (Severity: high)**
Three FOMC members already voted to raise rates in July, futures put a September increase near **80%**, and the 30-year is at **5.21%** — the highest since 2007. Chair Warsh has explicitly disclaimed any "soft implicit target" on inflation. Meanwhile the S&P sits at a record on a five-day winning streak. A hike into record highs, with the long end already at generational yields, is the most direct threat to multiples in the market — and it arrives with a fresh dot plot on **September 16**. Note the reflexivity: the more the reopening trade suppresses oil and lifts stocks, the more room Warsh has to tighten.

**2. Hormuz talks collapse and the oil relief unwinds. (Severity: high)**
The entire five-day rally is built on an **interim, unsigned** proposal between combatants who were exchanging fire five months ago. Brent below $79 and WTI near $75 represent a full pricing of success. A breakdown re-closes a chokepoint carrying 20–30% of seaborne crude, sends oil back through $80, and simultaneously reignites the inflation problem splitting the FOMC — hitting equities through earnings and the discount rate at once. This risk is not independent of risk #1; they compound.

**3. The AI capex-to-revenue gap. (Severity: high, longer-fuse)**
Capex across the AI complex is growing roughly **46% faster than sales**, wider than the **32%** divergence of the 2001 telecom cycle, against **$660–690 billion** of committed 2026 hyperscaler spend. Tech and AI equities carry EV/EBITDA near **25x**, close to historical extremes and above telecom's valuations preceding the 2000 peak. The financing is increasingly circular — Nvidia/OpenAI backstop talks up to $250B, AMD's up-to-$5B into Anthropic — meaning vendors are partly funding their own reported demand. Morgan Stanley's flagged 2026 risk is precisely that this capital boom fails to lift productivity.

**4. Expectations risk in semiconductors and momentum names. (Severity: medium-high, and already materializing)**
This one is not hypothetical — it is happening. Of 45 hardware and semi companies reporting since July 1, **38 beat on revenue and 36 beat on earnings**, yet the group's average one-month performance is **−13.4%** with 36 of 45 lower. AMD beat on every line, grew data center revenue 107%, guided up, and fell 7%. With ~90% of all reporters beating estimates, beats convey no information and only accelerating guidance is rewarded. Portfolios crowded into 2026's momentum winners carry asymmetric downside into every remaining print.

**5. Labor market deterioration meeting a hawkish Fed. (Severity: medium, rising)**
ADP private payrolls of **+44,000** in July was the weakest since January, following a revised +95,000. Pay growth remains at **4.4%** for job-stayers and **7.0%** for job-changers. This is the genuinely dangerous configuration: an employment cycle that is cooling while wage and price data still argue for tightening. It leaves the Fed with no clean move and raises the probability of a policy error in either direction — hiking into a slowdown, or holding into an inflation re-acceleration.

## Bottom Line

The index prints look serene — a fifth straight advance, another record — but the composition is telling you something important: this rally belongs to small caps, cyclicals and the oil-relief trade, not to the mega-cap AI complex, which is up just 4% year to date against roughly 20% for the Russell 2000. The bear case for equities is not that earnings are bad; it is that a market at record highs is simultaneously long an unsigned Iran deal and short a September rate hike that futures put at 80%, with the 30-year at its highest since 2007. **Own the broadening, not the crowding.** Today's five picks target the market's most indiscriminately repriced group — profitable, cash-generative enterprise software trading at 7x to 15x forward earnings because the market has extrapolated agentic-AI disruption uniformly across a sector where it plainly does not apply uniformly. When a bank's system of record, a claims network, or a transaction-priced payment rail is priced as though an AI agent will replace it next year, that is not a business risk being compensated — it is a narrative discount, and Shopify's 19% day just showed what happens when one of these names proves AI is an operating-leverage story instead.
