## Macro & Monetary Environment

**The policy rate and the September decision.** The FOMC held at **3.50%–3.75%** in July on a **9–3 vote**, with the three dissents pushing for a hike. That vote split is the single most important number in this report. A 9–3 hold with hawkish dissents is not a stable equilibrium — it is a committee one data print away from moving. Markets are currently pricing roughly a **60% probability of a 25bp hike** at the September meeting, a probability that firmed after employers added **162,000 jobs in August**.

**The inflation picture.** The Fed's preferred gauge, core PCE, is running just over **3%**, comfortably above the 2% target. Headline CPI is **+3.4% year-over-year**, with core at **2.5%**. The gap between headline and core is the energy shock, and it is precisely the gap that a central bank is supposed to look through — except that a Hormuz closure entering month seven stops being a transitory supply shock and starts being a persistent input cost that feeds into goods and services with a lag.

**Today's data (8:30 ET).** **August PPI final demand rose 0.4% month-over-month.** For a Fed worried that energy is bleeding into the pipeline, a 0.4% producer-price print is not the reassurance the doves needed. Also released this morning: **initial jobless claims**, with **existing home sales** and **EIA crude inventories** later in the session.

**Tomorrow's data (8:30 ET, Friday September 11).** **August CPI** — consensus **+0.4% m/m and +3.4% y/y headline**, **+0.4% m/m and +2.4% y/y core**. This is the last inflation reading before the FOMC meets. A core print above 0.4% m/m would, in our view, move September from a coin-flip-plus to a near-certain hike and take the 10-year through 4.90%. A 0.3% core would relieve the long end and produce a sharp relief rally in exactly the long-duration software names in today's spotlight.

**The political overlay.** The administration is publicly pressuring the Fed to cut ahead of the September decision. We would treat this as a *risk-increasing* factor rather than a dovish one: perceived political interference raises the term premium the market demands on long-dated Treasuries, which is a headwind for equity multiples regardless of where the funds rate settles. The 10-year at 4.85% while the funds rate sits at 3.50–3.75% is a steep curve that says the bond market is pricing inflation persistence, not policy accommodation.

**What to watch this week and next:** August CPI (Friday), then the FOMC decision, statement, and dot plot next week. Also watch the EIA inventory data for evidence that the Hormuz disruption is finally drawing down US commercial stocks — that would be the signal that $100 Brent has further to run.

## Geopolitical Factors

**The Strait of Hormuz (severity: highest).** Shipping through Hormuz has been largely blocked by Iran since **February 28, 2026**, when the US and Israel initiated an air campaign against Iran. As of **September 9, 2026**, the strait is effectively **closed to commercial shipping**, with tanker traffic down roughly **90%** as insurers withdrew coverage and Iran issued direct threats against commercial vessels. Both sides have attacked vessels in transit. This is now **day ~192** of the closure.

The market implication is not the headline oil price — it is the *duration*. A two-week disruption is a spike; a seven-month closure is a structural repricing of the global energy cost curve. Roughly a fifth of seaborne crude normally moves through that chokepoint. Forecasts that assumed normalization have been repeatedly wrong: the EIA's end-2026 projection of **$79/bbl** assumes "relatively stable geopolitical conditions," a condition that has not held for six months. Scenario work now puts WTI in a **$69.92–$102.18** range for September. Prediction markets are actively handicapping whether traffic normalizes by September 30.

**US–Canada trade war (severity: high, and escalating on a known schedule).** This is the underappreciated risk in today's tape. The US imposed a **50% tariff on $27.6bn of Canadian goods** effective August 22. Canada matched dollar-for-dollar: effective **September 8, 2026** — two days ago — Canada imposed **15%, 25%, and 50% tariffs** on US products mirroring the Section 338 and Section 232 rates, hitting **steel, household appliances, agricultural equipment, and dairy**. The US has responded with import bans on Canadian **dairy, motorcycles, and alcoholic beverages** beginning **September 29**. Prime Minister Carney has described the relationship in the language of economic warfare.

Two reasons this matters more than the market is pricing: first, it is an *inflationary* shock arriving simultaneously with the energy shock, at exactly the moment the Fed is deciding whether to hike. Second, it has a **scheduled escalation date (September 29)**, which means the risk is not diffuse — it is a calendar event that can be positioned around. The Dow's YTD underperformance is the first-order expression of this.

**Iran–US diplomatic stalemate.** The absence of a negotiating track is what converts the Hormuz situation from a risk into a baseline. Any credible de-escalation headline would be worth 5–8% on energy-sensitive equities in a session; we would not underwrite it.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Enterprise Software & SaaS

**The setup: the most violent de-rating in the sector's history is (probably) over.**

To understand why we are allocating today's picks here, you need the magnitude of what happened. Software's forward P/E multiple fell from **84.1x at the 2020–2022 peak to 22.7x by March 2026**. That took the sector *below the S&P 500's overall multiple* — for the first time in recorded history. Not in 2022 when rates spiked. Not in 2008. Not even in the dot-com unwind, which was a speculative bubble deflating rather than a challenge to the business model itself. Approximately **$2 trillion** of software market capitalization was destroyed. The IGV software ETF fell roughly **30% from its September 2025 peak** and was down over 21% at its year-to-date worst.

**The bear thesis, stated fairly.** The argument that gutted the sector was **seat compression**: if an AI agent does the work of five people, an enterprise buys 100 licenses instead of 500, and seat-based recurring revenue — the entire economic foundation of SaaS — deflates. This was never a fantasy. The evidence is real and it is disclosed:

- **Salesforce cut approximately 4,000 customer support roles** after deploying AI systems that handle a growing share of service requests. A vendor demonstrating on its own P&L that its product replaces headcount is, definitionally, demonstrating seat compression.
- **Workday disclosed headcount reduction as a direct revenue risk in its 10-K.** When the CFO's lawyers put it in the risk factors, the market is entitled to price it.
- **ServiceNow disclosed that 50% of its net new business no longer comes from seat-based pricing.** Management frames this as successful adaptation; a bear frames it as the seat model already being half-dead.

**What actually happened to the fundamentals.** Here is the disconnect that creates the opportunity. While multiples were collapsing, the operating results *improved*:

| Metric | 2025 | 2026 (est.) |
|---|---|---|
| Median revenue growth | 12.2% (Q1 est.) | **13.2%** (Q2 est.) |
| Median EBITDA margin | 20.0% | **23.3%** |
| Median gross margin | — | **77.1%** |

Growth **accelerated** and margins expanded by 330bp. DevOps, ITOps, and developer/automation platforms led at **21.9%** median growth. This is not a sector whose business model is collapsing; it is a sector whose business model is being *repriced on a narrative*.

**The dispersion is the whole game.** The market is not paying for growth in the aggregate — it is paying for growth that survives the agentic thesis:

- Companies at or above the **Rule of 40** trade at a median **6.6x trailing revenue**. Companies below it trade at **2.3x**. That is a ~3x valuation spread on a single operating threshold.
- **CRM, sales/marketing/CX, and collaboration/productivity have fallen into single-digit growth.** These are the categories where an agent most plausibly substitutes for a seat.
- Sub-sectors where agents replace the workflow itself — support ticketing, basic CRM, generic analytics — receive **lower multiples than their Rule of 40 score alone would predict**. The market is explicitly discounting for disruption exposure, not just for growth.
- Companies pairing **net revenue retention above 120%** with disciplined customer acquisition post **nearly double** the Rule of 40 scores of weaker-retention peers.

**Where we are in the cycle.** By late spring, evidence accumulated that leading software companies were *monetizing* AI rather than being disrupted by it. **Deutsche Bank has called the SaaSpocalypse over**, citing the discount valuation. August was a strong month for the group — software led tech higher after a strong week of earnings, easing months of panic. IGV traded at **$106.81 on September 3** against a 52-week range of **$73.93–$117.99**, with the trailing quarter **+8.96%** but year-to-date NAV total return only **+1.23%**. Read those two numbers together: the sector has recovered violently off the lows and is still roughly flat on the year, ~9.5% below its 52-week high. The recovery is real but far from complete, and — critically — **the gains have not been remotely even**.

**Our framework for today's picks.** Uneven recovery in a de-rated sector is the classic setup for security selection over sector beta. We want four characteristics:

1. **Agent-resistant revenue.** Either the product is priced on something other than human seats (consumption, data volume, assets, devices, transactions), or the buyer is a machine-scale problem that grows as AI proliferates.
2. **Demonstrated margin discipline** — real GAAP or near-GAAP profit and free cash flow, not adjusted-EBITDA storytelling.
3. **A multiple that already prices the bear case.** We are not paying 15x revenue to find out whether agents eat the seat.
4. **An AI story that is additive rather than defensive** — the company sells more because AI exists, not less.

There is a second-order point worth stating: the AI capex boom itself creates software demand. The five largest cloud and AI infrastructure providers — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle — have committed to **$660–690bn of capital expenditure in 2026**, nearly double 2025. Every one of those data centers has to be observed, secured, backed up, and orchestrated. That is a tailwind for observability, cyber resilience, and infrastructure software that has nothing to do with seat counts — and it is the single most under-priced linkage in software today.

### Artificial Intelligence

**"Model fatigue" has arrived.** The past week saw a remarkable release cluster: **Anthropic** shipped **Claude Fable 5.1** and **Claude Mythos 5.1** on Tuesday, describing them as the world's most advanced models for coding and knowledge work (Fable 5.1 reached general availability September 1 at the same $10/$50 per-million pricing as Fable 5, with cache reads dropping to $0.25). **OpenAI** followed with **GPT-6 Astra** ($10 input / $1 cached / $12.50 cache write / $50 output per 1M tokens, ~1.05M context, 128K output). **Meta** announced **Muse Spark 1.3** and **Google** unveiled **Gemini 3.8 Flash**, both emphasizing coding and agentic tasks.

The investable observation is not any individual model — it is the **compression of the release cycle and the stability of pricing**. Four frontier labs shipping within days, at flat or falling prices, with cache reads dropping to a quarter of a dollar per million tokens, is the signature of a commoditizing input. That is bad for anyone whose moat is model access and good for anyone who sells the layer above (workflow, data, governance) or the layer below (compute, memory, power). It also directly undercuts the crudest version of the SaaS-disruption thesis: if intelligence is a cheap, undifferentiated input, the durable value accrues to proprietary data and embedded workflow — which is what incumbent enterprise software actually owns.

**The capex-to-revenue gap remains the central AI risk.** Pure-play AI vendors led by OpenAI and Anthropic are posting rapid revenue growth, but **their combined revenues remain a fraction of the infrastructure investment being deployed on their behalf**. Against $660–690bn of 2026 hyperscaler capex — Amazon ~$200bn, Alphabet $175–185bn, Meta $115–135bn, Microsoft $120bn+, Oracle ~$50bn — that gap is the defining financial question of this cycle. It does not have to close this year. It does have to close.

**Tonight's read-through: Oracle.** Oracle reports fiscal Q1 2027 after the close, and it is the cleanest available test of whether AI infrastructure demand is still compounding. Consensus is **$19.13bn revenue and $1.74 adjusted EPS**, with OCI growth guided to **58–64% year-over-year**. Remaining performance obligations stood at **$638bn, up 363%**, of which **12% is expected to convert within 12 months and 34% between 13 and 36 months**. Approximately **$75bn** of prepaid and customer-supplied hardware is associated with large AI contracts.

Two cautions on that RPO figure. First, an RPO number that large relative to current revenue is a *concentration* statistic as much as a demand statistic — it reflects a small number of very large AI contracts whose counterparty credit quality is now a genuine analytical question. Second, the market has already shown it will punish this stock on tone rather than results: Oracle fell roughly **7% after-hours on its June fiscal Q4** despite beating on both revenue and EPS, purely because an unchanged full-year outlook read as a demand plateau. **Scotiabank's Patrick Colville today maintained Sector Outperform but cut his target from $241 to $215** ahead of the print. We are not picking Oracle — it is coverage — but every AI-infrastructure and data-center name trades on tonight's OCI number.

### 1. DOX — Amdocs Limited *(Communications & media software, BSS/OSS, managed services)*

**Thesis.** Amdocs sells the billing, charging, and operational support systems that carriers cannot run without, on multi-year contracts with switching costs measured in years and hundreds of millions of dollars — and it trades at a **single-digit forward P/E with a ~4% dividend yield**. This is the most agent-proof revenue stream in software: no one replaces a tier-one carrier's billing stack with an AI agent, and the revenue is priced on subscribers and systems, not human seats.

**Valuation Metrics.** Forward P/E **8.46** (as of May 25, 2026) — **54.5% below the software industry median of 18.59**. Price **$62.67** against a GuruFocus GF Value of **$86.71**, i.e. **27.7% below** estimated fair value. Market cap **~$6.45bn**. Dividend yield **~4%**. Trailing P/E in the low double digits. (P/S ~1.4x and EV/EBITDA in the high single digits on a ~$4.69bn revenue base; PEG is not meaningful at low-single-digit growth — this is a value-and-yield holding, not a growth compounder.)

**Growth Profile.** FY26 revenue growth guided **+2.6% to +4.6%** (narrowed from 1.5%–5.5% on May 13, 2026), consensus revenue ~**$4.69bn**. Free cash flow reaffirmed at **$710–730mn**. On a ~$6.45bn market cap, that is a **~11% free cash flow yield**. EPS growth runs meaningfully ahead of revenue growth via buybacks and margin discipline.

**Competitive Moat.** Mission-critical systems of record inside telecom operators, with regulatory and billing-accuracy requirements that make replacement genuinely dangerous for the customer. Multi-year contracted backlog. A managed-services book that embeds Amdocs staff inside customer operations. Displacement cycles in this category run a decade.

**Key Risk.** Growth is genuinely low — 2.6–4.6% is barely above inflation, and a value trap is a real possibility if telco capex compresses further. Customer concentration among a small number of tier-one carriers means a single contract loss is material. The stock has already de-rated substantially; the question is whether 8.5x forward is the floor or the new normal.

**Rating: Buy.** An ~11% FCF yield with a 4% dividend and contracted revenue is compensation enough for low growth. Position as a defensive anchor, not a compounder.

### 2. CVLT — Commvault Systems, Inc. *(Data protection, cyber resilience & recovery)*

**Thesis.** Commvault has completed the hardest transition in infrastructure software — perpetual license to SaaS — and is now compounding SaaS ARR at 38–42% while posting a decade-high EBIT margin. Its revenue scales with **data volume and ransomware risk**, both of which grow monotonically with AI adoption; it is structurally immune to seat compression.

**Valuation Metrics.** Trading at a discount to high-growth security peers on an EV/EBITDA basis. Consensus analyst price target **$160.90** across 18 analysts, implying **~26% upside** from the recent price (~$128). Rated "Buy" on consensus. Note that the earnings line — **$70.66mn in FY26, down 7.16%** — makes trailing GAAP P/E optically expensive at roughly 25–30x; the correct lens here is ARR, EBIT margin, and cash generation during a subscription transition, where GAAP earnings are structurally depressed by deferred revenue accounting.

**Growth Profile.** FY26 revenue **$1.18bn, +18.89%** year-over-year (from $995.62mn). **Subscription ARR +22%**; **SaaS ARR +38%**, reaching **$400mn, up 42%**. Q4 FY26 SaaS revenue **+43% to $93mn**; full-year SaaS revenue **+52% to $333mn**. Added **500 new subscription customers**. **Net dollar retention 114%**. **EBIT margin 23% in the quarter — a 10-year high.** Revenue growth of ~19% plus a 23% margin puts Commvault comfortably above the Rule of 40, which the data says is worth roughly 3x on the multiple.

**Competitive Moat.** Backup and recovery is the definition of sticky: the software holds the customer's recovery point objectives, its compliance evidence, and its last line of defense against ransomware. Migration risk is existential for the buyer, so incumbency is durable. Commvault's unification of data security, identity resilience, and cyber recovery on one cloud-native platform is a genuine consolidation play against point solutions — and the $660–690bn hyperscaler capex build creates data that must be protected regardless of how many humans remain employed.

**Key Risk.** Competition from Rubrik, Veeam, and Cohesity is intense and well-funded, and all are pursuing the same cyber-resilience positioning. Management's **softer fiscal 2027 guidance** is the live concern — the setup is balanced precisely because FY27 growth and margin expansion may both moderate. If SaaS ARR growth decelerates below 30%, the Rule of 40 premium compresses quickly.

**Rating: Buy.**

Qualys is priced on **assets scanned**, not seats, which makes its revenue base mechanically expand with the AI infrastructure build rather than contract with headcount.

The August rally (+15.8% on the Q2 print) has already captured some of the re-rating.

### 4. PEGA — Pegasystems Inc. *(Workflow automation & agentic process orchestration)*

**Thesis.** Pegasystems is the rare application-software company where the agentic AI transition is an accelerant rather than a threat — and management has made the sharpest competitive pricing decision in enterprise software by offering **AI agents with no per-token runtime costs**. Free cash flow has gone from **$22mn to $491mn** and the company is targeting **$700mn+ by 2028**, while the stock fell 40% year-to-date.

**Valuation Metrics.** The stock **fell 40% YTD**, with analysts seeing **~31% upside to a $43 consensus target**; TIKR's mid-case model sets **$64.93**, implying ~48.5% total return over 4.8 years (~8.5% IRR). A DCF puts shares roughly in line with fundamentals while **market-based multiples still screen as undervalued** — the classic signature of a name where cash generation has re-rated but the multiple has not. On **$288.3mn of first-half free cash flow**, the annualized FCF yield is compelling against the current market capitalization.

**Growth Profile.** **Record first-half 2026 free cash flow of $288.3mn**; operating cash flow **$298.2mn**. FY2025 free cash flow **$491mn**, against **$22mn** a few years prior — a genuine structural inflection, not a one-off. **Pega Cloud ACV grew 33%** and is approaching the **$1bn** mark. **Blueprint AI is now the primary go-to-market engine**, with almost all new pipeline growth connected to Blueprint usage and an "unusually high level of new logo pipeline growth."

**Competitive Moat.** Pega owns the decisioning and case-management layer inside large banks, insurers, and government agencies — deployments that take years to implement and are wired into regulated processes. The **no-per-token-cost pricing for AI agents** is a real strategic weapon: as enterprises discover that consumption-priced AI produces unpredictable utility-style invoices (the central complaint about the agentic pricing shift), a vendor offering deterministic cost becomes structurally attractive to CFOs. Blueprint AI also lowers the implementation barrier that historically limited Pega's sales velocity.

**Key Risk.** **ACV growth has slowed on client delays amid AI market changes, with management guiding to continued impact for the rest of the year.** That is the bear case in the company's own words: enterprises are pausing large workflow commitments while they figure out their agent strategy. If that pause extends into 2027, the $700mn FCF target slips and the multiple stays compressed. Pega also competes directly with ServiceNow, which has vastly greater distribution.

**Rating: Buy.** The FCF trajectory is the signal; the ACV pause is the noise creating the entry point.

### 5. DT — Dynatrace, Inc. *(Observability & application performance monitoring)*

**Thesis.** Observability is the purest software expression of the AI infrastructure build: **$660–690bn of 2026 hyperscaler capex** creates an enormous volume of complex, distributed systems that must be monitored, and Dynatrace is priced on **data ingested and hosts monitored**, not seats. It just crossed **$2bn ARR** with growth *accelerating* to 17%.

**Valuation Metrics.** InvestingPro's fair-value work flags DT as **undervalued**, with analyst targets ranging **$55 to $70**. On guided FY27 revenue of **$1.97–1.98bn**, the shares trade at a mid-single-digit price-to-sales multiple — a sharp discount to where observability assets traded in 2024 — while the company is GAAP-profitable, which most of its high-growth peer set is not. Growth of 14–15% with the company's margin structure places it above the Rule of 40, in the 6.6x-revenue-multiple cohort rather than the 2.3x cohort.

**Growth Profile.** **Surpassed $2bn in ARR** with **four consecutive quarters of 16% ARR growth** in fiscal 2026, then **accelerated to 17% ARR growth in fiscal Q1 2027**. FY27 guidance: **ARR growth 13–14% in constant currency**, total revenue **$1.97–1.98bn (+14–15%)**. Note that the reported acceleration to 17% is running ahead of the 13–14% guide — the setup of a management team guiding conservatively into strengthening demand.

**Competitive Moat.** Dynatrace's Davis AI causal-inference engine and OneAgent automated deployment are genuine technical differentiators against Datadog and open-source alternatives — the platform automatically maps dependencies rather than requiring manual instrumentation, which matters enormously at AI-scale complexity. Hyperscaler co-sell partnerships provide distribution. Switching costs are high because observability data and alerting logic accumulate over years. The addressable market is **$65bn** ($51bn observability, $14bn security).

**Key Risk.** Datadog is the stronger competitor on developer mindshare and land-and-expand motion, and Dynatrace's enterprise-first heritage makes it slower into cloud-native greenfield accounts. Consumption pricing cuts both ways: if enterprises optimize their observability spend during a capex digestion phase — a real possibility if the AI-capex-to-revenue gap forces discipline — ingestion volumes and therefore revenue fall directly. The gap between 17% actual and 13–14% guided ARR growth also implies management expects deceleration.

**Rating: Buy.**

### 6. ADBE — Adobe Inc. *(Creative, document & marketing software)*

**Thesis.** Adobe is the single largest valuation dislocation created by the SaaSpocalypse — a franchise with 85%+ gross margins and enormous free cash flow trading at a **forward P/E in the low teens or below**, roughly half the software industry median, because the market has priced generative AI as an extinction event for creative software rather than as a feature Adobe is already monetizing.

**Valuation Metrics.** Forward P/E of **13.31 as of September 8, 2026**, with other data providers showing **11.24** and **9.37** depending on estimate vintage and share-count treatment — the dispersion itself tells you how contested the earnings base is, but every source puts it well below the **software industry median of ~17.1**, a **45.1% discount**. GuruFocus flags the stock as **significantly undervalued at 56.9% below an estimated fair value of $595.91**. The stock is **−18.82% over the trailing 52 weeks**. Consensus for tonight's fiscal Q3: **$6.07 EPS on ~$6.68bn revenue** — annualizing to roughly $24 of EPS against a share price in the low-to-mid $200s.

**Growth Profile.** Mid-to-high single-digit revenue growth with continued buyback-driven EPS accretion. The key growth datapoint is that **AI-first ARR has tripled past $500mn** — small against a ~$23bn revenue base, but it is the proof point that directly contradicts the disruption thesis: customers are paying Adobe *more* because of generative AI, not less.

**Competitive Moat.** The Creative Cloud file formats (PSD, AI, PDF) are industry standards embedded in the training and workflow of essentially every professional creative and every enterprise document process. Document Cloud is a quasi-utility. Adobe's Firefly models are commercially indemnified and trained on licensed content — an increasingly valuable differentiator as enterprises grow cautious about the copyright provenance of generative output. The Experience Cloud enterprise relationships are separately sticky.

**Key Risk.** **Adobe reports fiscal Q3 after the close tonight — this is a binary event, and we are recommending into it.** That is the honest framing. Beyond tonight: the structural bear case is real. Generative image and video models genuinely lower the barrier to competent creative output, and the mid-market and prosumer tiers of Creative Cloud are the most exposed cohort in the company. The cheap multiple is cheap because the market assigns meaningful probability to terminal-value impairment, not merely to a growth slowdown. This is a value opportunity only if you believe professional creative workflow — versioning, collaboration, brand governance, rights management — is the real product rather than pixel generation.

**Rating: Accumulate.** The valuation is the most compelling in large-cap software, but with earnings tonight we would stage the position: partial entry now, the balance after the print and the guide. Do not size this as a full position ahead of a binary event.

## Risk Factors

**1. Tomorrow's CPI print and next week's FOMC (severity: highest, imminent).** A 9–3 hold with hawkish dissents, ~60% odds of a hike already priced, core PCE above 3%, and a 10-year at cycle highs. Consensus for tomorrow is +0.4% m/m headline and +0.4% m/m core. A core print at 0.5% would, in our judgment, take the September hike to near-certainty, push the 10-year through 4.90%, and hit every long-duration name in today's pick list simultaneously. This is the dominant near-term risk and it resolves in under 24 hours.

**2. Hormuz closure duration and the $100 oil floor (severity: high, persistent).** Six months of closure with 90% traffic reduction and no diplomatic track. Every additional month converts a supply shock into embedded cost inflation, which directly constrains the Fed's ability to ease at any point in 2026. The asymmetry is unpleasant: further escalation is worth perhaps $15–20/bbl of upside, while the de-escalation scenario is not currently forecastable.

**3. US–Canada tariff escalation on a September 29 trigger (severity: high, scheduled).** Canada's retaliatory tariffs took effect two days ago; the US import bans on Canadian dairy, motorcycles, and alcohol begin September 29. This is a second inflationary impulse hitting steel, appliances, and agricultural equipment — sectors that feed directly into CPI goods — arriving while the Fed is already leaning hawkish. Unlike the Hormuz situation, this one has a date on it.

**4. The AI capex-to-revenue gap (severity: high, medium-term).** $660–690bn of 2026 hyperscaler capital expenditure against AI-native revenues that remain a fraction of that figure. Oracle's $638bn RPO is concentrated in a small number of large AI contracts whose counterparty credit is now an analytical question, not an accounting one. Tonight's Oracle print is the nearest checkpoint. A visible deceleration would hit not only semis and data-center infrastructure but the observability and cyber-resilience names that are levered to the build — including two of today's picks.

**5. Complacent volatility (severity: moderate, but it amplifies everything above).** A VIX of 16.44 into a CPI print, an FOMC meeting, a $100 oil market, and two mega-cap software earnings reports is not pricing much. Cheap volatility ahead of a dense event calendar means any negative surprise gets amplified by the unwind of short-volatility and systematic positioning. The Russell 2000's −1.30% against the S&P's −0.48% suggests the deterioration has already begun beneath the surface.

## Bottom Line

The macro tape is genuinely hostile — $100 oil, a 4.85% 10-year, two active trade and geopolitical escalations, and a Federal Reserve that may hike next week — and we would not add broad equity beta into tomorrow's CPI print. But hostile macro is precisely what creates the security-selection opportunity in enterprise software, where a $2tn de-rating on a seat-compression narrative has left the sector trading below the S&P 500 on forward earnings while its median growth rate *accelerated* to 13.2% and its median EBITDA margin expanded 330bp. The market is applying a uniform disruption discount to a sector where the disruption exposure is anything but uniform.

Our six picks are built on that dispersion: four of them — Amdocs, Commvault, Qualys, and Dynatrace — do not price their software on human seats at all, which makes them structurally immune to the thesis that de-rated them, while Pegasystems and Adobe are contrarian bets that agentic AI is a feature to be monetized rather than an extinction event. Respect the calendar: CPI tomorrow, the FOMC next week, and Adobe's own print tonight all argue for staged entries rather than full positions today.
