## Executive Summary

The market has spent three sessions celebrating a Fed that did nothing, and this morning it is being handed the bill. The FOMC held at 3.75%–4.00% on Wednesday in a 10–2 split where **both dissents were hawkish** — Cook and Hammack wanted a hike — and futures now put roughly 85% odds on a 25bp increase in December. This morning's data did nothing to argue with them: Q3 GDP printed **3.4% annualized** against a 3.2% consensus, headline PCE ran **3.6% year over year**, and core PCE came in at **3.1% y/y / 0.3% m/m**. That is a hot economy with sticky, energy-amplified inflation and a 10-year note sitting at **5.00%** — a combination that justifies tight policy and compresses the multiple on everything long-duration.

Into that macro, the AI complex delivered its most important earnings night of the quarter and the tape did not like the answer. Alphabet fell about 10% pre-market on a rare revenue miss and, more damagingly, **negative free cash flow of $9.07 billion** against a $2.70 billion consensus — the first time the market has seen a hyperscaler's AI spending visibly overrun its cash generation. Microsoft beat and rose 3–4%, but its capex *missed* at $45.2 billion versus $50.7 billion expected, and it trimmed FY27 capex guidance from $175 billion to $170 billion. CFO Amy Hood attributed the shortfall to third-party GPU supply delays pushing usable capacity into the first half of 2027. The second-order casualties were immediate: CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN and Applied Digital all fell 4–7%, and ServiceNow cratered 15–19% on soft Q4 guidance while Caterpillar dropped 11% despite a record $78.1 billion backlog.

The distinction that matters today — and the organizing idea of this report — is that **the AI capex is not shrinking; its delivery schedule is slipping.** Alphabet held 2026 capex at $195–205 billion and guided to a "significant increase" in 2027. Microsoft still expects to spend more than $50 billion next quarter. What changed is that the physical world cannot absorb the money as fast as the spreadsheets assumed. That is bearish for anyone whose equity is a levered claim on *contract timing* and constructive for whoever owns the **physical constraint** — transformers, switchgear, skilled electrical labor, optical interconnect, powered shells and on-site generation. Today's spotlight and today's picks are built entirely on that distinction.

## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The Fed held, and the dissents were the story

The October 28 FOMC left the target range at **3.75%–4.00%**, where it has sat since the committee *raised* by 25bp on September 16. The vote was **10–2, with Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack dissenting in favor of an immediate 25bp hike.** The statement retained the line that "inflation remains elevated" and said the committee is waiting for an "accumulation of more information" before acting further.

Read the asymmetry carefully. A year ago the dissent risk on this committee was doves wanting cuts. Today there are zero dovish dissents and two hawkish ones. Chair **Kevin Warsh**, asked whether the proximity of the November 3 midterms influenced the decision, said politics "played no role." The committee also restructured its 2027 calendar, dedicating one meeting exclusively to a broad monetary policy strategy review — a housekeeping item that nonetheless removes a rate decision point from next year's schedule.

Post-meeting, **CME FedWatch has a December hike at roughly 80–87.5%**, with most feeds clustering near 85%. The September dot plot put year-end rates at 4.1%–4.4%, up from a prior 3.6%–4.1%. Some strategists now model hikes continuing into 2027.

## Geopolitical Factors

Energy is not a sector story this quarter; it is a macro variable, and it is the reason core and headline PCE are diverging.

**Prices.** Brent is trading around **$114–115** with WTI near **$101–103**. Brent settled at $115.32 on October 28 (+1.3%) before easing roughly 0.7–1.1% on Thursday. UBS expects Brent near **$110 at year-end**, citing tight inventories and record freight costs. Goldman Sachs flags a "stickier" geopolitical premium with further shipping attacks and infrastructure damage as upside risks.

**The conflict.** The Iran war remains the dominant input. Houthi militants said Tuesday they struck Saudi Arabia's **Ras Tanura refinery** with a ballistic missile — an attack on the single most important crude export facility on the planet. Transit through the **Strait of Hormuz is running at roughly 60% of normal capacity**, and October has been the worst month for commercial vessel incidents in and around the Strait since tracking began in 2017.

**The calendar risk.** The Wall Street Journal reported that Washington has warned Gulf Arab states to prepare for a possible renewed US military campaign against Iran **after the November 3 midterms**. Separately, prices eased modestly on reports of a diplomatic overture, including a letter from JD Vance to an Iranian security official. The result is a genuinely two-sided, binary-feeling setup over the next week: meaningful diplomatic progress could strip $10–15 off Brent quickly, while a resumption of strikes on Iranian infrastructure — or another successful attack on Saudi export capacity — could put $130+ in play.

**Why an equity investor should care even if they own no energy.** A $115 Brent that becomes $140 Brent does three things at once: it adds to headline inflation and hardens the Fed's hawkish bias; it compresses industrial and transport margins (see Caterpillar); and it raises the discount rate applied to long-duration growth equities via the long end. There is no clean hedge for that in a tech-heavy portfolio, which argues for owning businesses whose revenue is contractually fixed and near-term rather than those whose value sits in 2030 terminal cash flows.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: AI Infrastructure & Data Centers — Power, Cooling, Networking, Interconnect, Memory

*(Rotating spotlight bucket for day-of-year 302: 302 mod 6 = 2.)*

This is the most consequential 24 hours the AI infrastructure trade has had this year, and the market is drawing the wrong lesson from it. Let me set out what actually happened, then what it means.

#### What the hyperscalers actually said

**Alphabet.** Q3 capex was **$51.36 billion against a $55.09 billion consensus**. Full-year 2026 capex guidance was *held* at **$195–205 billion**. CFO Anat Ashkenazi said capex will "increase significantly" in 2027, with detail to come on the Q4 call. Revenue of roughly $127.6 billion slightly missed the ~$128.2 billion consensus, and Google Cloud revenue *more than doubled* to $30.71 billion. The stock is down 10% pre-market — on track for its worst-ever post-earnings reaction — and the reason is not the revenue line. It is **free cash flow of negative $9.07 billion** against a consensus of negative $2.70 billion. To fund the quarter Alphabet raised $28.7 billion in bonds and $12.4 billion in new equity, pushing long-term debt to $125.1 billion.

**Microsoft.** Beat on both lines. Fiscal Q1 2027 capex including finance leases was **$45.2 billion versus $50.7 billion expected**, up about 30% y/y. Full-year FY27 capex guidance was *cut* from $175 billion to **$170 billion**. Amy Hood's explanation is the key sentence of the entire earnings cycle: **delays among outside GPU suppliers will defer some usable capacity into the first half of 2027.** Microsoft still expects to spend more than $50 billion next quarter and emphasized it will remain free-cash-flow positive — FCF was $18.63 billion, down 27% but comfortably positive. The stock is up 3–4%.

#### The correct interpretation

Put those two reports side by side and the signal is unambiguous. **Neither company reduced its intention to spend. Both companies spent less than expected because the supply chain could not deliver.** Microsoft said so explicitly. Alphabet's capex undershoot sits alongside guidance for a significant 2027 increase. Aggregate hyperscaler capex estimates for 2026 still cluster around **$725–800 billion**, with Meta having *raised* its range to $135–145 billion on October 21 and Goldman's baseline taking AI-related capex from roughly $765 billion toward $1.64 trillion by 2031.

So the constraint has moved. For three years the question was "will they fund it?" The answer is now definitively yes — to the point where Alphabet is funding it with negative free cash flow, bonds and equity issuance. The question for the next three years is **"can anyone build it?"** Wood Mackenzie has estimated a 15% shortfall in transformer supply this year. NEXTDC cut FY27 capex guidance on power and permitting delays and paused projects not backed by customer contracts. Eaton's US data center backlog is reported at **307 gigawatts — roughly 15 years of backlog at 2025 build rates**, of which only about 20% is near-term convertible.

#### The three-tier framework

This is how I would organize the entire AI infrastructure universe right now, and it explains every move on the tape today.

**Tier 1 — Constraint owners.** Businesses that own a physically scarce input and are paid on contracted delivery: high-voltage electrical equipment, skilled electrical labor, optical interconnect, on-site generation, powered colocation shells. Their revenue recognition tracks *physical installation*, which is precisely the thing that is now the bottleneck. A capex delay does not destroy their revenue; it pushes it right while the backlog grows. **This tier is where today's picks live.**

**Tier 2 — The hyperscalers themselves.** Spending enormously, generating real revenue from it (Google Cloud doubling is not a bubble artifact), but with free cash flow now visibly compressing. Alphabet is the cautionary case: the market will tolerate capex intensity right up until it eats FCF, and then it will re-rate the stock 10% in a morning.

**Tier 3 — Leveraged capacity resellers.** The neoclouds. CoreWeave fell 5.8%, Nebius 6.3%, IREN 5.3% and Applied Digital 4.3% on Microsoft's capex miss, with CoreWeave and Nebius down a further ~7% and IREN ~5% pre-market Thursday. Over the past month CoreWeave and Nebius are off more than 30% and IREN roughly 40%. The mechanism is brutally simple: these are debt-funded businesses whose equity value is a levered call option on contracted capacity coming online *on schedule*. Microsoft — a major customer of both CoreWeave and Nebius, with a nearly $20 billion Nebius compute agreement — just said the schedule is slipping. Elon Musk's observation on October 26 that more than 200 neoclouds face "guppy-level death" is less a prediction than a description of what leverage does to a business with no cost advantage when its customer's timeline moves six months right.

**The trade this implies:** own Tier 1, be selective and FCF-disciplined in Tier 2, and avoid Tier 3 until the capacity-delivery curve inflects. Today's sell-off is indiscriminately marking down Tiers 1 and 3 together. That is the opportunity.

#### Sub-sector detail

**Power generation and electrical equipment.** The binding constraint. GE Vernova's Q3 call on October 28 reported total backlog of **$197 billion** with Electrification data center orders exceeding **$6 billion year-to-date**. Generac disclosed a **$4.5 billion data center backlog** anchored by a new long-term supply agreement with Amazon, of which $2.4 billion delivers across 2027–2028 and more than half of the total in 2027 alone. Eaton raised full-year adjusted EPS guidance to $13.40–$13.60 with Electrical segment backlog up 43% y/y in Q2 (one source puts Electrical Global backlog growth at 103%).

**Electrical construction and skilled labor.** Arguably the scarcest input of all, because you cannot import a licensed high-voltage electrician. MYR Group posted record Q3 revenue of $1.26 billion with backlog up 38.2% to **$3.68 billion** and authorized a $100 million buyback. Sterling Infrastructure's combined backlog hit **$6.14 billion, up 78%**, with more than 92% of E-Infrastructure signed backlog tied to mission-critical work. IES Holdings grew fiscal Q3 revenue 40% to $1.24 billion with a $4.53 billion backlog, no debt, and announced a two-for-one split.

**Cooling and thermal.** Johnson Controls' most recent quarter showed Applied HVAC growing at high-teens rates driven primarily by data center cooling, orders up 27%, and a record **$21 billion backlog (+32% y/y)**. Management expects data centers to move from a high-teens percentage of revenue to roughly one third within three to five years, and has a $1 billion coolant distribution unit pipeline. The absorption-chiller reference design it introduced claims a ~44% reduction in cooling electrical demand — in a world where power is the constraint, efficiency *is* capacity.

**Optical interconnect and networking.** The scale-out layer. Coherent grew fiscal 2026 revenue 22.5% to $7.12 billion and reports fiscal Q1 on **November 4** — the next real datapoint on 800G/1.6T transceiver demand. Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue was up 221% y/y in its most recent quarter. Arista remains the reference architecture for connecting GPU clusters.

**Memory — the one sub-sector that is unambiguously tightening.** SK Hynix reported record Q3 revenue and operating profit late on October 27, with **DRAM average selling prices up a high-20s percentage sequentially**, early HBM4 shipments, and a statement that demand will outpace supply **through 2030**. Micron rose 5.9% to $983.23 on Wednesday; Sandisk gained 4.9%. KB Securities estimates Samsung has already locked in roughly 80% of its 2027 output through long-term supply agreements. Meta explicitly cited higher component pricing when it raised capex guidance — memory inflation is now showing up in hyperscaler budgets.

The bear case deserves a hearing: CNBC's mid-October reporting flagged China's CXMT making progress in high-density DRAM, and models projecting DRAM prices falling ~23% in 2028 versus 2027 (though still well above 2025 levels). Morningstar warns of a 2029–2030 downcycle. I am not picking a memory name today — Micron at $983 after a run from sub-$850, with sources unable to agree whether its P/E is 12x or 51x, is a momentum vehicle rather than a valuation case, and it would consume my single above-$1T allocation for the privilege. But the sub-sector's fundamentals are the strongest in the spotlight.

#### What would falsify this thesis

Intellectual honesty requires naming the disconfirming evidence. If Alphabet's Q4 call in January guides 2027 capex *down* rather than "significantly" up; if Microsoft's FY27 number drops below $150 billion; or if a major hyperscaler cancels rather than defers contracted capacity — then this is a demand problem, not a delivery problem, and the Tier 1 backlogs are overstated. Watch the order books, not the stock prices: an order cancellation in a quarterly backlog disclosure would be the real signal, and none of the Tier 1 names disclosed one this quarter.

### 4. ETN — Eaton Corporation plc *(Electrical power distribution equipment — transformers, switchgear)*

**Thesis.** Eaton owns the deepest and most quantified bottleneck in the entire AI buildout: a **307 gigawatt US data center backlog, equivalent to roughly 15 years of construction at 2025 build rates**, in a market where Wood Mackenzie estimates a 15% transformer supply shortfall this year. ## Risk Factors

**1. A December rate hike into a 5% ten-year — SEVERE.** The combination of 3.4% GDP, core PCE re-accelerating to 3.1%, two hawkish FOMC dissents and 85% market-implied odds of a December hike is the dominant risk to every multiple in the market. At a 5.00% risk-free rate the equity risk premium on the S&P is close to zero. Tomorrow's Employment Cost Index is the near-term trigger: a hot wage print effectively locks December and puts 5.25% on the ten-year in play. This risk is not sector-specific and cannot be diversified away within equities.

**2. Iran escalation after the November 3 midterms — SEVERE, and date-specific.** Washington has reportedly warned Gulf states to prepare for renewed military action against Iran after the midterms. Hormuz is already at ~60% of normal transit capacity, October has been the worst month for vessel incidents since 2017, and the Houthis have struck Ras Tanura. Brent at $114–115 already contains a premium; a successful strike on Iranian or Saudi export infrastructure puts $130–140 in play, which would feed directly into an already-hot headline PCE and harden the Fed's bias. **This is the single most asymmetric event on the calendar and the VIX at 16.91 is not pricing it.**

**3. AI capex demand disappointment (as distinct from delay) — HIGH.** This report's central thesis is that the capex is intact and the delivery is slipping. If that is wrong — if Alphabet guides 2027 capex down in January, or a hyperscaler *cancels* rather than defers contracted capacity — then Tier 1 backlogs are overstated and today's picks fall together. Alphabet's negative $9.07 billion free cash flow is the mechanism by which this could become real: there is a level of FCF destruction at which boards stop approving the spend, and we are closer to finding it than we were a week ago.

**4. Neocloud credit contagion — HIGH and under-appreciated.** CoreWeave and Nebius are down more than 30% in a month, IREN roughly 40%, and these are debt-funded businesses whose customers just told them the schedule is slipping. If one of them cannot refinance, the damage will not stay contained: these entities are counterparties to equipment orders, power purchase agreements and leases across the Tier 1 complex. A neocloud credit event would be read by the market as an AI demand signal even if it is purely a leverage event.

**5. Enterprise software multiple compression — MODERATE but broadening.** ServiceNow's 15–19% decline came on a revenue *beat* with soft guidance. Fortinet was downgraded the same morning. At a 5% discount rate, high-multiple software has no cushion, and this quarter's pattern — in-line results, guide-down, violent re-rating — has not finished working through the sector.

## Bottom Line

The most important thing that happened in the last 24 hours is that Microsoft told the market its AI capacity is arriving late because its suppliers cannot deliver, and the market sold the suppliers. That is backwards. **The AI capital expenditure cycle is not decelerating — Alphabet held 2026 capex at $195–205 billion and guided 2027 significantly higher while burning $9 billion of free cash flow to do it — but the physical delivery of transformers, switchgear, electricians, optics and powered shells has become the binding constraint, and constraint owners get paid on a backlog that lengthens rather than evaporates when schedules slip.**

What the slip does destroy is leverage. The neoclouds, whose equity is a levered call on capacity arriving on time, are down 30–40% in a month and deserve to be. Owning the constraint instead of the leverage is the whole trade, and today's indiscriminate sell-off is pricing both the same way.

Hold the macro firmly in view while doing it: a 5% ten-year, a Fed with two hawkish dissents and 85% odds of a December hike, $115 Brent, and a November 3 election that Washington has tied to renewed military action against Iran. That argues for contracted near-term cash flows over terminal-value stories, for position sizes smaller than conviction would suggest, and against treating a 16.91 VIX as permission to be fully invested into next week.
