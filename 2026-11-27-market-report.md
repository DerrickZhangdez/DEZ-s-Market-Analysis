### Why this matters more for software than for anything else

This is the connective tissue between the macro section and today's spotlight, so it is worth being explicit. Enterprise software is the **longest-duration cash-flow asset in the equity market**. A SaaS business is a bond-like stream of renewals discounted over a decade-plus. When the terminal discount rate rises, the present value of year-eight revenue falls far more than the present value of year-one revenue.

The 2026 software de-rating is therefore **doubly driven** — AI-disruption fear compressing the growth assumption *and* a rising discount rate compressing the multiple applied to it. Both blades of the scissors have been cutting simultaneously. That is precisely why the sector now trades where it does, and why a valuation-based screen finds so much to work with there today.

## Geopolitical Factors

Geopolitics is currently a **secondary** driver versus rates and the AI trade, but it is maintaining a persistent bid under energy.

**Russia–Ukraine — the live variable.** Continued attacks on energy infrastructure are adding upward pressure on crude. Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries and export terminals had knocked out an estimated **13% to 20% of Russia's refining capacity by the end of October**. Offsetting this, investors are awaiting Washington's announcement of a date for a new round of peace talks, with some hope of an energy truce. This is genuinely two-sided: a credible deal brings Russian supply back to market and is **bearish** crude, while continued strikes are bullish. Positioning into a binary headline event in thin holiday liquidity is poor risk management.

**Middle East — the structural premium.** The World Bank's 2026 Commodity Markets Outlook characterised the year's disruption as the largest oil supply shock on record, with the premium tied principally to Middle East conflict and Strait of Hormuz disruption rather than to Venezuela or Ukraine. The Bank's own elasticity estimate — that a 1% geopolitically driven output drop lifts prices ~11.5% on average — explains why relatively modest physical disruptions have produced outsized price moves.

**Venezuela — overrated as a driver.** Following the US strike in early January 2026, analysts characterised the immediate oil-market effect as minimal, amounting to little more than another uptick in an already-existing risk premium. It remains a headline generator more than a supply driver.

**Trade and tariffs — the new entrant.** New tariffs were cited as a contributing factor in the November 25 memory-stock selloff alongside softer DRAM pricing forecasts. Tariff policy is quietly becoming a semiconductor-specific risk rather than a generalised macro one, and it compounds the memory-cost squeeze described below.

## Sector Deep Dives

### Today's Spotlight: Enterprise Software & SaaS

**This is the most dislocated major sector in the US equity market right now, and it is dislocated for a reason that is half-true.**

#### The "SaaSpocalypse": what actually happened

2026 has been, by several measures, the worst year for enterprise software in over three decades. JP Morgan characterised it as the industry's **largest non-recessionary drawdown in more than 30 years**. The damage, by the available markers:

| Metric | 2026 reading | Prior/context |
|---|---|---|
| IGV (software ETF) drawdown | −21% YTD (late March); ~−30% by April | — |
| Broader SaaS index | down ~40% YTD at the trough | — |
| Bessemer Cloud Index median revenue multiple | ~6x by early 2026 | down from ~9x |
| Software sector P/E | fell to **at or below the S&P 500 multiple** | ~22.7x early 2026 |

That last line is the remarkable one. For the first time on record, **enterprise software trades at a discount to the broad market**. A sector with 75–85% gross margins, 90%+ recurring revenue and negative working-capital dynamics is being priced below the multiple of an index containing utilities, miners and discretionary retail.

#### The bear thesis, stated fairly

The core argument is **seat compression**, and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed:

1. **The pricing model is the vulnerability.** Most enterprise SaaS is priced per-seat. If an AI agent performs the work of three analysts, the customer needs fewer seats. Revenue falls even if the product is working perfectly and the customer is delighted.
2. **AI-native competitors attack the application layer directly.** Firms like Anthropic — now public, having completed a record IPO priced at $83 and raising $81.3 billion — compete with incumbent workflow software rather than merely supplying it.
3. **The build-vs-buy calculus shifted.** When code generation is cheap, the "just build it internally" option becomes credible for workflows that previously justified a vendor.
4. **Budget cannibalisation is real and immediate.** Every dollar of enterprise AI spend is competing against an existing software line item in the same IT budget, during the same fiscal year.

#### Where the bear thesis breaks down

The thesis is strongest as a **sector-level narrative** and weakest when applied to **specific business models**. Four distinctions matter enormously, and the market is currently not making them:

**1. Not all software is priced per seat.** Consumption-priced, asset-priced, transaction-priced and network-priced software have the *opposite* exposure. If AI agents increase the number of queries, the volume of telemetry, the count of transactions or the number of monitored entities, then consumption pricing means AI adoption is a **tailwind**. Observability is the cleanest example: agentic AI running in production generates vastly more to monitor, not less. So is supply-chain network software, where revenue scales with trading-partner connections and document volume rather than headcount.

**2. Switching costs are not uniform.** A horizontal productivity tool with a monthly contract is genuinely exposed. A system of record embedded in a municipal government's statutory workflow, with a decade-long contract, mandatory compliance reporting and a multi-year implementation cycle, is not. Regulated and public-sector verticals are structurally insulated from "we'll just build it with an LLM" — because the buyer cannot accept the compliance risk, and the procurement cycle alone outlasts the hype cycle.

**3. The market has stopped distinguishing growth from profitability.** The de-rating has hit 40%-FCF-margin cash machines growing 10% alongside unprofitable 30% growers. Those are opposite risk profiles. The cash-generative, slower-growing name is being punished for a *growth* fear despite deriving most of its value from near-term cash flows that the discount-rate move affects far less.

**4. Some of this is forced selling, not analysis.** A sector down 30% in a year produces fund outflows, which produce indiscriminate selling of the liquid names in the index regardless of fundamentals. Tyler Technologies dropping 6.6% in a day on "sector-wide selling" with no company-specific news, and Dynatrace falling 3% in a move explicitly attributed to broad software weakness "rather than any new company-specific setback," are both symptoms of this.

#### The counter-evidence is accumulating

- **JPMorgan** saw room for a rebound; **Deutsche Bank** upgraded the sector in mid-March.
- **Morningstar** currently rates **SAP at 22% below fair value with a wide moat**, and **Tyler Technologies at 21% undervalued**.
- **Bank of America** named SAP its top large-cap software pick for 2026, with FCF generation on track to reach €8.0bn annually.
- By August 2026, IGV had briefly returned to positive territory for the year as investors rewarded firms demonstrably monetising AI features — evidence that the de-rating is **narrative-driven and reversible**, not a permanent structural repricing.
- **Palo Alto Networks** (reported Nov 18) delivered fiscal Q1 2027 revenue of **$3.45bn, +39% YoY**, against a $3.31bn estimate, and materially raised full-year guidance. Shares jumped 8–9% after hours. That is not the print of an industry being destroyed by AI — though notably, **the gain did not hold**, with the stock quoted at $342.95 after hours on Nov 25, below its close. The market is refusing to pay for good software results right now. That refusal is the opportunity.
- **Workday and HP** were both classified as double beats (EPS and revenue) in the November 24 earnings roundup.

**Model competition is compressing economics.** Google shipped **Gemini 4 Neon on November 18** — reportedly the fourth Gemini 4 variant in about a month — at $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output. An active price war between OpenAI and Google at the model layer is excellent for application-layer software margins and problematic for anyone whose business model depends on inference pricing staying elevated.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

**The memory shortage is the most underappreciated macro story in the market right now, and today it stopped being a semiconductor story.**

**The mechanism.** TrendForce estimated conventional DRAM contract prices rose **93–98% in Q1 2026** quarter-over-quarter, with a further 10–15% expected in Q4. AI data centers are absorbing supply faster than it can be added.

**The transmission event.** On **November 24, Ambarella (AMBA)** guided Q4 revenue to **$70–75 million against $115.2 million** in the quarter just reported — roughly **37% below consensus**. Ambarella does not make memory. Its chips *pair* with memory, and its customers are cutting builds because the DRAM that pairs with its SoCs is too expensive or unobtainable, rationing parts for priority products. Shares fell **34–38%**; Morgan Stanley cut to a $42 target and Bank of America moved to Underperform. The stock was indicated **−31%** pre-market.

**The contagion list is already long.** Intel, Qualcomm, Apple, Ford, Nintendo, Samsung and Amazon have all cited memory costs in 2026 results or forecasts. HPE has reportedly pointed to disruption persisting into 2028.

**This is a classic second-order cost shock, and the market is pricing it first-order.** The market correctly bid up memory makers. It has **not** yet systematically marked down the long tail of businesses whose unit economics depend on cheap DRAM — consumer electronics, automotive, IoT, edge devices, robotics. Ambarella is the first clear casualty, not the last. **Screen your hardware exposure for memory attachment ratio, not for AI narrative.**

**Meanwhile the memory trade itself is wobbling.** Micron fell **7% on November 25** on softer DRAM pricing forecasts and new tariffs, and is **down ~33% in a month despite record revenue** — fiscal 2026 revenue reportedly rose 256% to $133 billion, with management guiding to tighter supply-demand in 2027 and 2028 than 2026. TrendForce puts the start of declining negotiated prices in Q1 2027. **Record fundamentals and a collapsing stock is the signature of a cycle the market believes is peaking.** SanDisk also fell 7%.

Elsewhere: **Broadcom** management guides AI chip revenue to ~$58bn in 2026 (+190% YoY) with projections toward $115bn in FY2027 — though note Broadcom is a memory *buyer*, so the shortage is an input-cost and supply risk. **TSMC** raised full-year 2026 revenue growth guidance.

### 1. GDDY — GoDaddy Inc. (NYSE)
*Sub-sector: SMB web presence, domains & commerce infrastructure*

**Thesis.** A domain-registry and SMB-platform near-monopoly generating **~$1.8bn of free cash flow against a ~$14–15bn market cap** — roughly **8x free cash flow** — while buying back stock aggressively enough to have cut the share count 7%. The market has applied the AI-disruption discount to a business whose revenue is tied to domain renewals and subscription hosting, not to seat counts, and which is itself an AI *deployer* (its Airo product scaled 5x to over $100M in run-rate bookings).

**Valuation Metrics.** Price ~$101.78–106.71 (sources span Nov 17–25). Trailing P/E **~13.9–14.6x** — against a 5-year median of ~30x. Morningstar normalized P/E ~14.3; P/S **~2.7x**. P/FCF **~8x**. Market cap ~$14–15bn. PEG is not meaningful on mid-single-digit revenue growth; the case is explicitly cash-flow-based, not growth-based. 52-week range $71.59–$131.74.

**Growth Profile.** FY26 revenue guidance $5.215–5.255bn, trending to the high end, ~6% growth. Q3 bookings $1.4bn, **+6% YoY** (reported and constant currency), accelerating from +3% in Q1. Q3 FCF **$480.8m, +9% YoY**; TTM FCF ~$1.8bn. Normalized EBITDA margin 33.5%. Management targets a three-year FCF CAGR above 25% and Q4 bookings growth slightly ahead of revenue growth.

**Competitive Moat.** The largest domain registrar globally, with registry scale economics and genuinely high switching costs — migrating a domain, DNS, email and hosting stack is disruptive to a small business with no IT staff. Tens of millions of customers, overwhelmingly subscription and auto-renewal. Critically, **an SMB that wants an AI-built website still needs a domain and hosting**; AI changes what sits on the site, not the registry layer beneath it.

**Key Risk.** AI website builders commoditise the higher-margin "build and manage" layer faster than Airo captures it. Management has already flagged an **A&C bookings slowdown** tied to the Airo transition and declining demand for traditional do-it-for-you services. Secondarily, insider selling of ~$14.1m over trailing twelve months with no open-market purchases is an unhelpful signal, and Evercore ISI cut its target to $95 in February.

**Rating: Strong Buy.** The highest-conviction name here. 8x free cash flow for a toll booth on small-business internet presence, with a shrinking share count, is the clearest valuation anomaly today's screen produced.

### 2. DT — Dynatrace, Inc. (NYSE)
*Sub-sector: Observability & application performance monitoring*

**Thesis.** Observability is the single SaaS sub-sector where **agentic AI is a direct volume tailwind rather than a seat-compression threat** — autonomous AI running in production generates dramatically more telemetry, more entities and more failure modes to monitor. Dynatrace is priced as a victim of the AI narrative while being a structural beneficiary of it, and management is buying stock with a $1bn incremental authorisation.

**Valuation Metrics.** Price quoted between **$43.72 and $55.13** depending on source and date — the lower figure appears the more recent, following the software selloff, and sits near the 200-day average against a 50-day near $56. Forward P/E ~28.2x at $55.13, materially lower at $43.72. Trailing P/E 84–110x on GAAP (not meaningful; heavy SBC amortisation). GuruFocus GF Value **$71.44**. **Growth Profile.** Q2 FY27 (reported Nov 3): revenue **$579.9m, +17% YoY**; total ARR **$2,207m, +16%**; non-GAAP EPS $0.54 vs. $0.49 consensus; non-GAAP operating margin **32%, nearly 200bp above the top of guidance**. FY27 ARR guidance raised to $2,393–2,413m (17.5–18.5% growth including the Arize acquisition; standalone guidance held at 15.5–16.5%).

**Competitive Moat.** Deep agent-based instrumentation creates heavy switching costs — ripping out observability means re-instrumenting the entire production estate, a project nobody undertakes voluntarily. Consumption-based pricing aligns revenue with workload growth. The **Arize acquisition adds AI-observability capability** precisely as monitoring LLM applications in production becomes a mandatory enterprise requirement.

**Key Risk.** **Q2 net new ARR was flat YoY at $70m and below expectations**, with large enterprise deals pushed to the second half — if that is demand weakness rather than timing, the growth thesis breaks. Arize dilutes non-GAAP operating margin by ~175bp (guidance cut to 28–28.25%) and full-year EPS by ~9 cents. The buyback is partly **debt-funded**: $1.41bn of new exchangeable notes, with FY27 diluted share count guided *up* from 295–297m to 306–308m. A buyback that does not shrink the share count is a weaker signal than it appears.

**Rating: Buy.** The second-half ARR reacceleration is the thing to verify. Investor Day is February 16, 2027.

**Competitive Moat.** A genuine **network effect** — the rare one in enterprise software. Retailers mandate that suppliers connect via SPS; each additional retailer makes the network more valuable to suppliers and vice versa. Revenue is overwhelmingly recurring, and the divestiture sharpens focus on the higher-ARPU 1P supplier market. An AI agent cannot replace a network that the counterparty requires you to join.

**Key Risk.** Deceleration may be more than optical — **Amazon-related headwinds** were explicitly cited, and shares fell nearly 5% after hours on the Q3 print as investors judged guidance soft. If retail trading-partner volumes are genuinely contracting, the network's growth algorithm weakens. Activist involvement (Anson Funds, disclosed December 2025, pushing for a sale and management changes) cuts both ways: it could deliver a premium or signal a board under pressure.

**Rating: Buy.** The divestiture-adjusted growth rate and 17% EBITDA growth are not what a 4%-revenue-growth headline implies.

### 6. PRGS — Progress Software Corporation (Nasdaq)

## Risk Factors

**1. A December Fed hike — and the tail risk of 50bp. (Severity: Highest)**
Hike odds of 94–99% are priced; a 25bp move is not the risk. The risk is the **dot plot** signalling a sustained hiking cycle, or Waller's 50bp option being exercised. Core PCE at 3.3% and rising, with no further PCE print before the decision, leaves the Committee reacting to deteriorating data. For long-duration software — including every pick above — the terminal rate assumption is the dominant valuation input. This is the single largest risk to today's slate, and it is explicitly a risk *to these picks*, not merely to the market.

**2. The AI capital cycle is increasingly debt-financed. (Severity: High)**
$600bn of 2026 data-center and silicon spend, Anthropic's ~$900.9bn of committed compute obligations, OpenAI raising ~$30bn at >$1.4tn — with debt financing drawing explicit warnings. The fragility is that this capex is **committed** while the revenue to service it is **forecast**. Any credit-market disruption transmits directly into AI infrastructure demand, and from there into the semiconductor and power complexes.

**3. Memory shortage contagion into end demand. (Severity: High and rising)**
Ambarella's 37% guidance cut is the proof of transmission, not an isolated miss. With DRAM contract prices up 93–98% in Q1 and another 10–15% in Q4, and Intel, Qualcomm, Apple, Ford and Nintendo already citing memory costs, Q4 guidance season across hardware is likely to produce more of these. Simultaneously the memory producers themselves are de-rating — Micron −33% in a month on record revenue — so the trade is uncomfortable at **both** ends.

**4. Nvidia governance overhang and index concentration. (Severity: High)**
A 31.5% single-day move on a CEO decision, substantially retraced in four sessions, demonstrates the index's hostage position to one company's personnel matters. S&P Global Ratings has formally flagged governance risk; export-control and national-security questions remain unresolved. With Nvidia at a $3.6tn market cap — $2tn below its October peak — key-man risk in the largest index weight is now a permanent, priced risk factor.

**5. Thin holiday liquidity amplifying the tape. (Severity: Moderate, acute today)**
A 3.5-hour session with holiday-depleted order books and a VIX at 28.3 is a setup for exaggerated moves in both directions. Today's percentage change carries **materially less information** than a normal session's. Do not update your thesis on today's close, and avoid initiating sized positions into the final thirty minutes.

## Bottom Line

The index-level bounce is real but narrow, retraced and liquidity-distorted — the Nasdaq remains 14% below its high, the VIX sits at 28.3, and the Fed is now priced to **hike** into accelerating core inflation on December 9. The genuine opportunity this week is not chasing the AI relief rally; it is that **enterprise software now trades at or below the S&P 500 multiple for the first time on record**, and the market is applying a single seat-compression narrative indiscriminately to businesses priced on consumption, transactions, network connections and assets — where AI adoption is a volume tailwind, not a threat. Today's six picks are selected precisely on that mismatch, with GDDY at roughly 8x free cash flow the clearest anomaly the screen produced.

Two cautions carry equal weight with that call. First, the December FOMC is the dominant variable for every long-duration name recommended here, and a hawkish dot plot would hurt these positions specifically — this is a valuation call being made *into* a rising discount rate, deliberately and with eyes open. Second, the memory shortage has crossed from a chip-pricing story into an end-demand story, and Ambarella's 37% guidance cut should be read as the first of several rather than an idiosyncratic miss. Accumulate quality software into weakness on a multi-quarter horizon; do not read anything into today's half-session close.
