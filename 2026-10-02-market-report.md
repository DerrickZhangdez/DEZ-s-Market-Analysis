## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The Fed is hiking. This is the single most important fact in the report.

The federal funds target range stands at **3.75%–4.00%** (effective rate 3.88%), set on September 17. The next decision is **October 28**, and fixed-income markets are now "firmly pricing in back-to-back hikes for October and December," which would carry the range to **4.00%–4.25%** and then higher. Sixteen of the eighteen participants in the September Summary of Economic Projections penciled in at least one further quarter-point rise by year-end; four expect two. The median end-2026 policy rate is **4.1%**.

This is not a hawkish-hold. It is a tightening cycle resuming, and the inflation data explains why:

| Measure | 2026 Projection (September SEP) | Prior (June SEP) | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headline PCE | **3.7%** | 3.6% | Revised **up** |
| Core PCE | **3.4%** | 3.3% | Revised **up** |

The Committee does not see a return to the 2% target **until 2029**. That is a three-year admission of persistent overshoot, and it is the reason the long end has broken out: the 10-year at **5.24%** is at levels not seen in 24 years, and it climbed 6bp over the past week even as equities rose.

### What this does to valuation arithmetic

A 5.24% risk-free rate is not a detail; it is the denominator. Three mechanical consequences:

1. **Long-duration equity gets repriced hardest.** This is visible, not theoretical: the cloud/software EV-to-sales premium versus the broad market has compressed from roughly **6x in mid-2021 to ~2x today**.
2. **Floating-rate borrowers get squeezed.** This is the Russell 2000's structural problem — small caps carry disproportionate floating-rate debt, which is why they took "the brunt of the selling" this week and why Q3 was -7.52% against a positive S&P.
3. **Balance sheets become a differentiating asset again.** A company with net cash earns ~5% on it. A company with 5x leverage pays ~7-8% on it. In a zero-rate world that gap was noise. It is now several hundred basis points of annual earnings differential — and it is the explicit reason one of today's five picks is a debt-free company with $1.42B of cash.

### Economic releases and what to watch

The Fed's preferred inflation gauge (PCE) was released Wednesday, September 30, into this setup. The calendar risk now compresses into **October 28 (FOMC)** with the Q3 earnings season running underneath it. Watch three things specifically:

- **Whether companies guide for 2027 margin compression from energy input costs.** Accenture's FY27 guide of +3-6% revenue and adjusted EPS growth is the first large data point and it is notably modest for a company that just beat.
- **Whether the long end keeps rising on a hiking Fed.** Yields retreated from multi-decade highs *during* today's session, which is what let equities close green. If 5.24% becomes 5.50%, the equity math changes materially.
- **Energy pass-through into core.** Energy prices are forecast to rise ~24% on average in 2026 (World Bank). Core PCE at 3.4% already embeds some of that; diesel at record cracks is a freight-cost input into nearly every physical good.

## Geopolitical Factors

Geopolitics is not a sidebar in this market; it is currently the dominant marginal driver of both energy prices and industrial earnings.

### The two-sided oil trade

Brent fell **2.89% to $99.35** and WTI nearly 4% to **$89.35** today on reports of talks over releasing additional **diesel and crude stockpiles** — a coordinated administrative response rather than a change in physical supply. The underlying risk premium remains intact: concerns around security in the **Strait of Hormuz** continue to amplify volatility, and the market has a demonstrated sensitivity that is worth quantifying precisely, because it defines the risk on every energy-levered position:

> A ceasefire announcement in Gaza can take **$3 off Brent**. A missile strike near Iranian oil infrastructure can add **$8 within hours**.

That is an $11 range of pure headline risk sitting on top of a $99 barrel. No fundamental model survives contact with it, which is why today's picks deliberately avoid names whose earnings are a direct linear function of the crude price.

### The refined-products dislocation is separate from, and larger than, the crude story

This distinction matters more than any other single item in today's spotlight:

- **Russia** normally produces roughly **5.5 million bpd** of refined products. That output has fallen by an estimated **25-30%**.
- Combined with refinery strikes and outages, this drove the **diesel crack spread to a record ~$102/bbl — roughly five times the normal level.**
- The WTI 3-2-1 crack sits near **$59/bbl**, having nearly tripled since January.
- The oil market ran a **3.7 mb/d deficit in 2026Q2** on reduced Middle East production.

Crude releases cannot fix a *refining* shortage. Releasing crude into a market short of distillation capacity moves the crude price (as it did today, -3%) while leaving the product shortage — and therefore the crack — largely intact. That asymmetry is why refiner equities have been able to compound while crude chops.

### Trade and second-order effects

Today's Nike print carried an under-discussed geopolitical signature: revenue fell 4.3% year-over-year and the weakness propagated immediately into **Adidas, Puma and JD Sports** in European trading. Global consumer-brand supply chains are transmitting demand softness across borders faster than they transmit cost inflation, which compresses margins at both ends.

Flowserve — a company discussed at length below — explicitly guided full-year **organic sales down ~1% specifically citing "the continued impact of Middle East conflict"** on project execution, while simultaneously booking orders up 26%. That is the geopolitical risk premium showing up not as a price, but as a *delay*.

### Today's Spotlight: Industrials, Energy, Materials & "Picks-and-Shovels"

This bucket is where the macro regime described above actually cashes out. Three structural flows are running simultaneously:

**Flow 1 — Public infrastructure money is still largely unspent.** Roughly **60% of IIJA funds have yet to be deployed**, and public infrastructure spending is expected to remain the construction industry's strongest demand driver in 2026. This is the rarest thing in the current market: a multi-year revenue stream that is *insensitive to the 10-year Treasury* because it is appropriated, not financed. Knife River's record **$1.3B backlog** and Eagle Materials' heavy-materials growth (+10% to ~$1.43B) are both expressions of it.

**Flow 2 — Gas and power infrastructure is being built for AI.** North American natural gas demand is set for a historic increase on LNG exports plus data-center power load. This flows to contract compression (Archrock's 2027-2030 growth capex guidance of **$1.4–1.6B cumulative**), to midstream (Targa's Q2 adjusted EBITDA of **$1,603M, +38% YoY**), and to structures and grid hardware (Valmont raising its Infrastructure outlook to **$3.3–3.45B**). The "picks-and-shovels of AI" trade has migrated out of semiconductors and into steel, gas and galvanizing.

**Flow 3 — Energy capex is being *booked* but not yet *delivered*.** Flowserve's first-half bookings of **$2,495.7M** (vs $2,299.4M), Q2 bookings of **$1.35B (+26%)**, record aftermarket bookings of **$696M**, and backlog of **$3.3B (+16.3% since December)** — against guided organic sales of *minus 1%* — is the cleanest statement of this flow in the market. Demand is being signed. Revenue is being deferred by conflict-driven project timing.

#### The refiner question — and why the answer is no

Refining is, on the surface, the single most obvious trade in this spotlight. **Marathon, Valero and HF Sinclair have each gained over 80% in 2026 against an 11% S&P 500 gain.** HF Sinclair screens at a **P/E of 8.97**, Ovintiv at **8.34**, EOG at **9.19 forward**, Equinor at **9.79 forward**. By any naive screen these are the cheapest large businesses in the market.

They are excluded from today's picks, deliberately, for a reason worth stating plainly:

> **A single-digit P/E computed on cyclical-peak earnings is not a low valuation. It is a high valuation wearing a disguise.**

The $102 diesel crack is five times normal. Valero's own COO, Gary Simmons, has argued the mid-cycle margin floor has shifted *structurally* higher on European hydroskimming economics and compliance costs — and that argument is probably correct. But as the sell-side commentary itself concedes, **"structurally higher" is not "permanently at $102."** If the crack normalizes even halfway toward historical levels, the "8x P/E" becomes a 20x+ P/E on unchanged price, and the equity does not get there gracefully. CNBC has twice flagged (August 17 and September 14) that the refiner run is "nearly unprecedented" and that history suggests it ends.

The trigger is explicit and binary: **"a ceasefire in the Gulf that actually holds would push crack spreads sharply lower, and take the refiners with it."** That is a geopolitical coin-flip, not a fundamental thesis. A report whose mandate is "only recommend stocks where the valuation is justified by fundamentals — no hype picks" cannot own a position whose entire earnings base is a hostage negotiation. Noted, analyzed, **not purchased.**

#### The momentum-metals question — also no

Specialty metals are the other obvious call, and also excluded. **ATI has rallied 177% over the past year; Carpenter Technology 111.2%.** Both have genuinely excellent fundamentals: Carpenter guided FY26 operating income to **$680–700M (+30-33% over FY25)** with Specialty Alloys net sales up 10% to **$2,826.8M** and segment operating margin of **27.7%** (34.7% ex-surcharge); ATI lifted FY26 adjusted EBITDA guidance to **$1.01–1.06B**. Aerospace build rates and defense spending are real and durable.

But CRS trades at a forward price-to-sales of **6.07** and ATI at **4.04**. Those are software multiples on a metals business with a surcharge-driven revenue line. The fundamentals justify a premium; they do not justify paying 6x sales for a specialty alloys producer after a 111% run. Excluded on valuation, not on business quality.

#### Where the spotlight actually yields value

Stripping out the peak-cycle names and the momentum names leaves the genuinely interesting residue, and it has a consistent character: **businesses with contracted or appropriated revenue, aftermarket annuities, or fortress balance sheets, trading at 14–19x forward earnings.** That residue is today's pick list.

Two sub-sectors within the spotlight were examined and rejected on structure rather than valuation:

- **Gas compression — leverage screen.** Kodiak Gas Services posted genuinely strong numbers (record Compression Infrastructure revenue of **$315.1M**, record adjusted EBITDA of **$216.8M, +21.7% YoY**, FY26 EBITDA guidance raised to **$820–860M**). But it carries **$2.83B of total debt at roughly 5.6x net debt/EBITDA** and trades at a *premium* to peer median EV/EBITDA (~10-11x forward). In a cycle where the Fed is hiking into 2027, paying a premium multiple for 5.6x leverage is the wrong side of the rate trade. Archrock — materially less levered, 8.25x EV/forward-EBITDA, 3.1x dividend coverage — is the preferred expression of an identical thesis.
- **Midstream — adjacency and valuation.** Targa is excellent and expensive (one intrinsic-value model marks it **overvalued by ~15%** against a $291.35 price). Antero Midstream's P/E "nearing 20" was flagged as pushing valuation to "uncomfortable highs" despite solid 2026 free-cash-flow guidance of **$755–815M before dividends**. DT Midstream screens better (fair value lifted to **$153.33** on a **$3.4B growth capital outlook**) but sits adjacent to a name already taken in the one-month look-back. Excluded.

The broader context for the energy-services valuation argument: the oil and gas field services industry trades at a trailing EV/EBITDA of **7.04x against the S&P 500's 18.59x**. That is a 62% discount to the market for an industry whose end-market capital spending is being driven by both LNG exports and AI power demand. Not every name in it is cheap — but the sector-level discount is real and is the backdrop for the Archrock recommendation.

### Artificial Intelligence

The AI capital cycle continues to escalate in magnitude while narrowing in beneficiary. The five largest US cloud and AI infrastructure providers — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle — have collectively committed to **$660–690 billion of capital expenditure in 2026.**

**Oracle / OpenAI — Stargate operational.** The flagship Stargate data center in **Abilene, Texas is now live and operational**, with Oracle Cloud Infrastructure powering its **1.2 GW** campus; SB Energy will build, own and operate under a **20-year lease to OpenAI**. OpenAI's July agreement to rent **4.5 GW** of Oracle capacity in the US is now partly in service. Stargate is expanding toward **~7 gigawatts of planned capacity and over $400 billion of investment**, and Oracle raised FY2026 capex guidance by **$15 billion to $50 billion**.

**NVIDIA.** The company paid its quarterly cash dividend of **$0.25 per share on October 1**, and in fiscal Q2 2027 introduced a new business model with select AI cloud partners intended to broaden access to its data-center infrastructure products — a structural change worth watching, since it alters how revenue is recognized across the partner channel.

**The investable read-through is increasingly physical.** Seven gigawatts of planned Stargate capacity is not primarily a chip order; it is a power, cooling, structural steel, switchgear, gas-turbine and transmission order with a 20-year lease attached. The marginal dollar of AI capex is migrating from silicon toward the things that have to be welded, galvanized, compressed and energized — which is precisely why today's spotlight bucket and today's AI story are converging. Natural gas compression (Archrock), utility and transmission structures (Valmont), and engineered flow control for power and process (Flowserve) are all downstream of this number in a way they were not two years ago.

**The concentration risk cuts both ways.** A $660-690B capex commitment from five buyers is an extraordinary revenue stream and an extraordinary single point of failure. Any one of those five signaling digestion would hit not only semiconductors but the entire industrial supply chain now being valued on it.

### Hardware & Semiconductors

**The onsemi / Synaptics deal was restructured, and the restructuring is the story.** On October 1, onsemi amended its merger agreement with Synaptics, **converting an all-stock transaction into an all-cash acquisition at $123 per share** and reducing the headline value from roughly **$7 billion to approximately $5.7 billion**. A supply-constrained commodity with three years of visibility and pre-sold capacity is an unusual structure, and it is the mechanism by which AI capex converts into durable semiconductor pricing power rather than a one-year order spike.

### 1. KNF — Knife River Corporation *(Construction materials & aggregates)*

**Thesis.** Knife River is the cleanest publicly traded claim on the ~60% of IIJA infrastructure funding that has been appropriated but not yet spent — revenue that is *legislated* rather than *financed*, and therefore uniquely insensitive to a 5.24% 10-year Treasury. A record **$1.3 billion backlog** against roughly $3.5B of annual revenue gives unusual forward visibility in a quarter when visibility is the scarcest commodity in the market.

**Valuation Metrics**
- Trailing P/E: **20.51**
- Forward P/E: **18.19**
- EV/EBITDA: **9.08**
- P/S: **~1.26** *(derived: ~$4.35B approximate market cap ÷ $3.45B 2026E revenue)*
- PEG: **~1.29** *(derived: 18.19 forward P/E ÷ 14.13% 3-yr EPS growth forecast)*

**Growth Profile.** 2026 revenue guidance of **$3.4–3.6B** with adjusted EBITDA of **$520–560M**; consensus models $3.45B revenue and **$2.76 EPS**. Three-year forecast revenue growth of **6.08%** with **14.13%** EPS growth — margin expansion, not just volume. Aggregates volumes and pricing are both guided up mid-single-digits, which is the combination that drives operating leverage in this business.

**Competitive Moat.** Aggregates are the best local-monopoly economics in public markets. Crushed stone has a freight radius of roughly 25-50 miles before transport cost exceeds product value, permitting new quarries takes years and is often politically impossible, and the reserves themselves are irreplaceable. A competitor cannot enter your market; they can only buy your quarry. Knife River pairs this with vertical integration into ready-mix and asphalt, capturing the margin on its own aggregate.

**Key Risk.** Public infrastructure revenue is appropriated but *timing* is controlled by state DOTs, which have been chronically slow to convert authorization into active construction. A funding stream that is insensitive to interest rates is still sensitive to bureaucracy. Secondarily, the private non-residential and residential portions of the mix remain fully exposed to 5%+ financing costs.

**Data disagreement — disclosed.** Analyst price targets diverge sharply: one source reports a consensus target of **$85.38** (15 analysts: 11 buy, 4 hold, 0 sell) against a recent price of ~$76.29; another reports an average target of **$104.11**. That is a 22% spread between "consensus" figures. The lower, better-documented target is the one this thesis relies on; the $104.11 figure should not be treated as the base case.

**Rating: Buy**

Combined with the already-visible margin compression above, a copper reversal would hit an earnings line that is currently showing no growth — which is the specific scenario in which a 16x multiple becomes expensive rather than cheap.

Projected 2026 revenue growth of roughly 12.4%. The quarterly dividend of **$0.23** was raised ~10% year-over-year with 3.1x coverage, which is a conservative payout for this asset class.

**Competitive Moat.** Compression horsepower is genuinely scarce — fleet additions require long-lead equipment orders, and utilization across the industry has been running high. Once a compressor is installed at a wellhead or gathering facility, switching providers means an operational interruption to production, which producers avoid. The result is contracted, fee-based, recurring revenue with structurally low churn and minimal direct commodity-price exposure.

**Key Risk.** This is a capital-intensive fleet business in a rising-rate environment, and its growth plan is explicitly capital-funded through 2030. ### 4. FLS — Flowserve Corporation *(Flow control — pumps, seals, valves & aftermarket)*

**Thesis.** Flowserve is booking orders at **+26%** while guiding revenue to **-1%** — a dislocation caused by geopolitical project-timing delays, not demand destruction, and one that has parked **$3.3 billion of backlog** (up 16.3% in six months) on the balance sheet awaiting conversion. The stock trades roughly 16% below its February all-time high at **17.7x forward earnings**. You are being paid to wait out a delay that the company has already been paid for.

**Valuation Metrics**
- Trailing P/E: **26.5–26.7**
- Forward P/E (FY2026): **17.7**
- EV/EBITDA: **not cleanly sourced** — the gap between trailing and forward P/E implies substantial expected earnings growth; absence of a verified EV/EBITDA figure is a genuine gap in this analysis and is disclosed as such
- P/S: **~2.2x** *(derived: ~$9.74B market capitalization against ~$4.4B estimated annual revenue)*
- PEG: **~1.0 or below** *(derived and approximate, against the implied earnings ramp from 26.5x trailing to 17.7x forward)*

**Growth Profile.** FY2026 adjusted EPS guidance was **raised to $4.05–4.20**. First-half bookings reached **$2,495.7M versus $2,299.4M** a year prior; Q2 bookings alone were **$1.35B, up 26%**, including **record aftermarket bookings of $696M**. Backlog stood at **$3.3B at June 30, up $468.2M or 16.3%** versus December 31, 2025 — and **up 9% year-over-year excluding the Trillium acquisition**, confirming the growth is organic rather than acquired. Against all of that, organic sales are guided **down approximately 1%**, explicitly attributed to "the continued impact of Middle East conflict."

**Competitive Moat.** The aftermarket is the business. Flowserve pumps, mechanical seals and valves are *specified into* refineries, chemical plants, power stations and water infrastructure at the design stage and remain in service for decades. Replacement parts and service are sole-sourced or near-sole-sourced because requalifying a component in a hazardous-process installation is expensive and slow. **Record aftermarket bookings of $696M in a single quarter** is the moat printing money — that revenue stream is far less cyclical than the original-equipment line, and it is growing faster.

**Key Risk.** The same Middle East conflict that is inflating refining margins is delaying Flowserve's project execution — and the geopolitical resolution that would relieve the delay (a durable Gulf ceasefire) is also the event that would reduce energy-sector capital spending, which drives future bookings. The thesis is therefore exposed on both ends of the geopolitical outcome distribution, just with different timing. A secondary risk is backlog quality: $3.3B of backlog is only worth its margin, and conversion at depressed pricing would undercut the EPS ramp embedded in the forward multiple.

**Rating: Strong Buy** — the clearest demand-versus-revenue dislocation identified in today's screen, with the aftermarket annuity providing a floor under the thesis.

**Thesis.** Valmont makes the galvanized steel poles, transmission structures and lighting that grid expansion physically requires, and management is actively shifting its revenue mix *toward* that higher-margin infrastructure segment and *away* from agriculture — raising the Infrastructure outlook to **$3.3–3.45B** while trimming Agriculture to **$0.9–0.95B**. That is a quiet upgrade to earnings quality that the multiple has not fully recognized.

**Valuation Metrics**

- EV/EBITDA: **13.75x NTM**
- P/S: **~2.2x** *(derived: ~$9.5B approximate market capitalization against $4.3–4.5B revenue guidance)*

**Growth Profile.** FY2026 EPS guidance of **$22.25–23.50** against consensus of $22.83, on revenue guidance of **$4.3–4.5B**. The segment mix shift is the substance: infrastructure carries structurally higher margins than agriculture, so reallocating guided revenue from the latter to the former raises blended margin even at flat consolidated revenue. **Competitive Moat.** Specification lock-in with electric utilities, which qualify structural suppliers through long approval cycles and rarely re-qualify; a hot-dip galvanizing network whose fixed-cost footprint is difficult and environmentally expensive to replicate; and multi-decade relationships with transmission and distribution customers whose capital plans now extend a decade out on grid-hardening and data-center interconnection demand.

A further leg down in agriculture would offset the infrastructure mix benefit. The valuation premium to the industry median leaves little cushion if that happens.

**Rating: Accumulate** — the business quality and mix shift are genuine, but at a 43% premium to its industry median this is the one name on today's list where the price already reflects a meaningful share of the thesis. Sized accordingly.

## Risk Factors

**Ranked by severity for the week ahead.**

### 1. The Fed hikes into a 5.24% long bond — SEVERE

This is the dominant risk and it is not fully priced in equities. Markets expect **back-to-back hikes on October 28 and in December**, carrying the funds rate to 4.00-4.25% and beyond, against core PCE revised *up* to 3.4% and no return to target until **2029**. The equity market is still trading at 7,666 on the S&P with an 11.8% YTD gain. The specific danger is not the hike — it is the long end: if the 10-year moves from 5.24% toward 5.50% on a hawkish October statement, every multiple in this report compresses mechanically, and the longest-duration assets (software, already down $2T) compress most. **Watch: the October 28 statement language on the 2029 convergence path.**

### 2. Gulf resolution or escalation — both are losses, in different portfolios — SEVERE

The market's energy exposure is now a straddle with no cheap side. **A durable ceasefire takes $3 off Brent immediately and collapses the $102 diesel crack, taking the refiner complex — up 80%+ YTD — down with it**, and with it a meaningful share of this year's industrial earnings growth. **An escalation near Iranian oil infrastructure adds $8 to Brent within hours**, pushes energy's 2026 average increase past the forecast 24%, feeds directly into a core PCE already running at 3.4%, and hands the Fed justification for a third hike. The Strait of Hormuz remains the chokepoint. There is no configuration of this risk that is good for a balanced portfolio, which is the argument for owning contracted and appropriated revenue rather than commodity-price-levered revenue.

### 3. Small-cap and floating-rate credit stress — HIGH

The Russell 2000 entered this week with a **14-day RSI of 30.9**, having absorbed "the brunt of the selling," after a **-7.52% third quarter** against a +2.03% S&P. The mechanism is floating-rate debt meeting a hiking Fed. Today's downgrade of **First Busey (BUSE)** from Overweight to Neutral with a target cut from $35 to $30 is a small but real regional-banking data point. The transmission channel to watch is credit spreads, not equity prices: small-cap equity weakness is a symptom; a widening in high-yield spreads would be the confirmation that it is becoming a solvency question rather than a valuation one.

### 4. AI capex concentration — HIGH

Five buyers have committed **$660-690 billion** of 2026 capital expenditure, and Stargate alone is scaling toward **~7 GW and $400B+**. This is now the load-bearing beam under semiconductors, power equipment, industrial structures, gas compression and a large share of the S&P's earnings growth. Oracle has raised FY26 capex by $15B to $50B — financed, not self-funded, in a 5.24% rate environment. The risk is not that AI fails; it is that **one** of the five signals digestion, and the entire physical supply chain re-rates on a single conference call. The exposure is broader than it appears because the industrial complex has been bid on it.

### 5. Software de-rating spillover and the AI monetization question — MODERATE

Software has shed **~$2 trillion** over twelve months with SaaS down 32% YTD, and the cloud EV/sales premium has compressed from 6x to 2x the market. The unresolved question — whether seat-based SaaS can monetize AI rather than be disintermediated by it — has no near-term answer and a wide outcome distribution. Progress Software's **8.5% decline on a 0.5% revenue miss** demonstrates the asymmetry of the current setup: the sector is priced for zero error. The moderate ranking reflects that much of this damage is already realized rather than prospective, but a further leg down would pressure index-level earnings estimates.

## Bottom Line

The index level is lying to you. Beneath a +0.19% S&P close sits a market violently re-sorting itself around a 5.24% long bond, a Fed that is hiking rather than cutting into 3.4% core inflation, and a record $102 diesel crack — and the names that have won are increasingly the ones with contracted, appropriated or specified-in revenue rather than the ones with the best growth story. **The discipline that matters this week is refusing to buy peak-cycle earnings at single-digit multiples**: the refiners are the most obvious trade in the market and the one most likely to end on a headline nobody can forecast.

Today's slate instead buys the capital cycle at the point where it is least commodity-levered — legislated infrastructure aggregates (KNF), a debt-free manufacturer with $1.42B of cash earning 5% while levered peers refinance (MLI), contracted gas compression at 8.25x forward EBITDA (AROC), a flow-control aftermarket annuity booking +26% while revenue is guided -1% (FLS), and utility structures shifting mix toward higher-margin infrastructure (VMI). In a hiking cycle, the balance sheet *is* the thesis; growth you have to finance at 5% is worth materially less than growth someone has already contracted to pay you for.
