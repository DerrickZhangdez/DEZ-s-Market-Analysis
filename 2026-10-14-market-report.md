## Macro & Monetary Environment

### The September CPI print — the day's defining event

| Measure | Actual | Consensus | Prior |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headline CPI, YoY | **3.7%** | 3.6% | 3.4% |
| Core CPI, YoY | **2.6%** | 2.5% | — |
| Core CPI, MoM | **+0.3%** | +0.2% | — |

The composition matters more than the headline. **Gasoline rose 9.1% in the month and contributed more than half the entire monthly increase.** That is an energy-supply shock passing through the index, not a demand-driven broadening of inflation — a distinction with real policy consequences, discussed below.

But the core beat is the genuinely uncomfortable part. Core strips out food and energy precisely so that episodes like this can be seen through, and core still ran hotter than expected on both the annual and monthly reads. A +0.3% monthly core, annualized, is roughly 3.7% — nowhere near target. The market cannot dismiss this print as purely an oil artifact, and that is why yields moved as violently as they did.

### Market reaction

- 10-year Treasury yield: **5.19% → 5.29%**, a roughly 10 basis point move on the print.
- S&P 500 futures turned negative, down ~0.3%.
- Gold extended losses, consistent with a higher-real-rate impulse rather than an inflation-hedge bid — a notable tell. When gold falls on a hot CPI, the market is pricing *tighter policy*, not *inflation*.

Context for the yield move: both the 10-year and 30-year hit fresh **24-year highs** in the prior week. This is not a market drifting toward restrictive territory — it is already there, and today pushed it further.

### The Federal Reserve

The policy setup is the inverse of the one investors spent 2024–2025 positioned for:

- **Current target range: 3.75–4.00%**, following a **unanimous 12–0 quarter-point hike at the September meeting — the first increase since 2023.** The FOMC statement explicitly linked the move to "geopolitical developments," which is central-bank language for the energy shock.
- **Chair: Kevin Warsh**, who has deliberately curtailed forward guidance. This materially raises event risk around each meeting: with less guidance, the market has less ability to pre-price outcomes, and realized volatility around FOMC dates should be expected to run higher than the 2015–2024 norm.
- **Dot plot:** median FOMC member expects **one additional hike in 2026**, and no cuts or hikes in 2027.
- **Market pricing:** fed funds futures around **4.2%** by year-end; some sources put the implied terminal range at 4.00–4.25%. These are close but not identical readings.
- **Inflation projections:** September projections put 2026 headline PCE at **3.7%** and core PCE at **3.4%**, raised from June's 3.6%/3.3% and sharply above March's 2.7%/2.7%. Core PCE has run above 3% **every month of 2026**.
- **Policymakers do not expect a return to 2% inflation until 2029.**

**Next FOMC: October 27–28** — thirteen days away, and now the single most important date on the calendar.

### How to read today's print for Fed purposes

There is a genuine analytical tension here, and it is worth being explicit about both sides rather than asserting one.

*The case that the Fed looks through it:* Central banks conventionally look through supply-driven energy spikes, because hiking does not produce barrels. The gasoline contribution was more than half the monthly move. Bloomberg's pre-release framing was that a moderating core would be enough to keep the Fed on hold. If you believe the Hormuz risk premium is transitory, the right policy response is patience.

*The case that it forces their hand:* Core beat on both measures, so the "it's only energy" defense is incomplete. More importantly, with inflation having run above 3% for the entire year and the Fed's own projections conceding no return to target until 2029, the committee's tolerance for "looking through" another supply shock is close to exhausted — the credibility cost of repeatedly explaining away overshoots compounds. A central bank that has already hiked once explicitly citing geopolitics has signaled it is *not* in look-through mode. CNBC notes most policymakers still expect another increase this year.

**My read:** the second case is stronger, and I would position for a hike or an explicitly hawkish hold on October 28 rather than a dovish pivot. The asymmetry matters more than the base case: if the Fed holds dovishly, duration-sensitive and long-duration equity assets rally modestly; if it hikes with a hawkish statement, the 10-year tests 5.50% and multiple compression is broad and fast. The payoff profile argues for owning short-duration cash flows and pricing power — which is precisely how today's stock selection is constructed.

## Geopolitical Factors

This is the most consequential section of today's report, because geopolitics is currently the proximate driver of inflation, which is the proximate driver of rates, which is the proximate driver of equity multiples. The entire causal chain starts here.

### Middle East — the dominant risk

- **Saudi Aramco facilities were attacked**, per Saudi officials. Houthi strikes against Saudi Arabia are ongoing.
- **U.S. Central Command publicly rejected Iranian claims of control over the Strait of Hormuz.** The EIA estimates roughly **20 million barrels per day** transited the strait in 2024 — about **20% of global petroleum liquids consumption**. This is the single largest chokepoint in global energy, and claims and counterclaims over its control are not background noise.
- Brent settled near **$107** on October 13, its highest close since mid-September, trading around $105.90 earlier that day. ING attributes a persistent risk premium to Houthi activity and Persian Gulf flow risk.
- **Paul Tudor Jones has said oil could rally another $20–25** on the Iran conflict. That would put Brent in the $125–132 range and, mechanically, add materially to headline CPI in subsequent prints.

### Offsetting and ambiguous factors

- A **Russia–Ukraine energy ceasefire** and a prospective **Russian diesel export deal** briefly pulled prices lower, but the weakness did not hold.
- **Saudi Arabia raised production last month** despite shipping disruption, per OPEC.
- **Trump stated the U.S. will not attack Iran before the midterm elections**, which some traders read as easing near-term escalation risk. The dollar slipped on this. I would treat this as a *deferral* of risk past early November rather than a removal of it — and note that a pre-committed timeline is itself information an adversary can act on.

### Energy prices beyond crude

- **European natural gas reached its highest level since December 2022.** This is an underappreciated data point with direct equity implications: it simultaneously (a) crushes European industrial competitiveness, (b) creates a structural bid for U.S. LNG exports, and (c) advantages U.S. energy-intensive manufacturers against European competitors. It is the single most actionable geopolitical fact in today's report, and it informs the first stock pick below.
- **Hurricane-related Gulf of Mexico outages have shut in approximately 605,000 b/d.** Weather-driven and therefore genuinely transitory, but it is stacking on top of a geopolitical premium, and the two together produced the gasoline print.

### Second-order effects

Asian equities fell sharply on October 13 as higher energy prices fed inflation worries and bond selloffs. The transmission mechanism — energy → inflation → yields → equity multiples → global risk appetite — is currently operating with unusual speed and tightness. Investors who treat the oil price as a sector story rather than a macro story are mispricing the correlation structure of their entire portfolio.

#### The macro setup for this bucket is unusually favorable — with one critical caveat

Industrials, energy and materials are, right now, the sectors *causing* the inflation that is hurting everything else. That is a rare and valuable position. Specifically:

1. **Pricing power is real and currently being exercised.** Gasoline +9.1% in a month is the most visible instance, but it extends through refined products, marine freight and specialty metals.
2. **Short-duration cash flows are an asset when the discount rate is rising.** A business earning cash today is mathematically less damaged by a 5.29% 10-year than one earning cash in 2032. This is the precise opposite of the 2020–2021 regime, and most investors' mental models have not fully re-adjusted.
3. **Replacement-cost dynamics favor incumbents.** When building new capacity costs more every quarter, existing capacity appreciates and new entrants are deterred. This is the quiet moat-widener of an inflationary regime.

**The caveat, stated plainly: this is a late-cycle setup, and late-cycle commodity exposure is where investors reliably destroy capital.** The characteristic error is buying a commodity producer at trough multiples on peak earnings. A refiner earning $36/bbl margins at 6x earnings is not cheap — it is expensive on normalized earnings and the multiple is low *because* the market correctly anticipates mean reversion. I have applied this filter aggressively below, and it is the reason the most obvious beneficiary of today's news is **not** among my picks.

#### Where the fresh opportunity actually is

**1. U.S. LNG export infrastructure — the cleanest expression of the European gas dislocation.**
European gas at its highest since December 2022 is not a trading blip; it is a structural demand signal for U.S. liquefaction capacity. The attractive feature is that the best-positioned operator monetizes this through **long-term fixed-fee take-or-pay contracts**, which means the investor is not actually making a directional bet on gas prices — they are buying a contracted toll road with a commodity-linked option attached. That is a materially better risk-adjusted structure than owning the commodity, and it is why Cheniere is my highest-conviction name today.

**2. Metal packaging — the defensive anchor nobody looks at.**
Beverage cans are a consolidated oligopoly with contractual aluminum pass-throughs, meaning input inflation is largely neutralized. Volumes grew 5% last quarter. This is the lowest-oil-beta name in today's slate and deliberately so: with two energy-linked picks, the portfolio needs a counterweight that still benefits from pricing power.

**3. Specialty aerospace alloys — a genuine qualification moat.**
Aerospace-grade alloys require multi-year customer qualification per part per program. That is one of the most durable moats in all of industrials, and it is currently colliding with a sold-out capacity position and a defense spending cycle.

**4. Jones Act marine transport — regulatory moat plus structural supply shortage.**
The Jones Act restricts domestic waterborne freight to U.S.-built, U.S.-crewed vessels. Combine a statutory barrier to entry with essentially no new barge construction and utilization in the low 90s, and pricing power follows mechanically. Rising refined-product volumes — exactly what today's gasoline data implies — drive the demand side.

**5. Oilfield pressure control — the picks-and-shovels play proper.**
If Brent holds near $107, U.S. onshore activity responds. The equipment supplier with a net cash balance sheet captures that without the reserve-depletion treadmill or the commodity-price leverage of an E&P. This is the most literal interpretation of "picks-and-shovels" available in this bucket.

### Software & SaaS

Software has been the year's casualty, and the scale is historic: roughly **$2 trillion of market value lost**, with IGV down about **26% YTD** at its low. **J.P. Morgan characterized it as the industry's largest non-recessionary drawdown in more than 30 years** and called the selling "indiscriminate." Goldman Sachs warned it may be "the end of the beginning." Price-to-sales multiples compressed from roughly **9x to 6x**.

The thesis driving it: AI agents eroding per-seat SaaS licensing economics. The sourced material on this episode largely dates from February through April 2026, so the *current* state of the de-rating is less well documented than its origin — a genuine gap in today's research that I flag rather than paper over.

The analytical question that matters is whether per-seat pricing erosion is a *universal* software problem or a *segment-specific* one. It is almost certainly the latter. Vendors whose value is headcount-proportional (generic productivity, basic CRM seats, tier-one support tooling) face real structural pressure. Vendors whose value is consumption-, transaction- or data-volume-proportional — and those embedded in regulated workflows with switching costs measured in years — do not face the same mechanism, and some benefit as AI agents *increase* consumption of underlying data and compute. A 26% drawdown applied indiscriminately across both groups is, as J.P. Morgan says, indiscriminate — and indiscriminate selling is where valuation-based investors eventually find work. I am not picking from this group today, because the spotlight rotation points elsewhere and because the de-rating's current state is insufficiently documented in today's research to underwrite with confidence. It belongs on the watchlist.

### 3. CRS — Carpenter Technology Corporation *(Specialty alloys — aerospace & defense)*

**Thesis.** One of the most durable qualification moats in industrials — aerospace alloys require multi-year certification per part per program — colliding with sold-out capacity, a defense spending cycle and management guidance for 20%+ earnings growth over several years.

**Valuation Metrics.** Shares **$377–$388** (approximately $377.35 on October 13; $388.47 on October 2). **Trailing P/E ~36** (35.90 at the October 13 price; 36.68 against trailing EPS of $10.59). **Forward P/E 28.25** per GuruFocus as of October 4. Market capitalization approximately **$19.1 billion**. Against guided growth of 21–25%, **PEG is roughly 1.1–1.3**.

**I am disclosing the bear case on valuation rather than burying it.** GuruFocus rates the stock **"Significantly Overvalued"** against a GF Value of **$213.27** — far below market. Simply Wall St noted in February that CRS traded at 41.8x against peers at 36.9x and above its own fair ratio of 36.4x, flagging valuation risk if expectations cool. Fair value estimates across sources span **$213 to $722**, which is an extraordinarily wide dispersion and an honest signal that this name's value depends almost entirely on whether the growth plan executes. Offsetting: the current multiple sits roughly **49% below its own ten-year average of ~72x**.

**Growth Profile.** The FY26 Q4 deck projects **FY27 operating income 21–25% above the record FY26 level**, with an FY29 target implying a **20–23% earnings CAGR**. *Caveat: the deck's operating income table does not cleanly reconcile to those percentages — verify against the primary filing.* Fiscal Q3 2026 EPS of **$2.77 beat consensus of $2.59**. Consensus projects EPS from **$10.56 to $12.36 (+17%)** next year. A brownfield capacity expansion begins in FY28.

**Competitive Moat.** Specialty alloys for aerospace and defense must be qualified by the customer for each specific part on each specific program — a process taking years and, once complete, effectively locking in the supplier for the program's life. Capacity in this niche is genuinely scarce and cannot be added quickly, which is why the company can guide to multi-year double-digit growth with credibility.

**Key Risk.** **The multiple is the risk.** At 28x forward, the stock prices in successful execution of the growth plan; a slip in aerospace build rates, a defense budget disruption, or expansion delays would compress both earnings and the multiple simultaneously. The dispersion in fair-value estimates ($213 to $722) is the quantitative expression of this. This is a quality business at a full price, not a bargain.

**Rating: Accumulate.** Rated below Buy deliberately — the business merits ownership, the entry price merits patience and scaling in rather than a full position at once.

### 4. KEX — Kirby Corporation *(Jones Act inland marine transport & distribution)*

**Thesis.** A statutory barrier to entry combined with essentially no new barge construction has produced low-90s utilization and genuine pricing power — and today's refined-product dynamics drive the demand side directly.

**Valuation Metrics.** Shares around **$140** with a market capitalization of approximately **$7.4 billion**. *Price caveat: the most recent verifiable quote I could source is $140.77 from September 4, with other pages showing undated prices of $139–140. Verify against a live quote.* Against 2026 EPS tracking toward roughly **$6.50–$7.00** (Q1 $1.50, Q2 $1.67, guidance trending to the upper end of 5–15% growth), that implies roughly **20–21x**. Management repurchased **$59.7 million of stock in Q2 at an average price of $142.38** — a useful signal of where the people with the best information saw value. Full-year operating cash flow guidance **$575–$675 million**.

**Growth Profile.** Q1 2026 EPS **$1.50, +13% YoY**. Q2 EPS **$1.67**, flat year-over-year but **+11% sequentially**. Management **raised** full-year EPS growth guidance to **5–15%** from 0–12%, and subsequently indicated results should trend toward the **upper end** of that range. Inland barge **utilization in the low-90% range**, average spot rates **up low-to-mid single digits sequentially**, and term contract renewals **up low single digits** year-over-year.

**Competitive Moat.** The **Jones Act** restricts domestic waterborne freight to U.S.-built, U.S.-crewed, U.S.-flagged vessels — a statutory moat that cannot be competed away, only legislated away. Layered on top: **limited new barge construction continues to support stable market fundamentals**, meaning supply is structurally constrained even as demand grows. Kirby operates the largest inland tank barge fleet. The company is also adding capacity opportunistically rather than building it — **23 barges and three high-horsepower boats acquired for $95.8 million** — which is accretive precisely because newbuild economics are unattractive. **Key Risk.** **Free cash flow conversion is currently poor** — Q2 free cash flow was just **$0.7 million** as operating cash flow of $72.2 million was almost entirely consumed by $71.5 million of capital expenditures. If capital intensity remains elevated, the earnings growth will not translate into distributable cash at the rate the headline EPS suggests, and at ~20x that matters. Secondary risks: coastal ATB weakness in the 80,000–100,000 barrel class is producing **low-single-digit declines in term renewal rates**, and the fuel cost pass-through operates with a lag that compressed Q2 margins (management expects reversal in Q3, which is an assumption to verify).

**Rating: Accumulate.** Next earnings October 28, 2026 — the day of the FOMC decision.

### Portfolio Construction Notes

**A correlation I am disclosing rather than hiding.** Two of five picks — **LNG** and **WHD** — are energy-sector names, and a third, **KEX**, derives demand from refined product volumes. In a Middle East de-escalation that takes Brent from $107 back toward $80, all three would come under simultaneous pressure. That is a real, concentrated factor exposure and a reader deserves to see it stated rather than discover it in a drawdown.

I have accepted it deliberately, for three reasons. First, the exposures are genuinely differentiated in their drivers: Cheniere's cash flows are 95% contracted on fixed-fee take-or-pay structures and are largely insensitive to the spot price, Cactus is levered to onshore drilling *activity* rather than price, and Kirby is a regulated-moat transport business whose volumes depend on refined product movement, not crude economics. Second, today's spotlight bucket *is* industrials/energy/materials, and constructing an energy-light slate from an energy-centric bucket during an energy shock would be an exercise in false diversification. Third, **CCK is explicitly the hedge** — beverage can demand is essentially independent of oil, and its aluminum pass-throughs insulate it from the input-cost side.

A reader who disagrees with the oil view should size LNG and WHD down and CCK and CRS up. The analysis is offered so that judgment can be exercised, not substituted.

**Ticker-collision checks — verify CUSIPs before entry.**

## Risk Factors

**1. A hawkish October 28 FOMC — SEVERE, and the highest-probability near-term shock.**
The funds rate is 3.75–4.00%, the median dot shows one more 2026 hike, core CPI just beat on both annual and monthly measures, and core PCE has exceeded 3% every month this year. Chair Warsh's reduced forward guidance means the market has limited ability to pre-position. A hike accompanied by hawkish language would likely push the 10-year toward 5.50% and compress equity multiples broadly and quickly. This is the dominant risk and it has a date on it.

**2. Middle East escalation and the Hormuz chokepoint — SEVERE, lower probability, highest severity.**
Aramco facilities have been attacked, CENTCOM has publicly rejected Iranian claims over the Strait of Hormuz, and roughly 20% of global petroleum liquids consumption transits that waterway. Paul Tudor Jones sees scope for another $20–25 on crude. A genuine Hormuz disruption would be a simultaneous inflation shock and growth shock — the configuration monetary policy is least equipped to address, since hiking does not produce barrels. The stated U.S. deferral of action until after the midterms caps near-term risk but does not remove it, and a pre-announced timeline is itself exploitable information.

**3. The 10-year Treasury yield breaking above 5.50% — HIGH.**
At 5.29% and rising, with both 10s and 30s at 24-year highs, equity valuations are being discounted at levels unfamiliar to most market participants. Some market commentary identifies 6% as the threshold being watched. Mechanically, rising long rates compress long-duration growth multiples hardest — which is precisely why this report's picks are concentrated in short-duration, cash-generative businesses.

**4. AI capex outrunning adoption — HIGH, slower-developing.**
Hyperscaler spending is running in the high-$700-billions for 2026 on comparable definitions. Wedbush flags the buildout moving slightly ahead of adoption. The concern is not a demand collapse but a timing mismatch — and at 5.29%, financing a timing mismatch is dramatically more expensive than it was eighteen months ago. Watch the semis' refusal to rally on good news (ASML raised guidance three times and still sold off) as the leading positioning indicator.

**5. Credit quality deterioration surfacing in bank results — MODERATE but rising.**
Big banks posted strong Q3 profits while explicitly **warning that higher rates and inflation could bite**. Bank of America missed on revenue with trading approximately $750 million below expectations and absorbed a $1.2 billion regulatory penalty; State Street fell 7.6% on a revenue miss. Management warnings issued alongside strong headline results are typically the earliest observable signal of a credit cycle turning, and they deserve more weight than the beat-or-miss framing they usually receive.

## Bottom Line

The market is still trading as though the central question is when the Fed cuts, while the Fed has already hiked once, projects another, and concedes inflation will not reach target until 2029 — and today's hot core print moved the 10-year to 5.29% and narrowed the committee's room to look through an energy shock it has already cited by name. In that regime the winning exposures are short-duration cash flows, contractual inflation pass-through and moats that a high-rate environment widens rather than erodes, which is why today's slate concentrates on contracted LNG infrastructure, oligopoly packaging, qualification-gated aerospace alloys, a statutory marine transport moat and a net-cash equipment supplier — and why the most obvious beneficiary of this morning's gasoline number, Marathon Petroleum, is deliberately excluded at peak margins after a move of that magnitude.

The discipline that matters over the next two weeks is refusing to extrapolate either direction: do not underwrite $36/bbl refining margins as a run-rate, and do not assume the AI capex cycle breaks simply because semis have stopped rallying on good news. Position for the October 28 FOMC as a genuine two-sided event, keep dry powder for the software complex where a $2 trillion indiscriminate drawdown is creating work for valuation buyers, and size the energy-linked exposures with the honest acknowledgment that a Middle East de-escalation would hit three of today's five picks at once.
